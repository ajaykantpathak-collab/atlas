DEFINITION: Soil erosion is the removal of the topsoil layer by natural agents like water (sheet, rill, gully erosion) and wind, accelerated by human activities such as deforestation, overgrazing, and faulty farming methods. Conservation methods like contour ploughing (ploughing along contour lines to reduce runoff velocity), terracing (creating level steps on slopes to retain water), and shelter belts (rows of trees planted to break wind force) are adopted to check erosion and maintain soil fertility.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine standing on a farm in the Shiwalik hills after a heavy monsoon downpour: you see brown, muddy water rushing down the slope, carrying away the topsoil — that is sheet and rill erosion in action. Now recall Class 9 (c9_geo_ch1_8) where rivers erode and deposit; here the same hydraulic action works on a micro-scale on farm slopes. Begin by identifying the agent (water on slopes, wind in arid zones) and the human trigger (bare soil exposure). Then match the conservation method to the slope gradient and erosion agent: contour ploughing for gentle slopes (<10%) against water, terracing for steep slopes (>10%) in hilly regions, and shelter belts for flat, wind-prone plains like Rajasthan. The key principle is reducing the kinetic energy of the erosive agent (water velocity or wind speed) before it detaches soil particles.
