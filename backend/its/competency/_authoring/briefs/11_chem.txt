DEFINITION: Hund's Rule of Maximum Multiplicity states that when electrons occupy degenerate orbitals (orbitals of equal energy within the same subshell), they occupy separate orbitals singly with parallel spins before any pairing occurs. This arrangement maximizes the total spin multiplicity (2S+1) and minimizes electron-electron repulsion, leading to the lowest energy ground state.

This forces us to accept that electrons fill degenerate orbitals singly first with parallel spins (Hund's rule). This pattern — singles first, parallel spins, then pairs — is Hund's rule, driven by exchange energy stabilization and reduced electron-electron repulsion.

DEFINITION: The dipole moment (μ) of a polar covalent bond is the product of the magnitude of the partial charge (δ) on either atom and the distance (r) between the centres of positive and negative charge: μ = δ × r. It is a vector quantity directed from the positive centre to the negative centre by convention, but represented in chemistry by a crossed arrow (➤) pointing from positive to negative end on the Lewis structure, with the cross on the positive end. The SI unit is coulomb metre (C·m); the commonly used unit is the Debye (D), where 1 D = 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m.
WALKTHROUGH: Observe how a stream of water from a tap bends towards a charged plastic comb rubbed through dry hair — the water molecule acts like a tiny magnet with a positive and negative end. This happens because oxygen pulls the shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen, creating a charge separation. Recall from Class 9 (c9_chem_ch3_4) that covalent bonds involve electron sharing, but here the sharing is unequal. Start by identifying the more electronegative atom in a bond (using periodic trends from Class 10, c10_chem_cbse_05_010). Then determine molecular shape using VSEPR theory (c11_chem_cbse_04_005). Finally, add bond dipoles as vectors — if they cancel by symmetry, net μ = 0 (non-polar); if not, the molecule is polar. This vector addition uses trigonometry (c10_math_cbse_08_001) where the resultant of two equal dipoles at angle θ is μ_net = 2μ_bond cos(θ/2).
D1 Q: Define dipole moment and state its SI unit. Why is the crossed arrow representation used in chemistry instead of the conventional vector arrow?
D1 A: Dipole moment (μ) = charge (q) × distance of separation (r); SI unit = C·m. The crossed arrow (➤) points from δ+ to δ− with the cross on the δ+ end, opposite to the physics convention (vector from − to +). A wrong answer (e.g., stating unit as Debye only, or drawing arrow from negative to positive) reveals confusion between physics and chemistry conventions and ignorance of the 1 D = 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m conversion.

D2 A: CO₂ is linear (O=C=O, 180°); the two equal C=O bond dipoles are opposite and cancel vectorially → μ_net = 0. H₂O is bent (∠HOH ≈ 104.5°); the two O−H bond dipoles add at an angle, giving a resultant along the bisector. A wrong answer (e.g., "CO₂ has no polar bonds" or "H₂O is linear") shows failure to connect VSEPR geometry to vector addition of dipoles.

DEFINITION: Equilibrium in physical processes is a dynamic state in a closed system where opposing physical changes (such as melting/freezing, evaporation/condensation, or sublimation/deposition) occur at equal rates, resulting in no net change in the macroscopic properties (temperature, pressure, concentration) of the system over time. Now place an ice cube in a insulated flask at 0°C — it neither melts completely nor grows; solid and liquid water coexist at constant temperature. These everyday Indian kitchen observations reveal that physical equilibrium requires a closed system, a constant temperature, and opposing processes at equal rates. DEFINITION: The ionisation constant of a weak acid (Ka) is the equilibrium constant for its partial dissociation in aqueous solution: HA(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + A⁻(aq), where Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA]. VETTED-MIS C11_013 (fit 0.29)
  M: Students use [H+] = C (molarity) for weak acids instead of [H+] = sqrt(Ka x C).
  C: Strong acid: [H+] = C (complete dissociation). Weak acid: [H+] = sqrt(Ka x C) (partial dissociation). Example: 0.01 M CH3COOH (Ka = 1.8x10^-5) -> [H+] = sqrt(1.8x10^-5 x 0.01) = 4.24x10^-4, NOT 0.01.
VETTED-MIS misconception_5 (fit 0.24)
  M: All acids are strong electrolytes and all bases are weak electrolytes.
