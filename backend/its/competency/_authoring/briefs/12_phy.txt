DEFINITION: In electrostatic equilibrium, a conductor is a material containing mobile charge carriers (free electrons in metals) that redistribute themselves such that the electric field inside the conductor is zero, the conductor becomes an equipotential volume, and any net charge resides entirely on its surface with surface charge density σ (C/m²). An insulator (dielectric) lacks mobile charge carriers; charges remain bound to atoms/molecules and cannot move freely, so the electric field inside can be non-zero and charge distribution is fixed.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine touching a metal car door after sliding across the seat on a dry day — you feel a spark. That spark is excess charge on your body (a conductor) discharging through the air. Now consider a hollow metal box (Faraday cage) used to protect sensitive electronics in a lightning storm: the lightning's enormous electric field exists outside, but inside the cavity the field is zero. Why? Because free electrons in the metal instantly rearrange to cancel any internal field. Start by visualizing a neutral metal sphere placed in a uniform external field E₀. Free electrons drift opposite to E₀, creating induced surface charges that produce an opposing field E_ind inside. In equilibrium, E₀ + E_ind = 0 everywhere inside. Next, use a Gaussian pillbox straddling the surface to prove E = σ/ε₀ normal to the surface. Finally, argue that since E=0 inside, potential V is constant (∇V = -E = 0), making the entire conductor an equipotential volume. This logic extends to cavities: any cavity inside a conductor, even with external fields, has E=0 if no charges are inside the cavity.

D1 A: The electric field inside a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium is zero. Reason: If E were non-zero, free charges would experience a force (F = qE) and move, constituting a current, contradicting the static equilibrium condition. A wrong answer (e.g., "E is non-zero but small") reveals the student thinks conductors merely "reduce" field rather than cancel it completely, confusing conductors with dielectrics.

DEFINITION: Ohm's Law states that for a given metallic conductor at constant temperature, the current (I) flowing through it is directly proportional to the potential difference (V) applied across its ends, i.e., V ∝ I or V = IR, where R is the resistance of the conductor (SI unit: ohm, Ω). The law is valid only for ohmic materials where the V–I graph is a straight line passing through the origin, and resistance R is independent of V and I.
WALKTHROUGH: Observe a household incandescent bulb: when you switch it on, the filament glows because current flows through it. If you connect the same bulb to a variable power supply (like a lab rheostat) and measure voltage across and current through it at different settings, you will notice a pattern. Start by plotting V vs I on graph paper — for a metallic wire at room temperature, the points fall on a straight line through the origin. This linear proportionality is Ohm's law. Now ask: does every material behave this way? Connect a diode (from an old LED circuit) in forward and reverse bias — the graph is not a straight line, and reversing voltage doesn't simply reverse current. This leads to the limitations. The microscopic form E = ρj connects the macroscopic V = IR to the electric field and current density inside the material, which we will use in the next node to derive drift velocity.
D1 Q: State Ohm's law in its mathematical form and write the SI unit of resistance. What is the essential condition for a conductor to obey Ohm's law?
D1 A: Ohm's law: V = IR, where V is potential difference (volt, V), I is current (ampere, A), R is resistance (ohm, Ω). Essential condition: temperature of the conductor must remain constant. A wrong answer (e.g., missing "constant temperature" or giving unit as "ohm per metre") reveals the student treats Ohm's law as universally true without conditions, a common Board exam error.

D2 A: The flaw: R = V/I defines resistance for any conductor (ohmic or non-ohmic); it does not imply R ∝ V. For an ohmic conductor, R is constant (independent of V and I) — doubling V doubles I, keeping R unchanged. The student confuses the definition of resistance with Ohm's law (which asserts R is constant). A wrong answer shows the student cannot distinguish between a definition and a physical law.

DEFINITION: Resistivity (ρ) is an intrinsic material property quantifying how strongly a material opposes electric current; its SI unit is ohm-metre (Ω·m). The temperature dependence of resistivity for metals over a limited range is approximately linear: ρ_T = ρ_0[1 + α(T - T_0)], where ρ_0 is resistivity at reference temperature T_0, α is the temperature coefficient of resistivity (SI unit: °C⁻¹ or K⁻¹), and α > 0 for metals. For semiconductors and insulators, resistivity decreases with temperature (α < 0) due to a rapid increase in charge carrier density.
WALKTHROUGH: Observe an electric geyser heating element: when cold, its resistance is low; as it heats up to 80–90°C, the resistance increases noticeably, causing the current to settle to a steady value. Begin by measuring the resistance of a nichrome wire at room temperature (using a multimeter) and then at a higher temperature (by passing a known current). Use the formula R = R_0[1 + α(T - T_0)] to find α. Connect this to the drift velocity model (c12_phy_cbse_03_003): resistivity ρ = m/(ne²τ). In metals, n is nearly constant but τ decreases with temperature due to increased lattice vibrations, so ρ increases. In semiconductors, n increases exponentially with T, overwhelming the decrease in τ, so ρ decreases.
D1 Q: State the formula for the temperature dependence of resistivity of a metallic conductor over a limited temperature range. Define the temperature coefficient of resistivity (α) and give its SI unit.
D1 A: ρ_T = ρ_0[1 + α(T - T_0)], where ρ_T is resistivity at temperature T, ρ_0 at reference T_0, and α is the temperature coefficient of resistivity. α = (1/ρ_0)(dρ/dT) at T_0; SI unit: °C⁻¹ or K⁻¹. A wrong answer (e.g., writing α = (ρ_T - ρ_0)/ρ_0 without dividing by ΔT, or giving unit as Ω·m) reveals confusion between the coefficient and the fractional change, or between resistivity and its temperature coefficient.

DEFINITION: An electromagnetic wave is a self-propagating transverse wave consisting of mutually perpendicular, sinusoidally oscillating electric field vector E and magnetic field vector B, both perpendicular to the direction of propagation k. In vacuum, it travels at the universal constant speed \(c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0} = 3.00 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}\), with the field amplitudes related by \(E_0 = c B_0\). The wave carries energy, momentum, and angular momentum, and exerts radiation pressure on surfaces.
WALKTHROUGH: Observe a microwave oven: the rotating turntable ensures even heating because the microwaves form standing waves with hot and cold spots — this reveals that EM waves carry energy in a spatially varying pattern. Use Maxwell’s equations (c12_phy_cbse_08_002) to deduce that a changing E produces B and vice versa, forcing E, B, k to be mutually perpendicular. Derive \(c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}\) from the wave equation. Introduce the sinusoidal form \(E_x = E_0 \sin(kz - \omega t)\), \(B_y = B_0 \sin(kz - \omega t)\). Show that energy density \(u_E = u_B\) leads to intensity \(I = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 c E_0^2\). Finally, connect momentum \(p = U/c\) to radiation pressure \(P = I/c\) (absorption) and \(2I/c\) (reflection) — a measurable force used in solar sails.

DEFINITION: Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle states that it is fundamentally impossible to simultaneously determine the exact position (x) and exact momentum (p) of a particle. The product of the uncertainties in position (Δx) and momentum (Δp) is always greater than or equal to the reduced Planck constant divided by 2: Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2, where ħ = h/(2π) = 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s. This is not a limitation of experimental apparatus but a fundamental property of quantum systems arising from the wave nature of matter.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine trying to locate an electron inside a hydrogen atom (size ~ 0.1 nm) using a powerful microscope. To "see" the electron, you must illuminate it with light of wavelength λ ≤ 0.1 nm (X-rays). But each X-ray photon carries momentum p = h/λ. When it strikes the electron, it transfers a large, unpredictable momentum kick, making the electron's momentum uncertain. If you use longer-wavelength light to reduce the momentum kick, you lose position resolution because diffraction blurs the image. This trade-off is unavoidable — it stems from the de Broglie wave nature (c12_phy_cbse_11_004): a particle is a wave packet, and a narrower packet in position space requires a broader spread of wavelengths (momenta). The quantitative limit is Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2.

A wrong answer (e.g., Δx Δp ≥ h, or using h instead of ħ/2, or omitting the factor 1/2) reveals the student has memorised an approximate or incorrect form and does not know the precise lower bound from the standard deviation definition used in quantum mechanics.
