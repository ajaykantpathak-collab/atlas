DEFINITION: The thyroid gland is a bilobed endocrine gland located in the neck that secretes thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), iodine-containing hormones that regulate the basal metabolic rate (BMR) of lipids, proteins, and carbohydrates in almost all body tissues by increasing oxygen consumption and heat production.
WALKTHROUGH: Start by asking students to observe a person with a visible swelling in the front of the neck (goitre) — a common sight in iodine-deficient regions like the sub-Himalayan belt. Connect this to their Class 6 knowledge of "The 'Why' Question: Hormones Behind the Changes" where they learned hormones drive growth. Ask: "If this swelling is due to a hormone deficiency, which gland is involved and why does the neck swell?" Guide them to locate the thyroid gland in the neck, explain its need for dietary iodine (link to iodised salt packets in Indian kitchens), and trace how low thyroxine slows down cellular respiration (recall Class 6 "Cellular Respiration: How Cells Extract Energy" and Class 9 "Mitochondria: Powerhouse of the Cell"). Conclude with the key principle: thyroxine acts as the body's metabolic thermostat, setting the pace of energy release from food.
D1 Q: Name the hormone secreted by the thyroid gland that regulates basal metabolic rate and state the mineral element essential for its synthesis.
D1 A: The hormone is thyroxine (T4) or triiodothyronine (T3); the essential mineral is iodine. A wrong answer (e.g., "insulin" or "iron") reveals the student cannot distinguish between endocrine glands and their specific hormones, or confuses the mineral requirement for thyroid hormone (iodine) with that for haemoglobin (iron).

DEFINITION: A flower is the reproductive shoot of an angiosperm, composed of four whorls of modified leaves — calyx (sepals), corolla (petals), androecium (stamens), and gynoecium (carpels) — arranged on a thalamus, where the androecium produces male gametes (pollen grains) and the gynoecium houses the female gamete (ovule) for sexual reproduction.
WALKTHROUGH: Begin by asking the student to pluck a hibiscus (china rose) or mustard flower from a nearby garden — a familiar sight in Indian homes and fields. Have them gently separate the flower from the outermost green leaf-like structures inward, placing each whorl on a white paper. Connect this to Class 9 (c9_bio_ch3_5) where they learned the four whorls; now we deepen by examining the internal structure of anther (pollen sacs) and ovary (ovules on placenta), and the concept of essential vs accessory whorls. Emphasize that the flower is a condensed shoot where nodes are compressed — the thalamus represents the stem, and each whorl arises from a node. The key biological principle: every floral part is a modified leaf adapted for a specific reproductive role, illustrating the concept of homology in plant morphology.

The essential whorls are androecium and gynoecium because they directly participate in sexual reproduction (produce gametes).
