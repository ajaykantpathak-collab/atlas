DEFINITION: If p is a prime number and p divides a² (where a is a positive integer), then p must also divide a. In other words, if a prime number divides the square of an integer, it necessarily divides the integer itself.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine you are packing 36 identical square tiles (6×6) into boxes. If a prime number of boxes, say 3, exactly divides the total tiles, then 3 must also divide the side length 6. Why? Because 36 = 6×6, and if 3 divides 6×6, by the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic (unique prime factorization), the prime 3 must appear in the factorization of 6. Start by writing a as a product of primes: a = p₁p₂...pₖ. Then a² = p₁²p₂²...pₖ². If prime p divides a², p must be one of the pᵢ, hence p divides a. This insight is the engine that drives all irrationality proofs in Class 10.
D1 Q: State the theorem: "If p is a prime number and p divides a², then p divides a." Is the converse true? That is, if p divides a, does p always divide a²?
D1 A: Yes, the converse is true. If p divides a, then a = p·k for some integer k. Then a² = p²·k² = p·(p·k²), so p divides a². A wrong answer (saying converse is false) reveals the student confuses the direction of implication and does not understand that divisibility is preserved under multiplication.
D2 Q: Why does the theorem require p to be prime? Give a counterexample showing the statement fails if p is composite.
D2 A: The theorem fails for composites because a composite number's prime factors can be split between the two factors of a². Counterexample: Let p = 4 (composite), a = 6. Then a² = 36, and 4 divides 36, but 4 does not divide 6. A wrong answer (e.g., "it works for all numbers") shows the student has not grasped the role of unique prime factorization and the definition of prime numbers.
D3 Q: Let a = 180. Find all prime numbers p such that p divides a². For each such p, verify that p divides a.
D3 A: a = 180 = 2² × 3² × 5¹. So a² = 2⁴ × 3⁴ × 5². The primes dividing a² are 2, 3, 5. Each of these divides 180. A wrong answer (missing a prime, or including a non-prime) reveals the student cannot correctly perform prime factorization or does not connect the theorem to the factorization of a.
D4 Q: Suppose p is a prime and p divides a² + b². Can we conclude that p divides a and p divides b? Justify your answer with an example or proof. [IN-SCOPE-REWRITE]
D4 A: No, we cannot conclude that. The theorem only applies when a prime divides a perfect square of a single integer. For example, p = 5, a = 1, b = 2: a² + b² = 1 + 4 = 5, and 5 divides 5, but 5 does not divide 1 and 5 does not divide 2. A wrong answer (saying yes) shows the student overgeneralizes the theorem to sums of squares, confusing it with properties of Gaussian integers or misapplying the logic.
D5 Q: A student claims: "Since 9 divides 15² (225), by the theorem 9 must divide 15." Identify the error in the student's reasoning and correct it.
D5 A: The error is that 9 is not a prime number (9 = 3²). The theorem only applies when p is prime. Indeed, 9 divides 225 but 9 does not divide 15. The correct statement: The prime 3 divides 15², so 3 divides 15 (which is true). A wrong answer (not identifying the primality condition) shows the student memorizes the statement without the crucial hypothesis "p is prime".
D6 Q: Construct a new problem: Find a positive integer a and a prime p such that p divides a² but p does NOT divide a. If impossible, explain why.
D6 A: It is impossible. By the theorem, if p is prime and p|a², then p|a always. Any attempt to find such a pair will fail. A student who provides an example (e.g., a=6, p=4) has missed the condition that p must be prime. This reveals they treat the theorem as a pattern without understanding the logical necessity of the prime condition.

This is proved by contradiction using the sum/difference property: the sum or difference of a non-zero rational number and an irrational number is always irrational. Assume 5 − √3 is rational, say r. Then √3 = 5 − r. Since 5 is rational and r is rational (by assumption), and rational numbers are closed under subtraction, 5 − r is rational. DEFINITION: The complement of an event E, denoted E' or "not E", is the event that E does NOT occur. The probability of the complement is P(not E) = 1 − P(E). This follows from the fact that the sample space S is partitioned into E and E' (E and not E), so P(E) + P(not E) = P(S) = 1. The complement rule is one of the most useful tools in probability: if computing P(E) directly is difficult, compute P(not E) and subtract from 1.
WALKTHROUGH: When you roll a die, the event E = "rolling a 6" has P(E) = 1/6. The complement "not E" = "not rolling a 6" includes {1, 2, 3, 4, 5}, so P(not E) = 5/6. Notice: 1/6 + 5/6 = 1. This is the complement rule: P(not E) = 1 − P(E). The rule is powerful when direct counting is hard. For example, "at least one head in 3 coin tosses" — counting directly (HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH = 7) is tedious. Instead, compute P(no heads) = P(TTT) = 1/8, then P(at least one head) = 1 − 1/8 = 7/8. The complement trick ("at least one" → complement is "none") is a standard board exam technique.
D1 Q: State the complement rule for probability.
D1 A: P(not E) = 1 − P(E), where "not E" is the complement of event E. A wrong answer (e.g., "P(not E) = P(E)") shows the student does not understand the relationship between an event and its complement.
D2 Q: If P(E) = 0.3, find P(not E).
D2 A: P(not E) = 1 − 0.3 = 0.7. A wrong answer (e.g., 0.3 or −0.3) shows the student cannot apply the complement formula.
D3 Q: A die is rolled. What is the probability of NOT getting a prime number?
D3 A: P(prime) = 3/6 = 1/2 (primes: 2, 3, 5). P(not prime) = 1 − 1/2 = 1/2. (Non-primes: 1, 4, 6 — note 1 is not prime.) A wrong answer (e.g., 3/6 = 1/2 without showing the complement step, or including 1 as prime) shows incomplete reasoning or a number theory error.
D4 Q: Explain why P(E) + P(not E) = 1. [IN-SCOPE-REWRITE]
D4 A: The sample space S is the union of E and "not E", and they are mutually exclusive (an outcome either is in E or is not, but not both). So P(E) + P(not E) = P(S) = 1. A wrong answer (e.g., "because they are the same event") shows the student does not understand that E and not E partition the sample space.
D5 Q: A bag has 10 balls, 4 of which are defective. What is the probability of drawing a non-defective ball?
D5 A: P(defective) = 4/10 = 2/5. P(non-defective) = 1 − 2/5 = 3/5. (Directly: 6 non-defective out of 10, P = 6/10 = 3/5.) A wrong answer (e.g., 4/10) shows the student computed P(defective) instead of P(non-defective).
D6 Q: Three coins are tossed. Use the complement rule to find the probability of getting at least one head.
D6 A: P(at least one head) = 1 − P(no heads) = 1 − P(TTT) = 1 − 1/8 = 7/8. The complement of "at least one head" is "no heads" (all tails), which is easier to compute. A wrong answer (e.g., listing all 7 favourable outcomes and getting 7/8 without using the complement) is correct but misses the efficiency of the method.
