DEFINITION: The Marxist critique of liberal justice argues that liberal theories (like those of Rawls or Nozick) treat justice as a matter of fair procedures and formal rights within the existing capitalist framework, thereby ignoring the structural exploitation inherent in the capitalist mode of production where surplus value is extracted from the labour of the working class by the owners of the means of production. For Marx, justice is not about distributing goods fairly within a class-divided society but about abolishing the class relations that make such exploitation possible.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine a factory worker, Ramesh, and the factory owner, Mr. Kapoor. Under liberal justice (Rawls/Nozick), both have equal rights to property, contract, and vote. They sign a "voluntary" employment contract. A liberal theorist says this is just because the procedure was fair. Now, ask: why does Ramesh have no choice but to sell his labour, while Mr. Kapoor buys it? Marx directs us to the *history* behind the contract: the enclosure of commons, colonial plunder, and dispossession that created a class with nothing to sell but labour-power. The "free contract" hides the structural coercion. This is the starting point: liberal justice looks at the *surface* of exchange; Marx looks at the *hidden structure* of production. Connect this to the prior nodes on Rawls (veil of ignorance ignores class structure) and Nozick (entitlement theory ignores original acquisition by dispossession). The key civic principle: Justice cannot be assessed solely by the fairness of rules if the starting conditions are themselves products of historical injustice and ongoing exploitation.
