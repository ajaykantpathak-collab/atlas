## DEFINITION
In a Balance Sheet, liabilities are classified as **Current Liabilities** (obligations expected to be settled within 12 months after the reporting date or within the normal operating cycle, whichever is longer) and **Non-Current (Fixed/Long-term) Liabilities** (obligations not meeting the current criteria, typically payable after one year). This classification follows the principle of liquidity and permanence to show the timing of cash outflows.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can correctly classify any given liability (e.g., bank overdraft, 5-year loan, creditors, outstanding wages) as Current or Non-Current with justification based on the 12-month/operating cycle rule, and present them in the correct order (permanence or liquidity) in a Balance Sheet format.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the classification criteria, examples, and presentation of liabilities in a sole proprietorship Balance Sheet as per NCERT Class 11. It does NOT cover contingent liabilities, corporate Balance Sheet formats (Schedule III), or detailed measurement/valuation of liabilities (e.g., present value of long-term debt).

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are a bank manager reviewing a loan application from a small business. The owner hands you a Balance Sheet where all liabilities are jumbled together: a 10-year machinery loan, tomorrow's electricity bill, and a 3-month bank overdraft are listed randomly. How can you quickly assess if the business can repay its short-term debts? This is exactly why we classify liabilities into Current (due soon) and Non-Current (due later). We start by identifying the **reporting date** (Balance Sheet date) and the **operating cycle** (time to convert raw material into cash). Any liability due within 12 months or the operating cycle goes under Current Liabilities; the rest go under Non-Current Liabilities. The key insight: classification depends on **time to settlement**, not the name of the liability (e.g., a "Loan" can be current if payable within a year).

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Define Current Liabilities and give two examples from a trading business.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
**Correct Answer:** Current Liabilities are obligations expected to be settled within 12 months after the reporting date or within the normal operating cycle, whichever is longer. Examples: Sundry Creditors (payable within 30-90 days), Bank Overdraft (repayable on demand).  
**What a wrong answer reveals:** If a student defines it only as "liabilities payable within a year" without mentioning the operating cycle, they miss the nuance for businesses with cycles longer than 12 months (e.g., wine distillery). If they give examples like "Long-term Loan" or "Debentures", they confuse non-current with current.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
A business has an operating cycle of 15 months. On the Balance Sheet date, it has a loan installment of ₹50,000 due in 14 months. Is this Current or Non-Current? Explain.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
**Correct Answer:** Current Liability. Because the operating cycle (15 months) is longer than 12 months, the criterion becomes the operating cycle. Since 14 months < 15 months, it falls within the operating cycle and is classified as Current.  
**What a wrong answer reveals:** If the student says Non-Current because 14 months > 12 months, they are applying the 12-month rule mechanically without considering the operating cycle exception. This is a classic Class 11 error where students forget the "whichever is longer" clause.

**Correct Answer:**

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the presentation of Liabilities in the Order of Permanence vs. Order of Liquidity. Which order is more useful for a short-term creditor (supplier) and why?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
**Correct Answer:** 
- **Order of Permanence:** Capital → Long-term Loan → Current Liabilities (Creditors, Bills Payable, Bank Overdraft). Most permanent (Capital) at top.
- **Order of Liquidity:** Current Liabilities (Bank Overdraft, Bills Payable, Creditors) → Long-term Loan → Capital. Most urgent to pay (Bank Overdraft) at top.
**For a short-term creditor (supplier), Order of Liquidity is more useful** because it immediately shows the liabilities due soonest (Current Liabilities) and the liquid assets available to pay them, helping assess short-term solvency.
**What a wrong answer reveals:** If the student says Order of Permanence is better for creditors, they confuse the needs of long-term investors (who care about capital structure) with short-term creditors. If they cannot list the correct sequence in either order, they haven't mastered marshalling.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate this statement: "Capital is a Non-Current Liability because it is never repaid during the life of the business." Is this correct? Justify.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
**Correct Answer:** The statement is **partially correct but conceptually flawed**. Capital is shown separately under "Owners' Funds" or "Shareholders' Funds", not under "Non-Current Liabilities". While it is permanent (not repayable on a fixed date), it represents the owner's claim, not an obligation to outsiders. Liabilities are obligations to external parties. Capital is the residual interest (Assets - Liabilities). Classifying it as a liability misrepresents the accounting equation.
**What a wrong answer reveals:** If the student agrees fully, they confuse the accounting equation structure (Assets = Liabilities + Capital) and treat capital as a liability. If they say Capital is a Current Liability because drawings can be made, they misunderstand the nature of equity.

Create a mini Balance Sheet (Liabilities side only) for a fictional bakery as at 31st March 2026, using the Order of Liquidity. Include at least 2 Current Liabilities, 1 Non-Current Liability, and Capital. Invent reasonable amounts.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
**Expected Answer (Criteria):**
- Correct headings: Liabilities side, Order of Liquidity.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
**Misconception:** "Any loan from a bank is a Non-Current (Long-term) Liability."  
**Why students make this error:** Students associate "Bank Loan" with long tenure (e.g., home loan, car loan) and overlook that banks also give short-term loans (working capital loans, overdrafts) and that long-term loans have a **current portion** (installment due within 12 months). In NCERT illustrations, "10% Long-term Loan" appears as a single item, but in practice and adjusted problems, it must be split.  
**Concrete wrong answer example:** In a trial balance, "Bank Loan ₹1,00,000" is given with a note: "₹20,000 repayable within a year." The student puts the full ₹1,00,000 under Non-Current Liabilities, ignoring the current portion.

## THIS_FORMULA
Classification Rule: Liability = Current IF (Due Date ≤ Reporting Date + 12 months) OR (Due Date ≤ Reporting Date + Operating Cycle); ELSE Non-Current.

## FORMULA_VARIABLES
- Reporting Date: The date of the Balance Sheet (e.g., 31st March 2026).
- Operating Cycle: Time between acquisition of raw material and realization of cash from sales (if > 12 months, it replaces 12 months as the threshold).
- Due Date: The date on which the liability is contractually payable.

## FORMULA_UNITS
Time units: months or days. Monetary amounts in ₹ (Indian Rupees).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: From the following Trial Balance extracts as on 31st March 2026, prepare the Liabilities side of the Balance Sheet in Order of Liquidity. Operating Cycle = 12 months.

| Account Title | Dr. (₹) | Cr. (₹) |
|---------------|---------|---------|
| Capital | | 5,00,000 |
| 12% Bank Loan (taken 1st April 2023, repayable in 4 yearly installments of ₹50,000 starting 31st March 2024) | | 2,00,000 |
| Creditors | | 1,20,000 |
| Bills Payable (due 15th April 2026) | | 80,000 |
| Bank Overdraft | | 45,000 |
| Outstanding Salaries | | 15,000 |
| Net Profit for the year | | 75,000 |
| Drawings | 40,000 | |

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify each credit balance as Capital, Non-Current Liability, or Current Liability. Capital = ₹5,00,000 + Net Profit ₹75,000 - Drawings ₹40,000 = ₹5,35,000.
- Step 2: Analyze the 12% Bank Loan. Total loan ₹2,00,000. Installments: 31 Mar 2024 (paid), 31 Mar 2025 (paid), 31 Mar 2026 (paid? Wait, as on 31 Mar 2026, the third installment is due today. Usually, if the Balance Sheet is prepared *after* payment, the liability reduces. But if the installment is due *on* the Balance Sheet date and not yet paid, it's a current liability. Assume the third installment of ₹50,000 is due on 31 Mar 2026 and unpaid -> Current Liability. The remaining ₹1,00,000 (two installments left for 2027, 2028) is Non-Current.
- Step 3: Classify remaining items: Creditors (payable within 30-90 days) = Current; Bills Payable (due 15 Apr 2026) = Current; Bank Overdraft (on demand) = Current; Outstanding Salaries (due within month) = Current.
- Step 4: Arrange in Order of Liquidity (most urgent first): Bank Overdraft → Bills Payable → Creditors → Outstanding Salaries → Current Portion of Bank Loan → Long-term Portion of Bank Loan → Capital.
- Step 5: Verify totals: Current Liabilities = 45,000 + 80,000 + 1,20,000 + 15,000 + 50,000 = ₹3,10,000. Non-Current = ₹1,00,000. Capital = ₹5,35,000. Total Liabilities = ₹9,45,000. (Assets side would match this).

REASONING: The key is splitting the long-term loan into current and non-current portions based on the repayment schedule relative to the Balance Sheet date. Order of Liquidity demands the most pressing obligations (Bank Overdraft, Bills Payable) appear first because they require immediate cash outflow. Capital, being the most permanent, appears last.

| Sundry Creditors | 1,20,000 |
| Outstanding Salaries | 15,000 |

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A manufacturing company has an operating cycle of 18 months. On 31st March 2026, it has the following obligations. Classify each as Current or Non-Current.
1. Trade Payables due in 3 months: ₹2,00,000.
2. 10% Debentures redeemable on 30th June 2027: ₹5,00,000.
3. Term Loan from Bank: ₹10,00,000. Repayable in 20 quarterly installments of ₹50,000 starting 30th June 2024. (As on 31st March 2026, 7 installments paid, next due 30th June 2026).
4. Advance from Customers for goods to be delivered in 20 months: ₹3,00,000.
5. Provision for Taxation (payable by 30th September 2026): ₹1,50,000.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Note the operating cycle = 18 months (> 12 months). Threshold = 18 months from 31st March 2026 = 30th September 2027.
- Step 2: Item 1 (Trade Payables due in 3 months): 3 months < 18 months → **Current Liability**.
- Step 3: Item 2 (Debentures redeemable 30th June 2027): 30th June 2027 is 15 months away (< 18 months) → **Current Liability**. (Even though it's a "long-term" instrument, the redemption date falls within the operating cycle).
- Step 4: Item 3 (Term Loan): Next installment due 30th June 2026 (3 months away) → Current Portion = ₹50,000. Installments due within 18 months: 30 Jun 2026, 30 Sep 2026, 31 Dec 2026, 31 Mar 2027, 30 Jun 2027, 30 Sep 2027 = 6 installments = ₹3,00,000. So Current Portion = ₹3,00,000. Non-Current Portion = ₹10,00,000 - 7 paid (₹3,50,000) - ₹3,00,000 current = ₹3,50,000.
- Step 5: Item 4 (Advance from Customers): Goods to be delivered in 20 months. 20 months > 18 months (operating cycle). Since the obligation is to supply goods, not pay cash, and it's beyond the operating cycle → **Non-Current Liability** (often shown as "Advance from Customers - Long-term" or under Other Non-Current Liabilities).
- Step 6: Item 5 (Provision for Taxation payable 30 Sep 2026): 6 months < 18 months → **Current Liability**.

REASONING: The operating cycle extends the current classification window to 18 months. This pulls the Debentures (redeemable in 15 months) and more loan installments into Current Liabilities. The customer advance is a contract liability; if the performance (delivery) is beyond the operating cycle, it's non-current. This mirrors Ind AS 1 / Schedule III logic simplified for Class 11.

ANSWER:
1. Trade Payables: **Current Liability** (₹2,00,000)
2. 10% Debentures: **Current Liability** (₹5,00,000) – redeemable within operating cycle.
3. Term Loan: **Current Portion ₹3,00,000**; **Non-Current Portion ₹3,50,000**.
4. Advance from Customers: **Non-Current Liability** (₹3,00,000) – delivery beyond operating cycle.
5. Provision for Taxation: **Current Liability** (₹1,50,000)

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: (Combines Classification + Adjustment + Capital Calculation)
M/s Gupta Traders' Trial Balance as on 31st March 2026 shows:
- Capital (1st April 2025): ₹4,00,000
- Drawings: ₹50,000
- 10% Loan from IDBI Bank (taken 1st Oct 2025): ₹2,00,000 (No interest paid)
- Creditors: ₹1,50,000
- Outstanding Wages: ₹20,000
- Net Profit before adjustments: ₹1,20,000
Additional Information:
(i) Interest on Bank Loan is outstanding for 6 months.
(ii) Loan is repayable in 4 equal half-yearly installments starting 31st March 2026.
(iii) Operating Cycle = 12 months.
Prepare the Liabilities side of Balance Sheet in Order of Permanence.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate Interest Outstanding. Loan ₹2,00,000 @ 10% p.a. for 6 months = ₹10,000. This is an expense (added to P&L) and a Current Liability (Interest Accrued).
- Step 2: Adjust Net Profit: Net Profit after interest = ₹1,20,000 - ₹10,000 = ₹1,10,000.
- Step 3: Calculate Closing Capital: Opening Capital ₹4,00,000 + Net Profit ₹1,10,000 - Drawings ₹50,000 = ₹4,60,000.
- Step 4: Analyze Loan Repayment. Loan taken 1 Oct 2025. Half-yearly installments of ₹50,000 starting 31 Mar 2026. As on 31 Mar 2026:
   - First installment due *today* (31 Mar 2026) = ₹50,000 (Current Liability if unpaid).
   - Next installment due 30 Sep 2026 = ₹50,000 (Current Liability, within 12 months).
   - Remaining balance = ₹2,00,000 - ₹50,000 (current) - ₹50,000 (current) = ₹1,00,000 (Non-Current, due after 12 months).

- Step 5: Classify all liabilities:
   - Capital: ₹4,60,000 (Most permanent)
   - Non-Current: Loan from IDBI (Non-Current Portion) ₹1,00,000
   - Current: Loan from IDBI (Current Portion) ₹1,00,000 (two installments), Interest Accrued ₹10,000, Creditors ₹1,50,000, Outstanding Wages ₹20,000.
- Step 6: Arrange in Order of Permanence: Capital → Non-Current Liabilities → Current Liabilities (usually Creditors, then Outstanding, then Bank Loan current portion, then Interest Accrued – but within current, order of liquidity is often used; for permanence, current liabilities as a group come last).

REASONING: This example integrates three earlier nodes: Interest adjustment (c11_acc_cbse_08_010/011), Loan classification (this node), and Capital calculation (c11_acc_cbse_08_004/005). The half-yearly installments mean two installments fall within the next 12 months (31 Mar 2026 and 30 Sep 2026), making ₹1,00,000 current. The interest accrued is a separate current liability. Order of Permanence starts with Capital.

10% Bank Loan (taken 1st April 2024, repayable in 5 yearly installments of ₹40,000 starting 31st March 2025) | 2,00,000
Creditors | 1,50,000
Bills Payable (due 30th April 2026) | 50,000
Outstanding Rent | 10,000
**Total** | **10,10,000**

Identify **two** errors in classification/presentation and redraw the corrected Liabilities side in Order of Liquidity. Assume operating cycle = 12 months.

COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction in financial statement presentation.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Check Bank Loan classification. Loan taken 1 Apr 2024. Installments: 31 Mar 2025 (paid), 31 Mar 2026 (due today/this year), 31 Mar 2027, 2028, 2029. As on 31 Mar 2026, the second installment (₹40,000) is due within 12 months → Current Liability. Remaining ₹1,20,000 (3 installments) → Non-Current. ## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: M/s Sharma & Co. (a partnership firm) has an operating cycle of 15 months. On 31st March 2026, the firm has a "12% Loan from Partner's Spouse" of ₹3,00,000. The loan agreement states: "Repayable in 3 equal annual installments of ₹1,00,000 starting 31st December 2026." The accountant classified the entire ₹3,00,000 as Non-Current Liability because it is a "long-term loan from a related party." 
(a) Is the accountant correct? Justify using the classification criteria.
(b) If the loan agreement instead said "Repayable on demand after 2 years," how would you classify it? Explain.
(c) Why is the distinction between Current and Non-Current critical for the firm's bankers?

COMPETENCY: Applying classification criteria to complex scenarios (related party, operating cycle > 12 months, repayable on demand) and evaluating stakeholder utility.

STEPS:
- Step 1 (a): Operating cycle = 15 months > 12 months. Threshold = 15 months from 31 Mar 2026 = 30 June 2027. First installment due 31 Dec 2026 (9 months away) < 15 months → **Current Liability (₹1,00,000)**. Second installment due 31 Dec 2027 (21 months away) > 15 months → **Non-Current (₹2,00,000)**. Accountant is **incorrect**; related party does not change classification.
- Step 2 (b): "Repayable on demand after 2 years" means the lender *can* demand repayment after 2 years (i.e., earliest demand date = 31 Mar 2028). Since the firm has no unconditional right to defer beyond 12 months (the lender could demand on 31 Mar 2028, which is 24 months away), but "on demand" clauses typically make it current if the lender can demand *immediately*. Here, "after 2 years" restricts demand. As per Ind AS 1 / Schedule III, if the liability is due to be settled within 12 months *or* the entity does not have an unconditional right to defer settlement for at least 12 months after reporting date, it's current. Since the lender *cannot* demand before 2 years (31 Mar 2028), the firm *has* an unconditional right to defer for at least 12 months (actually 24 months). So it is **Non-Current**. (Class 11 simplification: if repayment is not due within 12 months/operating cycle, it's non-current).
- Step 3 (c): Bankers analyze **Current Ratio (Current Assets / Current Liabilities)** and **Liquidity**. Misclassifying a current portion as non-current inflates the Current Ratio, misleading bankers about short-term solvency and ability to repay working capital loans. It affects loan covenants and interest rates.

ANSWER:
(a) **Incorrect.** Operating cycle = 15 months. First installment (₹1,00,000) due 31 Dec 2026 falls within 15 months → **Current Liability**. Remaining ₹2,00,000 → **Non-Current**. Related party status does not alter classification.
(b) **Non-Current Liability.** The clause "repayable on demand after 2 years" means the lender cannot demand repayment before 31st March 2028. The firm has an unconditional right to defer settlement for at least 12 months (actually 24 months) after the reporting date. Hence, it does not meet the Current criteria.
(c) **Critical for Bankers:** Bankers rely on the Current Ratio and liquidity position to assess short-term repayment capacity. Misclassifying ₹1,00,000 as Non-Current would understate Current Liabilities, overstate the Current Ratio, and hide the true cash outflow pressure in the near term. This could lead to incorrect lending decisions or covenant breaches.
