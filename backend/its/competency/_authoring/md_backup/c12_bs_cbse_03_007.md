## DEFINITION
Economic Liberalisation refers to the process of reducing government controls and restrictions on industries, trade, and investment to promote competition, efficiency, and integration with the global economy. The New Industrial Policy of 1991 was the landmark policy package that initiated liberalisation in India by delicensing most industries, reducing the public sector's role, allowing foreign direct investment (FDI) and technology agreements, and simplifying import-export procedures, thereby shifting India from a licence-permit-quota regime to a market-oriented economy.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are the CEO of an Indian car manufacturer in 1990: you need a licence to expand capacity, you cannot import the latest engine technology without government approval, and foreign competitors are barred. Suddenly, in 1991, the government announces you no longer need a licence for expansion, you can sign technology agreements freely, and foreign companies can enter your market. How do you restructure your strategy? Begin by listing the pre-1991 restrictions (licensing, MRTP limits, public sector reservations). Then map each restriction to the specific 1991 policy change that removed it. Next, trace the chain: delicensing → freedom to expand → need for competitiveness → investment in technology and human resources. Finally, connect this to the broader LPG framework and assess whether the firm gained more from new opportunities (export markets, technology) or faced greater threats (foreign competition, demanding customers). The key insight is that liberalisation did not just remove barriers; it forced a structural shift from supply-constrained to demand-driven, competition-led business models.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What were the three immediate crisis indicators in 1991 that forced the Government of India to announce the New Industrial Policy? (Recall any three from the NCERT list.)

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Correct answer: Any three of: (1) Fiscal deficit reached 6.6% of GDP, (2) Foreign exchange reserves fell to barely 2-3 weeks of import cover, (3) Rupee depreciated 26.7% against the US dollar, (4) Inflation soared to 13-14%, (5) Industrial production showed negative growth, (6) Credit rating fell from AAA to BB+. A wrong answer that cites only "high inflation" or "low growth" without the specific 1991 magnitudes reveals the student memorised general problems but not the acute crisis thresholds that triggered the policy shift.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
A student says: "Liberalisation means the government completely withdrew from the economy and let the free market run everything." Explain why this statement is incorrect using the 1991 policy's actual provisions.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Correct explanation: Liberalisation reduced controls but did not eliminate the state's role. The 1991 policy kept licensing for 18 strategic/environmental industries, reserved four sectors (atomic energy, defence, railways, minerals) for the public sector, and retained the MRTP Act (though amended) and sectoral regulators. The student's error is equating liberalisation with laissez-faire; the policy was "liberalisation with regulation," not deregulation. This misconception arises from confusing the removal of the licence-permit-quota raj with the absence of any government intervention.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A textile firm in 1990 needed a licence to expand capacity beyond its MRTP limit of ₹100 crore assets. After the 1991 policy, it wants to double capacity and import new looms. List the specific policy changes that now allow each action, and identify one new challenge the firm faces.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Correct steps: (1) Delicensing: expansion no longer requires industrial licence (except for 18 industries). (2) MRTP Act amendment: prior government approval for expansion/diversification/merger removed for large firms. (3) Import liberalisation: capital goods (looms) can be imported freely under the new EXIM policy and later EPCG scheme. (4) New challenge: foreign competitors can now enter the domestic market (FDI up to 51% in textiles), forcing the firm to compete on quality, cost, and technology. A wrong answer that misses the MRTP amendment or thinks FDI was 100% immediately shows incomplete mapping of policy-to-action.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the impact of liberalisation on a public sector steel plant (SAIL) versus a private sector electronics firm (e.g., a TV manufacturer) in the 1990s. Analyse across: (a) competitive pressure, (b) access to technology, (c) financial autonomy.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Correct analysis: (a) SAIL faced new private competitors (Tata Steel, Jindal) and import competition; the electronics firm faced MNC entry (Sony, LG) but also gained from delicensing. (b) SAIL had to modernise using internal resources or costly borrowing; the electronics firm could sign automatic technology agreements and import components freely. (c) SAIL remained under government control with budgetary support declining (loss of budgetary support); the electronics firm could raise capital via equity, FDI, and GDRs. A weak answer treats both firms identically, ignoring the privatisation dimension (disinvestment, loss of budgetary support) that uniquely affected PSUs.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
"Globalisation under the 1991 policy benefited only large Indian corporations and MNCs, while small-scale industries (SSIs) suffered." Evaluate this statement with two supporting arguments and one counter-argument based on the policy provisions.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Evaluation: Supporting: (1) SSIs lost reservation protection as many items were de-reserved, exposing them to large domestic and foreign firms. (2) Credit squeeze and high interest rates in early reform years hit SSIs harder due to weaker bargaining power. Counter: The 1991 policy explicitly assured "all help and due recognition" to the small-scale sector; later, the Investment Limit for SSIs was raised, and technology upgradation schemes (e.g., Credit Linked Capital Subsidy) were introduced. A one-sided answer that ignores the policy's stated SSI support or the later corrective measures shows evaluative bias, not balanced analysis.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often conflate "liberalisation" with "privatisation" and "globalisation," using the terms interchangeably. For example, they write "liberalisation meant selling PSUs" (that's privatisation) or "liberalisation meant free flow of goods across borders" (that's globalisation). This happens because the NCERT text introduces LPG as a combined package and the crisis narrative blends them. The exact error: attributing disinvestment of PSUs to liberalisation instead of privatisation, or attributing import tariff reduction to liberalisation instead of globalisation (trade liberalisation). The distinction matters because exam questions ask: "Explain the impact of liberalisation on business" — and marking schemes expect delicensing, freedom to expand, price decontrol, etc., not disinvestment or tariff cuts.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: An Indian automobile company in 1990 wanted to (i) increase production capacity by 50%, (ii) import robotic welding machines, (iii) form a joint venture with a Japanese firm for 40% equity, and (iv) launch a new model at a market-determined price. For each action, state whether it was allowed pre-1991 and which specific 1991 policy change enabled it post-reform.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the pre-1991 restriction for each action: (i) Capacity expansion required industrial licence and MRTP clearance if assets > ₹100 cr. (ii) Import of capital goods required import licence, often denied if domestic alternative existed. (iii) FDI was capped at 40% and needed case-by-case government approval (FIPB didn't exist). (iv) Prices of many industrial goods were administered or required government approval.
- Step 2: Map each to the 1991 policy change: (i) Delicensing (industrial licensing reduced to 6 industries) and MRTP Act amendment (prior approval removed). (ii) Import liberalisation: capital goods moved to freely importable list (later OGL/EPCG). (iii) FDI policy liberalised: automatic approval up to 51% in 34 priority industries (automobiles included), FIPB for larger proposals. (iv) Price decontrol: freedom to fix prices for most industries.
- Step 3: Synthesize the combined effect: The firm can now expand, modernise, access foreign technology/equity, and price competitively without waiting for government permissions.
- Step 4: Note the new competitive pressure: The same policies allow foreign automakers (e.g., Suzuki, Honda, Hyundai) to enter directly, turning the domestic market into a competitive arena.
- Step 5: Conclude that the firm's strategic response must shift from "managing licences" to "building competitive advantage" through quality, cost, and innovation.
REASONING: This example demonstrates how multiple policy levers (industrial, trade, foreign investment, price) moved simultaneously, and a firm's response must be holistic. It connects the specific regulatory changes to concrete business decisions.
ANSWER: (i) Not allowed pre-1991; enabled by delicensing and MRTP amendment. (ii) Not freely allowed; enabled by import liberalisation (capital goods on OGL). (iii) Allowed but cumbersome; enabled by automatic FDI route up to 51% and FIPB. (iv) Not allowed for many goods; enabled by price decontrol.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A public sector enterprise (PSE) in the non-strategic sector (e.g., a hotel chain) faced the following in 1991: (a) It had a monopoly in its segment due to entry barriers. (b) It received budgetary support for losses. (c) It could not raise capital from the market. (d) Its management was appointed by the government. Explain how each of these four features changed due to the privatisation component of the 1991 reforms, and state one new challenge the PSE's management now faces.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Recall the privatisation measures: (1) Dereservation: number of industries reserved for public sector reduced from 17 to 4 (later 3), opening the sector to private entry. (2) Disinvestment: government equity sold to public/private investors, diluting ownership. (3) Loss-making/sick units referred to BIFR for revival or closure. (4) Greater autonomy for Navratna/Miniratna PSEs in investment and management.
- Step 2: Map each feature to the change: (a) Monopoly lost due to dereservation and private entry. (b) Budgetary support declined as government shifted to disinvestment and expected PSEs to be self-sustaining. (c) Capital market access improved if disinvested (listed) or if given autonomy to raise bonds/equity. (d) Management appointment: for disinvested companies, board includes private shareholders; for Navratna, more professional autonomy.
- Step 3: Identify the new challenge: The PSE must now compete on service quality and pricing with private hotels, operate without assured government bailouts, and meet shareholder expectations for profitability — requiring a shift from bureaucratic to commercial orientation.
- Step 4: Connect to the broader impact: This illustrates "loss of budgetary support to the public sector" listed in NCERT as a key impact of policy changes.
- Step 5: Highlight that privatisation did not mean full privatisation for all; strategic PSEs remained, but the policy direction was clear.
REASONING: The example isolates the privatisation pillar (often confused with liberalisation) and traces its specific impact on a PSE's operating environment, showing the transition from protected monopoly to market discipline.
ANSWER: (a) Monopoly ended due to dereservation. (b) Budgetary support withdrawn; expected to be self-financing. (c) Can raise capital via disinvestment/listing or autonomy. (d) Management becomes more professional/accountable to board. New challenge: Compete commercially without sovereign guarantee.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Assertion (A): The New Industrial Policy 1991 abolished the MRTP Act completely.
Reason (R): The policy aimed to remove restrictions on large firms' expansion, diversification, and mergers to promote industrial growth.
Choose the correct option:
(i) Both A and R are true and R correctly explains A.
(ii) Both A and R are true but R does not explain A.
(iii) A is true but R is false.
(iv) A is false but R is true.
Justify your choice with reference to the actual amendment made to the MRTP Act in 1991.
COMPETENCY: Assertion-Reason evaluation with factual precision
STEPS:
- Step 1: Evaluate Assertion: The MRTP Act was NOT abolished in 1991; it was amended. The threshold asset limit (₹100 crore) for MRTP companies was removed, and prior government approval for expansion/diversification/merger was dispensed with. The Act itself continued (later replaced by Competition Act 2002). So A is false.
- Step 2: Evaluate Reason: The policy did aim to remove those restrictions (which it did via amendment). So R is true.
- Step 3: Select option: A is false, R is true → option (iv).
- Step 4: Justify: The 1991 amendment deleted the pre-entry scrutiny provisions (Section 20, 21, 22) but retained the Act's provisions on restrictive and unfair trade practices. The MRTP Act was fully repealed only in 2009 when the Competition Act 2002 came into force.
ANSWER: Option (iv). A is false because the MRTP Act was amended, not abolished. R is true because the amendment did remove the restrictions on large firms' expansion, diversification, and mergers.
