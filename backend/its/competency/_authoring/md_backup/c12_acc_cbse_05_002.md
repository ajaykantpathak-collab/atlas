## DEFINITION
Public Issue, Private Placement, and Rights Issue are three distinct methods through which a company can issue shares to raise capital. A **Public Issue** involves offering shares to the general public through a prospectus. A **Private Placement** involves offering shares to a select group of investors (maximum 200 in a financial year) without a public prospectus. A **Rights Issue** involves offering additional shares to existing shareholders in proportion to their current holdings, giving them a pre‑emptive right to subscribe.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine a startup, "GreenTech Ltd.", needs ₹50 crore to expand its solar panel manufacturing. The board debates three routes: (a) go to the public via an IPO, (b) approach a few venture capital funds privately, or (c) offer new shares to current shareholders first. Which route suits which situation? We begin by defining each method, then compare their investor base, disclosure norms, and speed. Next, we see that irrespective of the method, the accounting entries for share capital (share application, allotment, calls) remain similar — only the source of applications differs. The key insight: the *method of issue* affects *who subscribes* and *regulatory compliance*, but the *double‑entry bookkeeping* for share capital follows the same pattern.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
A company wants to raise capital quickly without extensive regulatory filings and is willing to limit its investor base to fewer than 200 sophisticated investors. Which method of share issue should it choose?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Correct answer: Private Placement.  
A wrong answer (e.g., Public Issue) reveals the student confuses the speed and regulatory burden: a public issue requires a prospectus, SEBI approval, and a minimum subscription of 90%, making it slower and more cumbersome. Private placement is designed for exactly this scenario — fewer investors, less disclosure, faster execution.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why a rights issue is considered a "pre‑emptive right" for existing shareholders. How does it protect them from dilution of control?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Correct answer: A rights issue offers new shares to existing shareholders in proportion to their current holdings, allowing them to maintain their percentage ownership. If they subscribe fully, their voting power and earnings per share remain unchanged.  
A wrong answer (e.g., "it gives them a discount") shows the student focuses on the price aspect rather than the core purpose: protection against dilution of control and ownership percentage.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
GreenTech Ltd. decides on a public issue of 10,00,000 equity shares of ₹10 each at a premium of ₹2 per share, payable ₹3 on application, ₹5 on allotment (including premium), and the balance on first and final call. The issue is fully subscribed. Pass the journal entries for the receipt of application money and allotment money.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Correct entries:
1. Bank A/c Dr. 30,00,000 → To Share Application A/c 30,00,000 (Application money received on 10,00,000 shares @ ₹3)
2. Share Application A/c Dr. 30,00,000 → To Share Capital A/c 30,00,000 (Application money transferred to Share Capital)
3. Share Allotment A/c Dr. 50,00,000 → To Share Capital A/c 20,00,000 → To Securities Premium Reserve A/c 30,00,000 (Allotment money due @ ₹5 including ₹2 premium)
4. Bank A/c Dr. 50,00,000 → To Share Allotment A/c 50,00,000 (Allotment money received)

A wrong entry (e.g., crediting the entire ₹5 to Share Capital) reveals the student does not separate the premium portion into Securities Premium Reserve, a mandatory requirement under Section 52 of the Companies Act, 2013.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the disclosure requirements and maximum number of investors allowed in a Public Issue vs. a Private Placement. Why does SEBI impose a limit of 200 investors for private placement in a financial year?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Correct answer: Public Issue requires a detailed prospectus, SEBI approval, and is open to unlimited investors. Private Placement requires only a Private Placement Offer Letter (PPOL), no prospectus, and is limited to 200 investors (excluding QIBs and employees) per financial year. The 200‑investor cap ensures private placement remains a "private" arrangement and does not become a de facto public issue, protecting retail investors from inadequate disclosures.  
A wrong answer (e.g., "no limit for private placement") indicates the student misses the regulatory rationale: preventing circumvention of public issue norms.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A company issues shares via rights issue at a price lower than the current market price. The CFO argues this is beneficial because it raises capital cheaply. Evaluate this argument considering the impact on existing shareholders who do not subscribe.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The argument is flawed. While the company raises capital at a lower cost, non‑participating shareholders suffer dilution of both ownership percentage and earnings per share. The "cheap" capital comes at the expense of those shareholders. A rights issue priced below market price transfers wealth from non‑subscribing to subscribing shareholders. The CFO should instead consider a market‑price rights issue or a public issue to avoid this wealth transfer.  
A wrong evaluation (e.g., "it's always good because the company gets money") shows the student ignores the distributional effect on shareholders and the concept of theoretical ex‑rights price (TERP).

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a scenario where a company would choose a Private Placement over a Public Issue and a Rights Issue. Justify your choice with three specific reasons.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Scenario: A mid‑size pharmaceutical firm needs ₹200 crore for a clinical trial. It has a few interested strategic investors (foreign pharma majors) and wants to avoid market volatility and lengthy IPO process.  
Reasons: (1) Speed — private placement can close in weeks vs. months for IPO; (2) Cost — no underwriting fees, prospectus printing, or roadshow expenses; (3) Confidentiality — sensitive trial data need not be disclosed in a public prospectus.  
A poor answer (e.g., "because it's easier") lacks specific, situation‑based reasoning and fails to link the method's features to the company's needs.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think that the accounting entries for share capital differ based on the method of issue (public, private, rights). They write separate journal entry formats for each method. In reality, the double‑entry mechanics — Share Application → Share Capital, Share Allotment → Share Capital + Securities Premium, Share Calls → Share Capital — are identical. The only difference is the *source* of the application money (public, select investors, or existing shareholders) and the *regulatory paperwork*. This misconception leads to memorising redundant entries and losing marks in board exams where a single integrated entry is expected.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Zenith Ltd. issued 5,00,000 equity shares of ₹10 each at a premium of ₹3 per share through a public issue. The amount was payable as ₹2 on application, ₹5 on allotment (including premium), and the balance on first and final call. The issue was fully subscribed and all money was received. Pass journal entries up to allotment.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate application money received: 5,00,000 shares × ₹2 = ₹10,00,000. Debit Bank A/c, Credit Share Application A/c.
- Step 2: Transfer application money to Share Capital: Debit Share Application A/c ₹10,00,000, Credit Share Capital A/c ₹10,00,000.
- Step 3: Determine allotment money due: 5,00,000 shares × ₹5 = ₹25,00,000. Of this, premium portion = 5,00,000 × ₹3 = ₹15,00,000; capital portion = ₹10,00,000. Debit Share Allotment A/c ₹25,00,000, Credit Share Capital A/c ₹10,00,000, Credit Securities Premium Reserve A/c ₹15,00,000.
- Step 4: Record receipt of allotment money: Debit Bank A/c ₹25,00,000, Credit Share Allotment A/c ₹25,00,000.
- Step 5: Verify that total capital collected so far = ₹10,00,000 (application) + ₹10,00,000 (allotment capital) = ₹20,00,000 towards nominal value, and premium collected = ₹15,00,000.

REASONING: The method is public issue, but the entries are standard for any share issue at premium. The premium is credited to Securities Premium Reserve as per Section 52 of the Companies Act, 2013. The application and allotment stages separate the cash flow timing.

ANSWER:
Journal Entries:
1. Bank A/c Dr. 10,00,000 → To Share Application A/c 10,00,000
2. Share Application A/c Dr. 10,00,000 → To Share Capital A/c 10,00,000
3. Share Allotment A/c Dr. 25,00,000 → To Share Capital A/c 10,00,000 → To Securities Premium Reserve A/c 15,00,000
4. Bank A/c Dr. 25,00,000 → To Share Allotment A/c 25,00,000

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Apex Ltd. decided to raise ₹50,00,000 by issuing equity shares of ₹100 each at par through a private placement to 50 investors. The entire amount was payable on application. The issue was fully subscribed. Pass the necessary journal entries.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Determine number of shares: ₹50,00,000 ÷ ₹100 = 50,000 shares.
- Step 2: Since the entire amount is payable on application, record receipt: Debit Bank A/c ₹50,00,000, Credit Share Application A/c ₹50,00,000.
- Step 3: On allotment, transfer application money to Share Capital: Debit Share Application A/c ₹50,00,000, Credit Share Capital A/c ₹50,00,000.
- Step 4: No separate allotment or call entries because full amount collected upfront.
- Step 5: Note that private placement does not change the accounting entries; only the investor group and regulatory filings differ.

REASONING: Private placement simplifies the payment schedule (often single payment), but the double‑entry remains: Bank → Share Application → Share Capital. No premium/discount involved, so no Securities Premium or Discount on Issue account.

ANSWER:
Journal Entries:
1. Bank A/c Dr. 50,00,000 → To Share Application A/c 50,00,000
2. Share Application A/c Dr. 50,00,000 → To Share Capital A/c 50,00,000

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Meridian Ltd. has 2,00,000 equity shares of ₹10 each outstanding. It announces a rights issue of 1 new share for every 4 held at ₹15 per share (₹5 premium). The issue is fully subscribed. Pass journal entries for the rights issue assuming the entire amount is payable on application.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate rights shares: 2,00,000 × 1/4 = 50,000 shares.
- Step 2: Issue price per share = ₹15 (₹10 nominal + ₹5 premium). Total receipt = 50,000 × ₹15 = ₹7,50,000.
- Step 3: Record application money from existing shareholders: Debit Bank A/c ₹7,50,000, Credit Share Application A/c ₹7,50,000.
- Step 4: On allotment, split into capital and premium: Debit Share Application A/c ₹7,50,000, Credit Share Capital A/c (50,000 × ₹10) = ₹5,00,000, Credit Securities Premium Reserve A/c (50,000 × ₹5) = ₹2,50,000.
- Step 5: Verify that post‑issue share capital = 2,50,000 shares × ₹10 = ₹25,00,000; Securities Premium Reserve increases by ₹2,50,000.

REASONING: Rights issue is offered to existing shareholders proportionately. The accounting is identical to a public issue at premium — only the subscriber base differs. The premium is credited to Securities Premium Reserve.

ANSWER:
Journal Entries:
1. Bank A/c Dr. 7,50,000 → To Share Application A/c 7,50,000
2. Share Application A/c Dr. 7,50,000 → To Share Capital A/c 5,00,000 → To Securities Premium Reserve A/c 2,50,000

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A company, SolarMax Ltd., plans to raise ₹100 crore. It has three options: (A) Public Issue at ₹120 per share (face value ₹100), (B) Private Placement at ₹115 per share to 100 institutional investors, (C) Rights Issue at ₹110 per share to existing shareholders. The current market price is ₹130. The board wants to minimize dilution of control for existing promoters who hold 40% and minimize issue expenses. Analyze each option and recommend the best one, justifying with two reasons per option.

COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying financial decisions using accounting and regulatory knowledge

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the key criteria: dilution of promoter control, issue expenses, speed, and regulatory burden.
- Step 2: Analyze Public Issue: high expenses (underwriting, prospectus, marketing), dilute promoters if they don't subscribe, lengthy SEBI process.
- Step 3: Analyze Private Placement: lower expenses, only 100 investors, fast, but promoters' stake dilutes unless they participate; also, price ₹115 is below market ₹130, so wealth transfer to new investors.
- Step 4: Analyze Rights Issue: promoters can subscribe to maintain 40% (anti‑dilution), price ₹110 is a discount to market but offered pro‑rata, so no wealth transfer if all subscribe; expenses lower than public issue.
- Step 5: Recommend Rights Issue if promoters are willing and able to invest; otherwise Private Placement if speed and certainty are paramount.

ANSWER: Rights Issue is best if promoters subscribe — it protects their 40% holding, has lower expenses than public issue, and the discount is shared proportionately. Private Placement is second best for speed and lower cost but dilutes promoters. Public Issue is least suitable due to high cost, time, and dilution.

1. Bank A/c Dr. 1,20,000 → To Share Capital A/c 1,20,000
2. Share Application A/c Dr. 1,20,000 → To Share Capital A/c 1,00,000 → To Securities Premium Reserve A/c 20,000

COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction in accounting entries

STEPS:
- Step 1: Check the first entry: Bank should be debited and Share Application credited (not Share Capital directly) because money is received on application.
- Step 2: Check the second entry: Share Application should be debited (not credited again) and split between Share Capital and Securities Premium Reserve.

ANSWER:
Errors:
1. First entry credits Share Capital directly; it should credit Share Application A/c.
2. Second entry debits Share Application A/c again (already credited in first entry) but the first entry didn't create a Share Application balance. Correct sequence:
   (i) Bank A/c Dr. 1,20,000 → To Share Application A/c 1,20,000
   (ii) Share Application A/c Dr. 1,20,000 → To Share Capital A/c 1,00,000 → To Securities Premium Reserve A/c 20,000
