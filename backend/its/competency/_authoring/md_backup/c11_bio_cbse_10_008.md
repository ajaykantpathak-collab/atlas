## DEFINITION
Meiosis is a specialized reductional cell division in diploid germ cells that produces four genetically unique haploid gametes through two sequential divisions (Meiosis I and II) following a single DNA replication, thereby halving the chromosome number and generating genetic variation via crossing over and independent assortment.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
analyze

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
Student can explain how crossing over at chiasmata during pachytene and random orientation of bivalents at metaphase I create recombinant chromosomes and independent assortment, and can calculate the number of possible gamete combinations (2ⁿ) for a given haploid number n.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers the two sources of genetic variation in meiosis (crossing over and independent assortment), their cytological basis, and evolutionary significance. Does NOT cover molecular mechanisms of recombination (e.g., Holliday junctions, mismatch repair) or population genetics equations (Hardy-Weinberg).

## WALKTHROUGH
Observe a family photograph: siblings share parents but look different — different eye colour, height, hair texture. This variation arises because each sperm and egg carries a unique genetic combination. Start by recalling that homologous chromosomes pair in prophase I (synapsis) and exchange segments at chiasmata. Then visualize metaphase I: each bivalent aligns independently, so maternal and paternal chromosomes assort randomly. Trace how these two events shuffle alleles into new combinations never seen in either parent. The key principle: meiosis creates variation; fertilization samples it.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Define crossing over and state the exact stage of prophase I when it occurs, naming the enzyme responsible.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Crossing over is the reciprocal exchange of genetic material between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes, occurring during pachytene stage of prophase I, mediated by the enzyme recombinase. A wrong answer (e.g., "occurs in metaphase I" or "involves sister chromatids") reveals confusion between homologous vs. sister chromatid interactions and misplacement of the event in the meiotic timeline.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why independent assortment of chromosomes during metaphase I generates genetic variation, but independent assortment of sister chromatids in metaphase II does not.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
In metaphase I, homologous chromosomes (each carrying different alleles) orient randomly at the equator, so maternal and paternal chromosomes segregate independently into gametes, creating new allele combinations. In metaphase II, sister chromatids are identical copies (barring crossing over), so their separation does not produce new combinations. A wrong answer indicates failure to distinguish between segregation of homologous chromosomes vs. sister chromatids and their allelic content.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A diploid organism has 2n = 6 chromosomes. Calculate the number of genetically distinct gamete types possible from independent assortment alone, assuming no crossing over. Show your reasoning.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Haploid number n = 3. Independent assortment yields 2ⁿ = 2³ = 8 distinct gamete combinations. Reasoning: each homologous pair segregates independently, giving 2 choices per pair; total combinations = product of choices = 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. A wrong answer (e.g., 6 or 4) reveals misunderstanding of the 2ⁿ formula or confusion between chromosome number and haploid number.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the genetic composition of the four daughter cells produced by meiosis in a heterozygous individual (Aa) for a single gene, with and without crossing over between the gene locus and the centromere.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Without crossing over: two cells get A, two get a (parental types only). With crossing over between locus and centromere: recombinant chromatids form, so all four cells can be genetically distinct (A, a, and two recombinants if multiple crossovers). A wrong answer (e.g., "always four different types") shows failure to link crossover position relative to centromere and gene locus to the segregation pattern in meiosis II.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate the statement: "Meiosis reduces chromosome number by half, so it decreases genetic diversity in the population." Justify your answer with two specific mechanisms.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is false. Although meiosis halves chromosome number, it increases genetic diversity via (1) crossing over — creating recombinant chromosomes with new allele combinations, and (2) independent assortment — shuffling maternal/paternal chromosomes randomly into gametes. Fertilization then combines two such varied gametes. A wrong evaluation indicates conflation of chromosome number reduction with loss of genetic information, ignoring the combinatorial explosion of allelic arrangements.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple experiment using a model organism (e.g., Drosophila or Neurospora) to demonstrate that crossing over produces recombinant phenotypes in offspring. Outline the cross, the marker genes needed, and the expected phenotypic ratios.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Use Drosophila: cross a homozygous wild-type fly (vg⁺ vg⁺; b⁺ b⁺) with a double recessive (vg vg; b b) where vg = vestigial wings, b = black body. F1 heterozygotes (vg⁺ vg; b⁺ b) are test-crossed to double recessive. Parental phenotypes: wild-type and vestigial-black. Recombinant phenotypes: vestigial-normal body and wild-type-black. Recombination frequency = (recombinants/total) × 100%. A poor design (e.g., using unlinked genes or no test cross) reveals ignorance of linkage mapping principles and the need for a test cross to reveal gamete genotypes.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think crossing over occurs between sister chromatids because they look identical, or that independent assortment happens in metaphase II because chromatids separate then. They also confuse "reduction of chromosome number" with "reduction of genetic variation," not realizing that halving the chromosome set is precisely what allows fertilization to restore diploidy while combining two independently shuffled genomes.

## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Draw a single homologous pair with heterozygous alleles (A/a) on a whiteboard. Step 2: Show synapsis and chiasma formation between non-sister chromatids — label alleles before and after exchange. Step 3: Animate metaphase I: flip a coin for each pair to decide which pole gets maternal vs. paternal chromosome. Step 4: Count distinct gamete genotypes after one meiosis. Step 5: Repeat for 3 pairs to see 2ⁿ combinations. This visual, hands-on tracing corrects the sister-chromatid and metaphase-II errors.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: In a diploid plant with 2n = 8, a gene for flower colour (R = red, r = white) is on chromosome 2, and a gene for seed shape (S = round, s = wrinkled) is on chromosome 5. The plant is heterozygous for both genes (Rr Ss). Assuming no crossing over, list all possible gamete genotypes and explain how independent assortment produces them.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Determine haploid number n = 4 (since 2n = 8). Chromosome pairs: 1, 2, 3, 4.
- Step 2: Identify which chromosomes carry the genes: chromosome 2 has R/r, chromosome 5 has S/s (but there are only 4 pairs, so chromosome 5 is actually pair 3 or 4 — relabel as chromosome 3 for consistency).
- Step 3: In metaphase I, each bivalent aligns independently. For chromosome 2 pair, two orientations: R to pole A / r to pole B, or r to pole A / R to pole B. Same for chromosome 3 pair with S/s.
- Step 4: Combine orientations: 2 choices for chr2 × 2 choices for chr3 = 4 gamete types: RS, Rs, rS, rs. Chromosomes 1 and 4 segregate independently but carry no marked genes.
- Step 5: Verify: each gamete gets one allele per gene, all combinations equally likely (1/4 each). This is Mendel's independent assortment.
REASONING: Independent assortment acts on whole chromosomes, not individual genes. Genes on different chromosomes assort independently because their chromosomes orient randomly at metaphase I. The 2ⁿ formula counts combinations of chromosome sets, not genes.
ANSWER: Four gamete genotypes: RS, Rs, rS, rs each at 25% frequency.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A human male (2n = 46) is heterozygous for a gene on chromosome 12. During meiosis, a single crossover occurs between the gene locus and the centromere on one of the two homologous chromosome 12 pairs. Describe the genetic composition of the four resulting sperm cells for that gene.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Before meiosis, chromosome 12 homologues: one carries allele A, the other a. Each has replicated into two sister chromatids (AA and aa).
- Step 2: In prophase I, synapsis occurs. Crossing over between non-sister chromatids at a point between the gene and centromere exchanges segments. Now chromatids are: A (unchanged), a (unchanged), recombinant A-with-a-segment, recombinant a-with-A-segment.
- Step 3: Anaphase I separates homologous chromosomes (each still composed of two chromatids). One daughter cell gets the chromosome with chromatids A and recombinant-a; the other gets a and recombinant-A.
- Step 4: Meiosis II separates sister chromatids. First cell yields two sperm: one with A, one with recombinant-a. Second cell yields two sperm: one with a, one with recombinant-A.
- Step 5: Result: four sperm with four distinct alleles at that locus (two parental, two recombinant). This demonstrates how crossing over increases variation beyond independent assortment.
REASONING: Crossing over between gene and centromere ensures recombinant chromatids segregate into different gametes in meiosis II. If crossover were between gene and telomere, the gene would not be recombined.
ANSWER: Four genetically distinct sperm: A, a, recombinant-A, recombinant-a (each 25%).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: In Drosophila, genes for wing shape (vg⁺ = normal, vg = vestigial) and body colour (b⁺ = grey, b = black) are on the same chromosome (autosome 2). A test cross of a dihybrid female (vg⁺ vg; b⁺ b) with a double recessive male yields 1000 offspring: 420 normal-grey, 410 vestigial-black, 90 normal-black, 80 vestigial-grey. Calculate recombination frequency and explain what it reveals about the meiotic mechanism.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify parental phenotypes (most frequent): normal-grey (vg⁺ b⁺) and vestigial-black (vg b) — these are non-recombinant gametes from the female.
- Step 2: Identify recombinant phenotypes (less frequent): normal-black (vg⁺ b) and vestigial-grey (vg b⁺) — these arise from crossing over in the female.
- Step 3: Count recombinants: 90 + 80 = 170. Total offspring = 1000.
- Step 4: Recombination frequency = (170/1000) × 100% = 17%. This means 17% of female gametes were recombinant.
- Step 5: Interpret: The genes are linked (on same chromosome) but crossing over in prophase I separates them 17% of the time. The 17 map units distance reflects the physical distance between loci.
- Step 6: Connect to meiosis: Each crossover event in a bivalent involves two of four chromatids. A single crossover between these loci produces 50% recombinant chromatids in that meiosis. The observed 17% RF indicates crossovers occur in 34% of meioses (since each crossover yields 50% recombinants).
REASONING: Recombination frequency directly measures crossing over frequency in meiosis. Linked genes do not assort independently; their recombination frequency < 50% reveals physical linkage. The test cross exposes the gamete genotypes from the heterozygous parent.
ANSWER: Recombination frequency = 17%. Genes are linked, 17 map units apart. Crossing over in prophase I of female meiosis produces recombinant gametes.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A species of lizard has 2n = 36 chromosomes. During meiosis in a female, one bivalent fails to undergo crossing over. Explain how this affects genetic variation in the resulting eggs compared to a normal meiosis, and calculate the reduction in possible gamete combinations for that chromosome pair.
COMPETENCY: Predicting consequences of biological changes
STEPS:
- Step 1: Normal meiosis: independent assortment gives 2ⁿ combinations (n=18 → 2¹⁸ total). For each pair, crossing over creates additional recombinant chromatids beyond parental types.
- Step 2: Without crossing over on one pair, the two homologues segregate as whole chromosomes — only two allele combinations (parental) for genes on that chromosome, instead of many recombinant types.
- Step 3: For that specific pair, number of distinct gamete genotypes drops from "many" (depending on heterozygosity and crossover positions) to exactly 2 (maternal or paternal haplotype).
- Step 4: Overall gamete diversity = (combinations from other 17 pairs) × 2 instead of × (many). Quantify: if that pair had k heterozygous loci, crossing over could produce up to 2ᵏ haplotypes; without it, only 2.
- Step 5: Conclude: Variation is reduced but not eliminated; independent assortment of other pairs and crossing over on them still generate diversity.
ANSWER: Failure of crossing over on one bivalent eliminates recombinant haplotypes for genes on that chromosome, reducing gamete genotypes for that pair to only the two parental combinations. Total possible gamete combinations decrease from 2¹⁸ × (recombinant diversity) to 2¹⁸ × 2 for that pair's contribution.
DIFFICULTY: medium

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: In a flowering plant, a diploid megaspore mother cell (2n = 14) undergoes meiosis to produce four haploid megaspores, three of which degenerate. The functional megaspore undergoes three mitotic divisions to form the 8-nucleate embryo sac. If the mother cell is heterozygous for two linked genes (A/a and B/b) with 20% recombination frequency, what proportion of the final embryo sacs will contain only parental haplotypes (AB or ab) for these genes? Assume no crossing over in mitosis.
COMPETENCY: Interpreting cause-effect in life processes
STEPS:
- Step 1: Meiosis in megaspore mother cell: produces 4 megaspores. Each gets one of the four chromatids from the bivalent after meiosis I and II.
- Step 2: With 20% RF, 20% of meiotic products are recombinant (Ab, aB), 80% parental (AB, ab). Since 4 products per meiosis, expected: 3.2 parental, 0.8 recombinant chromatids.
- Step 3: Only one megaspore survives (random). Probability it carries a parental haplotype = 0.8 (80%).
- Step 4: That megaspore undergoes three mitotic divisions (no crossing over). All 8 nuclei in the embryo sac are clonal — identical haplotype.
- Step 5: Therefore, 80% of embryo sacs will have only parental haplotypes (either all AB or all ab). The other 20% will have only recombinant haplotypes.
- Step 6: Note: This differs from animals where all four meiotic products become gametes. Here, only one survives, so the embryo sac is genetically uniform.
ANSWER: 80% of embryo sacs will contain only parental haplotypes (AB or ab). The surviving megaspore's genotype determines the entire embryo sac because subsequent mitotic divisions are clonal.
DIFFICULTY: hard
