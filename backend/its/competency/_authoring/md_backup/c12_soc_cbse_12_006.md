## DEFINITION
Trade unions in India are formal organisations of workers formed to protect and promote their collective interests — primarily wages, working conditions, job security, and social security — through collective bargaining, legal advocacy, and, when necessary, industrial action such as strikes. The three major national federations — AITUC (All India Trade Union Congress, 1920), INTUC (Indian National Trade Union Congress, 1947), and CITU (Centre of Indian Trade Unions, 1970) — differ in their political affiliations (communist, Congress, and Marxist-Leninist respectively) but share the core function of mediating labour–capital relations in both organised and unorganised sectors.

You hear Dr. Datta Samant urging workers to strike for a union of their own choosing, not the management-approved one. Why did 2.5 lakh workers risk starvation for nearly two years? ## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
The NCERT text states: "Very few people in India have access to secure jobs with benefits. Of those who do, two-thirds work for the government." Explain why this fact directly undermines trade union strength in the private organised sector.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Secure jobs with benefits (pension, grievance redressal, legal protection) are concentrated in the public sector, where unions have institutionalised bargaining channels (e.g., Joint Consultative Machinery). In the private organised sector, liberalisation since 1991 has accelerated outsourcing, contract labour, and "just-in-time" production (as in the Maruti Udyog example), making permanent employment rare. Workers in precarious jobs fear victimisation if they unionise — the NCERT notes "it is more difficult for trade unions to organise in smaller firms." A wrong answer that focuses only on "anti-union laws" misses the structural point: informalisation of even formal-sector work erodes the material basis for unionisation.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A factory employs 500 workers: 200 permanent, 200 contract (through a contractor), and 100 casual daily-wage. The permanent workers want to form a union. The contractor threatens to replace contract workers if they join. Using the concept of "fragmentation of the workforce," explain why the union drive will likely fail even if permanent workers are supportive.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The workforce is segmented into three tiers with divergent interests and vulnerabilities: (1) Permanent workers have job security and legal standing to unionise under the Trade Unions Act. (2) Contract workers are employed by the contractor, not the principal employer — their "employer" can terminate them without the factory's direct involvement, and the contractor controls their wages. (3) Casual workers have no continuity of employment. The contractor's threat exploits this legal fissure: contract workers cannot legally strike against the principal employer, and the contractor can blacklist them across sites. The NCERT describes this in the "contractor system" section: "the loaned money is treated as an advance wage and the worker works without wages until the loan is repaid" — debt bondage coexists with contractual fragmentation. A wrong answer that says "all workers can join one union" ignores the legal reality that the Trade Unions Act requires a common employer, which contract workers lack.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the political orientation of AITUC, INTUC, and CITU. How does each federation's party affiliation shape its strategy toward the state — collaboration, confrontation, or conditional cooperation? Give one historical example per federation.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
AITUC (CPI-linked, originally broad nationalist-communist): Historically oscillated between collaboration (e.g., supporting Congress ministries 1937–39) and confrontation (e.g., 1946–47 strikes against colonial state). Post-1964 split, the CPI-aligned AITUC pursued "united front" tactics — conditional cooperation with Congress-led governments on labour legislation (e.g., 1970s labour law amendments). INTUC (Congress-linked): Founded 1947 to anchor labour in the nationalist project; strategy is institutional collaboration — works through state mechanisms (e.g., Indian Labour Conference, tripartite bodies) and avoids militant strikes that could embarrass Congress governments. Example: INTUC's role in the 1972 amendment to the Industrial Disputes Act strengthening layoff protections. CITU (CPI(M)-linked, formed 1970): Explicitly class-struggle orientation; rejects collaboration with "bourgeois state." Strategy: militant mobilisation, general strikes (e.g., 1974 railway strike led by George Fernandes — though he was socialist, CITU supported), and building unions in unorganised sectors (e.g., anganwadi workers, beedi rollers). A wrong answer that treats all three as "left-wing unions" misses the strategic divergence: INTUC seeks legislative gains via state access; CITU seeks power via mass disruption; AITUC (post-split) seeks united-front pressure.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Critique the statement: "The decline of trade unions in India after 1991 is solely due to anti-labour policies of liberalisation." Use evidence from the NCERT text on informalisation, contract labour, and the changing nature of work.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is reductionist. Liberalisation accelerated decline but did not initiate it. NCERT evidence: (1) Pre-1991, only 24% of workers were in regular salaried employment; over 52% self-employed — unions never had a broad base. (2) The Bombay Textile Strike (1982) — pre-liberalisation — failed because mill owners refused to modernise, workers lost jobs, and the union (RMMS) was seen as compromised. (3) Post-1991, outsourcing and "just-in-time" production (Maruti example) fragmented the workforce further: permanent jobs shrank, contract labour rose, and "it is more difficult for trade unions to organise in smaller firms." (4) The state withdrew from job creation: "employment by the government was a major avenue for increasing well-being… but now even that is coming down." Unions declined because the social structure of work shifted from stable, large-factory employment to precarious, dispersed, informalised labour — a structural transformation that liberalisation intensified but did not create. A wrong answer blaming only "neo-liberal policies" ignores the pre-existing weakness documented in the 1972–2019 employment status chart.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a union organising strategy for home-based women bidi rollers in Nipani (Karnataka) described in the NCERT. Your strategy must address: (a) the piece-rate payment system, (b) the contractor's control over raw materials and product collection, (c) the workers' domestic responsibilities, and (d) the lack of a common workplace. Justify each element.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A viable strategy: (1) **Form a "worker-producer cooperative" instead of a traditional union** — since there is no single employer, the cooperative collectively negotiates with contractors/manufacturers for piece rates, cutting out the contractor's margin. NCERT notes: "The contractor picks up these bidis and sells them to the manufacturer" — the cooperative replaces this link. (2) **Demand statutory minimum piece-rates under the Minimum Wages Act** — file a collective petition with the Labour Commissioner using the "scheduled employment" notification for bidi rolling. (3) **Leverage the Self-Help Group (SHG) structure** — women already meet for savings/credit; piggyback union meetings on SHG gatherings to overcome domestic time poverty. (4) **Use the Unorganised Workers' Social Security Act 2008** — register workers for life/disability cover, making membership tangible. (5) **Build a regional federation across villages** — since work is home-based, the union's "workplace" is the village cluster; a federation creates critical mass for bargaining. Justification: Traditional unions require a common employer and workplace — both absent here. The cooperative model converts individual piece-workers into collective sellers, shifting power from contractor to workers. A poor answer proposes "register a union under the Trade Unions Act" — legally impossible without an employer-employee relationship.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students conflate "trade union" with "political party front" and assume all unions are externally controlled. They write: "AITUC is communist, INTUC is Congress, CITU is CPI(M) — so workers have no real voice." This misses two NCERT-grounded realities: (1) Unions *are* the primary vehicle for workers to exercise collective agency in a labour market where individual bargaining fails — the Bombay Textile Strike (1982) was *led* by workers demanding "the right to form their own union" against a management-backed union. (2) Political affiliation provides resources (legal aid, parliamentary access, cadre) that independent unions lack — but it also creates tensions (e.g., CITU's 1974 railway strike vs. INTUC's opposition). The error is treating political linkage as *deterministic* rather than *strategic*: unions negotiate autonomy within party structures. Students also confuse "union density" (members/total workforce) with "union presence" — India has high union *visibility* in organised sector but low *density* overall (~10%) because 90%+ workforce is unorganised.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A sociologist surveys 1,000 workers in a metropolitan city: 300 in a public sector steel plant (permanent), 200 in a private auto ancillary unit (150 permanent, 50 contract), 300 home-based garment workers (piece-rate), 200 casual construction workers. Estimate the maximum potential union membership if: (a) all public sector permanents are unionised (typical density 80%), (b) private sector permanents at 40%, (c) contract workers at 5% (legal barriers), (d) home-based at 2% (no common employer), (e) casual at 1%. Show calculations and interpret the result for union density.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify each worker category and its assumed unionisation rate based on sector and employment type.
- Step 2: Calculate unionised workers per category: Public permanents = 300 × 0.80 = 240; Private permanents = 150 × 0.40 = 60; Contract = 50 × 0.05 = 2.5 ≈ 2; Home-based = 300 × 0.02 = 6; Casual = 200 × 0.01 = 2.
- Step 3: Sum unionised workers = 240 + 60 + 2 + 6 + 2 = 310.
- Step 4: Total workforce surveyed = 1,000. Union density = (310 / 1,000) × 100 = 31%.
- Step 5: Interpret: Despite 31% density in this sample (skewed by public sector), the *national* density is ~10% because public sector is only ~5% of total workforce. The example shows how sectoral composition distorts density — a city with large public sector (e.g., Bhilai, Bokaro) will have higher union visibility than the national average.

REASONING: This calculation operationalises the NCERT's claim that "very few people have access to secure jobs with benefits. Of those who do, two-thirds work for the government." Union density is not uniform — it concentrates where employment is stable and legal protections exist. The low rates for contract, home-based, and casual workers reflect the "legal fissures" and fragmentation discussed in Diagnostic Question 3.

ANSWER: 310 unionised workers out of 1,000 = 31% density in this sample; national density ~10% due to small public sector share.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: The Bombay Textile Strike (1982) involved 2.5 lakh workers. Mill owners refused to recognise the new union (Maharashtra Girni Kamgar Union led by Datta Samant) and continued dealing with the established Rashtriya Mill Mazdoor Sangh (RMMS, INTUC-affiliated). After 22 months, 1 lakh workers lost jobs permanently. Using the concepts of "union recognition," "inter-union rivalry," and "employer strategy," explain why the strike failed despite massive participation.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Define the core conflict — not just wages, but *which union* represents workers. Workers wanted democratic representation; RMMS was seen as management-friendly.
- Step 2: Analyse employer strategy — mill owners recognised RMMS (legal under Maharashtra Recognition of Trade Unions Act) and refused to negotiate with MGKU. They used "badli" (substitute) workers and waited out the strike.
- Step 3: Analyse inter-union rivalry — INTUC (RMMS) vs. independent socialist (MGKU) split the workforce. RMMS had legal recognition, access to labour courts, and political patronage.
- Step 4: Assess structural factors — mills were already uncompetitive (NCERT: "Mill owners did not invest in machinery and modernisation"). Owners used the strike to shed labour and later sell land for real estate.
- Step 5: Synthesise — the strike failed because it challenged the *institutional architecture* of industrial relations (recognised union, labour courts, political patronage) without state support. Workers' "right to form their own union" had no legal enforcement mechanism against employer refusal.

REASONING: The NCERT presents this strike as a turning point: it marked the decline of Mumbai's textile industry and the rise of powerloom towns (Bhiwandi, Malegaon) with *no* union presence. The failure was not due to worker apathy (22 months is extraordinary endurance) but to the asymmetry of power: employers + state + recognised union vs. unrecognised union + workers. This illustrates the gap between constitutional right (Article 19) and effective collective bargaining.

ANSWER: Strike failed due to employer refusal to recognise the new union, legal backing for the rival INTUC union, mill owners' disinvestment strategy, and absence of state mediation — not worker weakness.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Compare the organising strategy of CITU among anganwadi workers (Integrated Child Development Services scheme) with INTUC's strategy among railway workers. Both are government scheme employees, not regular civil servants. Why does CITU succeed in building militant unions among anganwadi workers while INTUC focuses on institutional negotiation for railway workers?

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the employment status — anganwadi workers are "honorary" part-time workers paid honorarium (₹4,500–10,000/month), not employees; railway workers include permanent, contract, and casual tiers.
- Step 2: Map the employer — anganwadi workers' employer is ambiguous (state government? central scheme? local panchayat?); railway workers have a clear employer (Indian Railways, a state monopoly).
- Step 3: Analyse CITU's strategy — since no legal recognition is possible, CITU uses *movement politics*: dharnas, gheraos, court petitions framing honorarium as "wages," demanding minimum wage and social security. Example: 2018–19 all-India anganwadi strikes led by CITU-affiliated federations.
- Step 4: Analyse INTUC's strategy — in railways, INTUC (via AIRF/NFIR) uses *institutional channels*: Permanent Negotiating Machinery (PNM), Joint Consultative Machinery, Railway Board meetings. They negotiate pay commissions, pension, allowances — gains are incremental but legally enforceable.
- Step 5: Explain the divergence — CITU organises where *no institution exists* (creating pressure from outside); INTUC operates where *institutions exist* (working inside them). CITU's class-struggle ideology fits the "no rights" condition of scheme workers; INTUC's collaborationist ideology fits the "established rights" condition of railway workers.
- Step 6: Connect to NCERT — both groups are in the "organised sector" legally (government employment) but anganwadi workers are *informalised within the state* — a new frontier for unions.

REASONING: This comparison reveals that union strategy is not determined by ideology alone but by the *institutional terrain* of the workforce. The NCERT's discussion of "government employment overcoming caste/religion boundaries" applies to railway workers (permanent) but not anganwadi workers (scheme-based). CITU's success with the latter shows unions can adapt to informalised state employment — a post-1991 phenomenon.

ANSWER: CITU uses extra-institutional mobilisation for workers with no legal employer recognition; INTUC uses intra-institutional negotiation for workers with established bargaining structures. Strategy follows institutional terrain, not just ideology.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: The NCERT states: "In India, over 90% of the work… is in the unorganised or informal sector… very few people are members of unions, a feature of the organised sector." Yet, the 2020–21 farmers' protest saw massive participation by agricultural workers (largely unorganised) coordinated by unions like AIKS (All India Kisan Sabha, CPI-linked) and BKU (Bharatiya Kisan Union, independent). 
(a) Explain why agricultural workers — technically "cultivators" or "agricultural labourers," not industrial workers — are organised by *peasant unions* (kisan sabhas) rather than trade unions. 
(b) Using the concept of "class fraction" (from Marx via c11_soc_cbse_09_003), analyse whether the 2020–21 protest represented a convergence of *industrial working class* and *agrarian class* interests, or a tactical alliance. 
(c) What does this imply for the future of trade unionism in India — should unions expand beyond "industrial workers" to "working people"?

COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying — using historical-materialist concepts (class fraction, mode of production) to analyse a contemporary mobilisation that blurs sectoral boundaries.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Distinguish peasant unions vs. trade unions — peasant unions organise around *land, credit, state procurement prices* (reproduction of petty production); trade unions organise around *wage labour, working conditions, employer relations*. Agricultural labourers (landless) are the overlap — they belong to both classes.
- Step 2: Apply "class fraction" — Marx: classes fragment into fractions based on position in production. Rich peasants (capitalist farmers), middle peasants (petty commodity producers), poor peasants (semi-proletarian), agricultural labourers (proletarian). The 2020 laws (APMC bypass, contract farming) threatened *all* fractions but differently: rich peasants lost mandi monopoly; poor peasants/labourers lost MSP safety net.
- Step 3: Assess convergence vs. alliance — The protest unity was *tactical*: SKUM (Samyukta Kisan Morcha) coordinated 500+ organisations with divergent demands (repeal laws vs. legalise MSP vs. loan waiver). Industrial unions (AITUC, CITU, INTUC) supported *in solidarity* but did not merge demands. No shared organisational form emerged.
- Step 4: Future implication — Trade unions *must* expand to "working people" (informal, platform, agrarian, domestic) because the industrial working class is shrinking (<17% in manufacturing). But this requires new organisational forms (welfare boards, sectoral councils, digital collectives) — not just extending the TU Act.

ANSWER: (a) Peasant unions address agrarian reproduction (land, price, credit); trade unions address wage-labour exploitation. Agricultural labourers straddle both. (b) Tactical alliance — class fractions united against a common threat (corporate agriculture) but retain distinct reproduction interests. (c) Unions must become "movements of the working poor" with flexible forms — the TU Act is obsolete for 90% of workers; new legal architectures (sectoral boards, platform cooperatives) are needed.
