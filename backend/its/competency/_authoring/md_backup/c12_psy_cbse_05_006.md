## DEFINITION
Cognitive therapy is a structured, short-term psychotherapy that identifies and modifies distorted thinking patterns (cognitions) causing emotional distress and maladaptive behaviours; Ellis' Rational Emotive Therapy (RET) uses the ABC model (Antecedent-Belief-Consequence) to dispute irrational "must/should" beliefs, while Beck's Cognitive Therapy targets negative automatic thoughts and core schemas through collaborative cognitive restructuring.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine your friend says, "I failed one test — I'm a total failure and will never succeed in life." You notice they jump from one event to a global self-verdict. This is exactly what cognitive therapy addresses: the *interpretation* of events, not the events themselves. Start by learning the ABC framework — write down a recent upsetting situation (A), your immediate thought (B), and your feeling/action (C). Then ask: "Is this thought a 'must/should' demand? What evidence supports/contradicts it?" For Beck's approach, identify the "negative automatic thought" (e.g., "I'm stupid"), name the distortion (e.g., labelling), and generate a balanced alternative ("I did poorly on this test; I can study differently next time"). The key insight: emotions follow *beliefs*, not events directly — changing the belief changes the emotion.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
State the full form of ABC in Ellis' Rational Emotive Therapy and briefly explain what each letter represents in the sequence of emotional disturbance.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
ABC stands for **Antecedent (Activating event) — Belief — Consequence**.  
- **A (Antecedent):** The external event or situation that triggers the reaction (e.g., "Friend didn't reply to my message").  
- **B (Belief):** The *irrational belief* the client holds about the event, typically containing rigid "musts/shoulds" (e.g., "Everyone *must* respond immediately; if they don't, it means I'm unlovable").  
- **C (Consequence):** The emotional and behavioural outcome *caused by the belief*, not the event itself (e.g., intense anxiety, withdrawal, sending angry follow-up texts).  

A wrong answer (e.g., "A = Anxiety, B = Behaviour, C = Consequence") reveals the student confuses the *order* of the model or mistakes the consequence for the belief. The critical point: **B mediates between A and C** — the same event (A) produces different consequences (C) depending on the belief (B).

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
A student thinks: "If I don't score 95% in Board exams, I am a complete failure and my life is ruined." Identify **two** irrational belief characteristics (per Ellis) in this statement and explain why each is irrational.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Two irrational characteristics:  
1. **Demandingness ("must/should"):** The thought implies "I *must* score 95%." Ellis calls this a *rigid demand* — it converts a preference ("I'd like to score well") into an absolute requirement. It is irrational because reality does not guarantee outcomes; tying self-worth to an uncontrollable result creates inevitable distress.  
2. **Awfulising/Catastrophising:** "My life is ruined" exaggerates the negative consequence to an unbearable, endless catastrophe. It is irrational because (a) one exam cannot determine an entire life trajectory, and (b) humans adapt and recover — the belief denies this resilience.  

A wrong answer listing "negative thinking" or "low confidence" misses Ellis' specific *structural* criteria: **demandingness, awfulising, low frustration tolerance, and global rating of self/others**. The student must name the *category* of irrationality, not just describe the thought as "negative."

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
**Case:** Rahul, 17, is not selected for the school football team. He thinks: "The coach hates me. I'll never make any team. I'm useless at sports."  
Using **Beck's model**, identify: (a) the **negative automatic thought (NAT)**, (b) the **cognitive distortion** (name it), and (c) the **likely core schema** activated.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
(a) **Negative Automatic Thought (NAT):** "The coach hates me. I'll never make any team. I'm useless at sports." (The *entire cluster* is the NAT — persistent, involuntary, and accepted as true.)  
(b) **Cognitive Distortion:** **Overgeneralisation** (drawing a sweeping negative conclusion from a single event) + **Labelling** ("I'm useless") + **Fortune-telling** ("I'll never make any team"). *Any one correctly named with explanation earns credit.*  
(c) **Core Schema:** "I am incompetent / inadequate / unlovable" — a deep, enduring belief formed from early experiences (e.g., repeated criticism from parents) that lies dormant until a critical incident (non-selection) activates it.  

A wrong answer confusing NAT with core schema (e.g., writing "Core schema: The coach hates me") shows the student doesn't grasp the *hierarchy*: **Core schema (deep, global) → Critical incident → NATs (situation-specific, multiple) → Distortions (thinking errors in NATs).**

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
**Compare:** How does the *therapist's role* differ in Ellis' RET vs. Beck's Cognitive Therapy during the belief-modification phase? Give one specific technique each uses.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
| Aspect | Ellis (RET) | Beck (Cognitive Therapy) |
|--------|-------------|--------------------------|
| **Therapist stance** | **Active-directive philosopher-teacher**: Directly *challenges* irrational beliefs as illogical/unscientific. Uses **disputation** (D) — logical, empirical, and pragmatic questioning: *"Where is the evidence that you MUST succeed? How does demanding perfection help you?"* | **Collaborative empiricist / Socratic guide**: Treats beliefs as *hypotheses to test*. Uses **guided discovery / Socratic questioning**: *"What evidence supports this thought? What evidence contradicts it? If your friend had this thought, what would you tell them?"* |
| **Key technique** | **Disputation (D)** leading to **Effective new philosophy (E)** — replaces "musts" with preferences ("I'd like to succeed, but I don't *have* to"). | **Cognitive restructuring** — identifies NATs, names distortions, generates **balanced alternative thoughts** ("I wasn't selected *this time*; I can practice and try again"), and tests them via behavioural experiments. |

A wrong answer saying "Both just ask questions" misses the **philosophical vs. empirical** distinction: Ellis *teaches* rational philosophy; Beck *collaborates* on evidence-testing.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
**Evaluate:** "Cognitive therapy is just 'positive thinking' — telling depressed clients to think happy thoughts." Defend or refute this statement using **two** specific differences between cognitive restructuring and positive thinking.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
**Refute.** Cognitive restructuring is **not** positive thinking. Two critical differences:  
1. **Evidence-based, not affirmation-based:** Positive thinking repeats "I am confident" regardless of evidence. Cognitive restructuring asks: *"What is the evidence for 'I'm a failure'? What is the evidence against it? What is a *balanced* thought that fits *all* the evidence?"* (e.g., "I failed this test; I've passed others; I can improve.")  
2. **Targets *distortions*, not valence:** Positive thinking suppresses negative thoughts. Cognitive restructuring *identifies the specific thinking error* (e.g., "All-or-nothing thinking: 'One failure = total failure'") and corrects the *logic*, not the mood. The goal is **accurate thinking**, not "happy" thinking — accurate thoughts naturally reduce distress.  

A wrong answer agreeing ("Yes, it makes you think positively") reveals the student conflates *outcome* (reduced negativity) with *process* (logical restructuring). Board exams often test this distinction in assertion-reason questions.

**Worksheet for: "IIT or Shame" Belief**  

| Component | Content |

| **B (Irrational Belief)** | "I *must* get into IIT. If I don't, my parents *will* be ashamed of me (and that's unbearable), and I *will* have no future (catastrophe)." |

A poor answer writes D as "Think positive" or E as "I will get into IIT next year" (still a demand). The student must show **D attacks the irrationality** (demandingness, awfulising) and **E is a flexible, evidence-based preference**, not a new rigid demand.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
**Misconception:** "Ellis and Beck are the same — both just challenge negative thoughts."  
**Why it's wrong:** Students conflate *cognitive therapy* as a monolith. **Ellis (RET)** is *philosophical-directive*: the therapist *knows* what's rational, actively disputes "musts," and teaches a rational philosophy of life (preferences over demands). **Beck (CT)** is *collaborative-empirical*: the therapist *does not know* the truth; client and therapist *jointly investigate* NATs as hypotheses using Socratic questioning and behavioural experiments. Ellis targets *core irrational beliefs* (global "musts"); Beck targets *situation-specific NATs* linked to deeper schemas. In Board exams, a question asking "Which therapy uses Socratic questioning?" — answer: **Beck**. "Which uses direct disputation of 'musts'?" — answer: **Ellis**. Mixing them loses marks.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A 17-year-old student, Priya, receives a 78% on her Chemistry pre-board exam. She thinks: "I'm stupid. I'll never get into a good college. My parents will hate me." She feels intense shame and stops studying for other subjects. Using Ellis' ABC model, analyse this case and formulate two disputing questions a therapist would ask.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the Activating Event (A) — the objective situation that occurred. Here: "Received 78% on Chemistry pre-board."
- Step 2: Identify the Belief (B) — the irrational interpretation containing demands/awfulising. Here: "I *must* score 90%+. Scoring 78% means I'm *stupid* (global rating), I'll *never* get into a good college (fortune-telling/awfulising), and my parents *will* hate me (mind-reading/catastrophe)."
- Step 3: Identify the Consequence (C) — emotional and behavioural outcome caused by B. Here: "Intense shame (emotion), stopping study for other subjects (behaviour — avoidance)."
- Step 4: Formulate Disputing Questions (D) — target the irrationality types. Question 1 (Empirical/Logical): "What is the evidence that 78% = 'stupid'? Many intelligent people score 78% on one exam." Question 2 (Pragmatic/Functional): "How does believing 'my parents will hate me' help you prepare for your next exam? Does it motivate or paralyse you?"
- Step 5: Verify the analysis — check that B contains "must/should" demands and awfulising, and that C follows from B, not A directly.

REASONING: The ABC model requires separating the *event* (A) from the *interpretation* (B). Priya's distress comes not from 78% but from her demands ("must score 90%") and catastrophes ("never get into college," "parents hate me"). Disputation questions must be specific to the *type* of irrationality: empirical questions challenge evidence; pragmatic questions challenge utility.

ANSWER: A = 78% on Chemistry pre-board. B = "I must score 90%+; 78% proves I'm stupid; I'll never get into a good college; my parents will hate me." C = Intense shame; avoidance of studying. D1 = "What evidence links 78% to 'stupid'?" D2 = "How does 'parents will hate me' help you study?"

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Rohan, 16, is excluded from a WhatsApp group by classmates. His negative automatic thought (NAT) is: "Nobody likes me. I'm totally unlikable." Identify the cognitive distortion(s), the likely core schema, and write a balanced alternative thought using Beck's cognitive restructuring.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the NAT — the immediate, involuntary thought. Here: "Nobody likes me. I'm totally unlikable."
- Step 2: Name the cognitive distortion(s) — the systematic thinking error. "Nobody likes me" = **Overgeneralisation** (one event → universal conclusion). "I'm totally unlikable" = **Labelling** (global negative label) + **All-or-Nothing Thinking** (totally unlikable vs. totally likable, no middle ground).
- Step 3: Infer the core schema — the deep, enduring belief activated. Likely: "I am unlovable / defective / socially inadequate" (formed from earlier experiences like bullying or parental criticism).
- Step 4: Generate a balanced alternative thought — evidence-based, nuanced, not "positive." Evidence FOR: "These classmates excluded me." Evidence AGAINST: "I have other friends who include me; exclusion happens for many reasons (group size, topic, mistake)." Balanced thought: "Being left out of *this* group hurts, but it doesn't mean *nobody* likes me or that I'm *totally* unlikable. I have friends who value me. I can ask what happened or focus on other connections."
- Step 5: Check the alternative — it acknowledges pain (validates), uses specific language (not global), and fits all evidence.

REASONING: Beck's restructuring doesn't replace negative with positive; it replaces *distorted* with *accurate*. The balanced thought must: (a) accept the grain of truth ("exclusion hurts"), (b) correct the distortion ("nobody" → "these classmates"; "totally unlikable" → "some people like me"), and (c) be believable to the client.

ANSWER: Distortions = Overgeneralisation, Labelling, All-or-Nothing Thinking. Core Schema = "I am unlovable/defective." Balanced Thought = "Being left out of this group hurts, but it doesn't mean nobody likes me or I'm totally unlikable. I have friends who value me."

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Integrate Ellis and Beck: A client believes "I must be perfect in everything I do, or I'm a complete failure." (Ellis: irrational demand + global rating. Beck: NAT "I'm a complete failure" from core schema "I'm inadequate" triggered by a minor mistake.) Design a **combined intervention plan** with 3 sessions: Session 1 (Ellis-style disputation), Session 2 (Beck-style thought record), Session 3 (Behavioural experiment).

STEPS:
- Step 1: Session 1 — Ellis Disputation. Target the *demand* ("must be perfect") and *global rating* ("complete failure"). Therapist asks: (a) "Where is the rule written that you *must* be perfect? Who enforces it?" (b) "If a friend made one mistake, would you call them a 'complete failure'? Why the double standard?" (c) "How does demanding perfection help you perform? Does it reduce or increase mistakes?" Goal: Client adopts Effective New Philosophy: "I *prefer* to do well, but perfection is impossible. Mistakes are human, not proof of failure."
- Step 2: Session 2 — Beck Thought Record (7-column). Situation: "Made a calculation error in Math test." NAT: "I'm a complete failure." Distortions: All-or-Nothing, Labelling, Overgeneralisation. Evidence For: "I made an error." Evidence Against: "I solved 18/20 correctly; I've passed every Math test this year." Balanced Thought: "I made one error on one test. That's disappointing, but my overall performance is strong. One mistake ≠ complete failure." Re-rate belief in NAT: 90% → 30%.
- Step 3: Session 3 — Behavioural Experiment. Hypothesis: "If I deliberately make a small, harmless mistake (e.g., wear mismatched socks), I will not become a 'complete failure' and people won't reject me." Prediction (if NAT true): "Everyone will notice, judge me, I'll feel like a failure." Prediction (if Balanced Thought true): "Few will notice; those who do won't care; I'll feel slightly awkward but okay." Action: Wear mismatched socks for half a day. Outcome recording: "3 people noticed, smiled, said nothing. I felt 20% anxious, not devastated. NAT belief now 10%."
- Step 4: Integration review — Ellis weakened the *philosophical demand*; Beck tested the *specific prediction* with evidence. Both needed for deep change.

REASONING: Ellis attacks the *core irrational philosophy* (demandingness); Beck tests *situation-specific predictions* derived from schemas. Combining them: Disputation (Ellis) creates cognitive flexibility; Thought Records (Beck) build evidence against NATs; Behavioural Experiments (Beck) test new beliefs in reality. This mirrors CBT integration (next node).

ANSWER: Session 1: Disputation of "must be perfect" → New philosophy: "I prefer excellence; mistakes are human." Session 2: Thought Record on Math error → Balanced thought: "One error ≠ failure." Session 3: Mismatched socks experiment → Outcome disconfirms "complete failure" prediction.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: **Assertion-Reason Question (Board + Competitive Pattern)**  
**Assertion (A):** In Beck's Cognitive Therapy, the therapist directly tells the client their negative automatic thoughts are wrong and provides positive replacements.  
**Reason (R):** Beck's therapy assumes the therapist is an expert who knows the correct way to think, similar to Ellis' RET.  

Choose the correct option:  
(1) Both A and R are true, and R correctly explains A.  
(2) Both A and R are true, but R does not explain A.  
(3) A is true but R is false.  
(4) A is false but R is true.  
(5) Both A and R are false.

COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying theoretical distinctions (Ellis vs. Beck) — error identification and correction.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Evaluate Assertion (A) — Does Beck's therapist *directly tell* clients thoughts are wrong and *provide* replacements? No. Beck uses **collaborative empiricism / Socratic questioning** — client discovers distortions and generates alternatives.
- Step 2: Evaluate Reason (R) — Does Beck assume therapist is an expert who knows correct thinking? No. Beck views therapist as **co-investigator**; beliefs are hypotheses to test. Ellis is more directive/expert.
- Step 3: Determine relationship — Both A and R are false. Beck is *neither* directive (A false) *nor* expert-led (R false).
- Step 4: Select option (5).

ANSWER: Option (5) — Both A and R are false.  
**Explanation:** Beck's Cognitive Therapy uses **guided discovery** (Socratic questioning), not direct correction. The therapist asks: *"What evidence supports this thought? What contradicts it?"* — the client generates the balanced thought. The therapist is a **collaborative empiricist**, not an authority. Ellis (RET) is more directive, but even Ellis uses *disputation* (questions), not simply "telling." This distinction is frequently tested in Board assertion-reason and NEET PG psychology questions.

DIFFICULTY: hard
