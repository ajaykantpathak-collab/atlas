## DEFINITION
Financial Planning is the process of preparing a financial blueprint of an organisation's future operations by estimating fund requirements (both quantum and timing) and specifying sources of funds to ensure adequate funds are available when needed while avoiding idle resources.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to: (1) explain the twin objectives of financial planning with a concrete business example, and (2) construct a simple 3-year financial plan outline showing sales forecast, fund requirements for fixed/working capital, internal accruals estimation, and external funding sources identification.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers: meaning of financial planning, its twin objectives (availability of funds & avoiding excess funds), importance (7 points from NCERT), and the step-by-step process (sales forecast → projected financial statements → fund requirement estimation → internal/external sources identification → cash budgets). It does NOT cover capital budgeting techniques (NPV/IRR), detailed capital structure theories (MM hypothesis), or working capital management techniques (cash budgeting models) — those are separate nodes.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you're the CFO of a startup planning to launch a new electric scooter plant. You have ₹50 crore in hand but need to know: will this last 3 years? When will you need more? Where will it come from? Start by asking: "What will we sell?" — that's the sales forecast. Then: "What assets do we need to make those sales?" — that's fixed capital (machinery) and working capital (inventory, receivables). Next: "How much profit will we keep after dividends?" — that's internal accruals. The gap is what you must raise externally. This sequence — forecast → asset needs → profit retention → funding gap → source identification — IS financial planning. The key insight: financial planning doesn't decide WHICH project to pick (that's capital budgeting) or WHICH source is cheapest (that's financing decision); it ensures the MONEY ARRIVES WHEN THE PROJECT NEEDS IT.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
State the two twin objectives of financial planning as per NCERT.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The two twin objectives are: (a) To ensure availability of funds whenever required — estimating quantum and timing for long-term assets and day-to-day expenses, and specifying sources; (b) To see that the firm does not raise resources unnecessarily — avoiding idle funds that add to cost and encourage wasteful expenditure.  
A wrong answer like "maximising profit" or "minimising cost" reveals the student is confusing financial planning's operational objective (smooth fund flow) with financial management's strategic objective (wealth maximisation). Financial planning is a SUPPORT function, not the decision-making function itself.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
"Financial planning is a substitute for financial management." — True or False? Justify your answer in two sentences using NCERT terminology.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
False. Financial management aims at choosing the best investment and financing alternatives by focusing on their costs and benefits to increase shareholders' wealth. Financial planning aims at smooth operations by focusing on fund requirements and their availability in the light of financial decisions. They are complementary: financial decisions (e.g., capital budgeting) trigger the need for financial planning to forecast the resultant fund flows.  
A student answering "True" reveals they don't distinguish between the DECISION function (financial management) and the FORECASTING/COORDINATION function (financial planning). This is a classic board exam trap — the NCERT explicitly states "financial planning is not equivalent to, or a substitute for, financial management."

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A company forecasts 20% sales growth next year. Its current fixed assets are ₹100 crore, current assets ₹40 crore, and current liabilities ₹15 crore. The fixed assets to sales ratio is 0.5, and current assets to sales ratio is 0.2. Current liabilities to sales ratio is 0.075. If the company wants to maintain these ratios, calculate the additional funds required (AFN) for next year. Current sales = ₹200 crore.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Projected sales = ₹200 crore × 1.20 = ₹240 crore.  
Step 2: Required fixed assets = 0.5 × ₹240 crore = ₹120 crore → increase = ₹20 crore.  
Step 3: Required current assets = 0.2 × ₹240 crore = ₹48 crore → increase = ₹8 crore.  
Step 4: Spontaneous current liabilities = 0.075 × ₹240 crore = ₹18 crore → increase = ₹3 crore.  
Step 5: AFN = (ΔFixed Assets + ΔCurrent Assets) – ΔSpontaneous Liabilities = (₹20 + ₹8) – ₹3 = ₹25 crore.  
A wrong answer forgetting to subtract spontaneous liabilities (getting ₹28 crore) reveals the student doesn't understand that trade credit (creditors, outstanding expenses) grows automatically with sales — a key working capital planning concept from c12_bs_cbse_09_007.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the role of financial planning in a stable, mature FMCG company (e.g., HUL) vs. a high-growth tech startup (e.g., a new EV battery firm). Explain how the "forecasting under different business situations" importance point applies differently.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
For HUL: Sales growth is predictable (5-8%), so financial planning focuses on fine-tuning working capital cycles, optimising cash buffers, and coordinating large-scale production-distribution. Alternative scenarios (10% vs 30% growth) are narrow; planning reduces waste/duplication in a complex, stable machine.  
For the EV startup: Sales could be 0% or 300% — scenarios are WIDE. Financial planning's primary value is preparing multiple blueprints (conservative, base, aggressive) for fund requirements so the firm doesn't run out of cash during scaling or sit on expensive idle capital if growth stalls. The "linking present with future" and "avoiding business shocks" importance points are CRITICAL here.  
A student giving identical answers for both reveals they treat financial planning as a mechanical ratio exercise, not a strategic uncertainty-management tool tailored to business risk profile.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Critique this statement: "Since financial planning aims to avoid excess funds, a company should always raise funds only when a payment is due — just-in-time financing eliminates idle cash." Use the concept of floatation costs and financial risk.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is flawed. Just-in-time financing ignores: (1) Floatation costs — frequent small issuances (equity/debt) incur repeated SEBI fees, underwriting commissions, credit rating costs, which can exceed the interest on temporarily idle funds. (2) Financial risk — markets may be closed (bear phase, credit freeze) exactly when funds are needed; a company with no cash buffer faces liquidation risk. (3) Signalling effect — frequent equity issues signal desperation, depressing share price. NCERT lists "floatation costs" and "stock market conditions" as capital structure factors; financial planning must balance idle cash cost against these risks.  
A student agreeing reveals they view "idle funds" only through an accounting lens (interest cost) ignoring corporate finance realities (market access, signalling, transaction costs) — a common error in board case-study questions.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a 3-step financial planning checklist for a Class 12 student's family planning a ₹15 lakh home renovation over 18 months. The father gets a ₹50,000/month salary, mother ₹30,000/month, and they have ₹3 lakh savings. Include: (a) sales forecast equivalent, (b) fund requirement estimation, (c) source identification.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Step 1 (Sales Forecast Equivalent): Create a monthly "income forecast" — father's salary (₹50k), mother's salary (₹30k), any bonuses/increments expected. Total monthly inflow = ₹80k. Project for 18 months = ₹14.4 lakh.  
Step 2 (Fund Requirement): Renovation cost ₹15 lakh + 10% contingency = ₹16.5 lakh. Monthly living expenses (rent, food, EMIs) = ₹40k × 18 = ₹7.2 lakh. Total outflow = ₹23.7 lakh. Net deficit = ₹23.7L – ₹14.4L = ₹9.3 lakh.  
Step 3 (Sources): Internal = ₹3 lakh savings + ₹9.3 lakh from salary surplus? But salary surplus is only ₹40k/month × 18 = ₹7.2 lakh. Gap = ₹2.1 lakh. External sources: (i) Top-up home loan (lowest cost, tax benefit), (ii) Loan against FD (if any), (iii) Personal loan (last resort). Prioritise by cost and flexibility.  
A poor answer that just says "save more" or "take a loan" without quantifying the gap and sequencing sources by cost reveals the student cannot translate the corporate financial planning process to personal finance — missing the core competency of "linking present with future."

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students think financial planning = making a budget. They write: "Financial planning is preparing budgets for the year." This is wrong because: (1) Budgets are SHORT-TERM (≤1 year) detailed plans; financial planning covers 3-5 years and is broader. (2) Financial planning INCLUDES budgets but STARTS with sales forecast and strategic fund requirement estimation for fixed/working capital. (3) The NCERT explicitly says: "Plans made for periods of one year or less are termed as budgets. Budgets are example of financial planning exercise in greater details." Confusing the two loses 1-2 marks in board "distinguish" questions and leads to wrong answers in case studies where long-term capital expenditure planning is needed.

## ON_FAILURE
If the student equates financial planning with budgeting:  
Step 1: Ask them to read the NCERT lines: "Typically, financial planning is done for three to five years... Plans made for periods of one year or less are termed as budgets."  
Step 2: Give a concrete example: "A company deciding to build a ₹500 crore plant over 4 years — is that a budget? No, it's financial planning. The monthly cash flow schedule for year 1 of that plant is a budget."  
Step 3: Re-do Diagnostic Question 6 but for a 4-year college education fund — force them to separate the 4-year plan (financial planning) from the semester-wise fee payment schedule (budget).  
Step 4: Re-attempt Diagnostic Question 2 (True/False) and Question 4 (HUL vs startup) to verify the distinction is internalised.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: "GreenTech Pvt Ltd" plans to set up a solar panel manufacturing unit. Current sales ₹80 crore. They forecast 25% sales growth next year. Fixed assets to sales ratio = 0.6, Current assets to sales ratio = 0.25, Current liabilities to sales ratio = 0.1. Current fixed assets = ₹48 crore, current assets = ₹20 crore, current liabilities = ₹8 crore. They retain 40% of profits. Net profit margin = 10%. Calculate the Additional Funds Needed (AFN) for next year using the percentage-of-sales method.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate projected sales = Current sales × (1 + growth rate) = ₹80 crore × 1.25 = ₹100 crore.
- Step 2: Calculate required fixed assets = Projected sales × Fixed assets/sales ratio = ₹100 crore × 0.6 = ₹60 crore. Increase in fixed assets = ₹60 crore - ₹48 crore = ₹12 crore.
- Step 3: Calculate required current assets = Projected sales × Current assets/sales ratio = ₹100 crore × 0.25 = ₹25 crore. Increase in current assets = ₹25 crore - ₹20 crore = ₹5 crore.
- Step 4: Calculate spontaneous current liabilities = Projected sales × CL/sales ratio = ₹100 crore × 0.1 = ₹10 crore. Increase in spontaneous CL = ₹10 crore - ₹8 crore = ₹2 crore.
- Step 5: Calculate retained earnings = Projected sales × Net profit margin × Retention ratio = ₹100 crore × 0.10 × 0.40 = ₹4 crore.
- Step 6: AFN = (Increase in Fixed Assets + Increase in Current Assets) - (Increase in Spontaneous CL + Retained Earnings) = (₹12 + ₹5) - (₹2 + ₹4) = ₹17 - ₹6 = ₹11 crore.

REASONING: The percentage-of-sales method assumes assets and spontaneous liabilities grow proportionally with sales. Retained earnings are an internal source. AFN is the external funding gap after using all spontaneous and internal sources. This mirrors the NCERT process: sales forecast → asset needs → internal accruals → external funding identification.

ANSWER: ₹11 crore

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: "Apex Logistics" (a trading firm) has current sales ₹200 crore. They expect a recession next year with sales dropping 15%. Fixed assets/sales = 0.2 (trading needs less fixed assets), Current assets/sales = 0.3, Current liabilities/sales = 0.12. Current fixed assets = ₹40 crore, current assets = ₹60 crore, current liabilities = ₹24 crore. Net profit margin = 5%, retention ratio = 60%. The company wants to know if it will have surplus funds or need external financing. Calculate AFN (negative = surplus).

STEPS:
- Step 1: Projected sales = ₹200 crore × (1 - 0.15) = ₹170 crore.
- Step 2: Required fixed assets = ₹170 crore × 0.2 = ₹34 crore. Change = ₹34 - ₹40 = -₹6 crore (release of fixed assets? Unlikely for trading firm; assume fixed assets stay at ₹40 crore as they are not easily liquidated. But percentage method says ₹34 crore. We'll follow method: decrease of ₹6 crore).
- Step 3: Required current assets = ₹170 crore × 0.3 = ₹51 crore. Change = ₹51 - ₹60 = -₹9 crore (reduction in inventory/receivables).
- Step 4: Spontaneous current liabilities = ₹170 crore × 0.12 = ₹20.4 crore. Change = ₹20.4 - ₹24 = -₹3.6 crore (payables decrease).
- Step 5: Retained earnings = ₹170 crore × 0.05 × 0.60 = ₹5.1 crore.
- Step 6: AFN = (ΔFA + ΔCA) - (ΔSpontaneous CL + Retained Earnings) = (-₹6 + -₹9) - (-₹3.6 + ₹5.1) = (-₹15) - (₹1.5) = -₹16.5 crore.

REASONING: Negative AFN means surplus funds generated. In a downturn, trading firms release working capital (inventory, receivables shrink) and may even dispose of some fixed assets. The surplus ₹16.5 crore can be used to repay debt or invest in marketable securities. This illustrates NCERT's importance point: "forecasting what may happen under different business situations" — here a recession scenario.

ANSWER: -₹16.5 crore (surplus)

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: "MedLife Pharma" is planning a 3-year financial plan. Year 0 (base): Sales ₹500 crore, Fixed Assets ₹300 crore, Current Assets ₹150 crore, Current Liabilities ₹50 crore, Debt ₹200 crore (10% interest), Equity ₹200 crore. Tax rate 30%. They forecast sales growth: Year 1: 20%, Year 2: 15%, Year 3: 10%. Ratios constant: FA/Sales=0.6, CA/Sales=0.3, CL/Sales=0.1. Net profit margin 12%, retention 50%. Debt can be raised at 10%. They want to maintain Debt/Equity ≤ 1.5. Prepare a summary of external funding needs for each year and check if debt capacity is exceeded.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Year 1 Sales = ₹500 × 1.20 = ₹600 cr. Year 2 = ₹600 × 1.15 = ₹690 cr. Year 3 = ₹690 × 1.10 = ₹759 cr.
- Step 2: For each year, compute required FA, CA, CL, and changes from previous year.
  Year 1: FA req = 0.6×600=360 (Δ=+60), CA req=0.3×600=180 (Δ=+30), CL req=0.1×600=60 (Δ=+10). Retained earnings = 600×0.12×0.5=36. AFN = (60+30) - (10+36) = 90-46 = ₹44 cr.
  Year 2: FA req=0.6×690=414 (Δ=+54), CA req=0.3×690=207 (Δ=+27), CL req=0.1×690=69 (Δ=+9). Retained earnings=690×0.12×0.5=41.4. AFN = (54+27)-(9+41.4)=81-50.4=₹30.6 cr.
  Year 3: FA req=0.6×759=455.4 (Δ=+41.4), CA req=0.3×759=227.7 (Δ=+20.7), CL req=0.1×759=75.9 (Δ=+6.9). Retained earnings=759×0.12×0.5=45.54. AFN = (41.4+20.7)-(6.9+45.54)=62.1-52.44=₹9.66 cr.
- Step 3: Cumulative external funding needed = 44 + 30.6 + 9.66 = ₹84.26 cr. Assume raised as debt (cheaper). New Debt = 200 + 84.26 = ₹284.26 cr. Equity increases by retained earnings: 36+41.4+45.54=₹122.94 cr. New Equity = 200+122.94=₹322.94 cr.
- Step 4: Debt/Equity = 284.26 / 322.94 = 0.88 ≤ 1.5. Constraint satisfied.
- Step 5: Check interest coverage: Year 3 EBIT ≈ Sales × (1 - operating cost ratio). Not given, but assume EBIT margin 20% → EBIT=151.8 cr. Interest = 284.26×0.10=28.43 cr. ICR=5.3 > 3 (safe).

REASONING: Multi-year planning requires rolling forward the balance sheet. Each year's AFN becomes next year's debt/equity base. The debt-equity constraint (from c12_bs_cbse_09_008) must be checked at the end. This integrates financial planning (node 006) with capital structure (node 008) and shows the "link between investment and financing decisions on a continuous basis" (NCERT importance point vi).

ANSWER: Year 1 AFN: ₹44 cr, Year 2: ₹30.6 cr, Year 3: ₹9.66 cr. Total external funding ₹84.26 cr. Final D/E = 0.88 (within limit). ICR Year 3 ≈ 5.3 (safe).

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A case study: "Solaris Energy" (from the NCERT 'S Limited' steel plant example style) is setting up a ₹2,000 crore solar cell plant. It has ₹500 crore internal accruals. The CFO proposes two financial plans:
Plan A: Raise ₹1,500 crore debt at 9% (current D/E = 0.5, industry avg 1.0).
Plan B: Raise ₹750 crore debt at 9% + ₹750 crore equity (issue price ₹200, face ₹10).
Tax rate 30%. Project RoI = 14%. Current EBIT = ₹300 crore on ₹1,000 cr capital.
Which plan better serves the TWIN OBJECTIVES of financial planning? Analyse using: (i) fund availability timing, (ii) cost of idle funds, (iii) financial risk (ICR), (iv) EPS impact (trading on equity), (v) control dilution. Recommend one.

COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying using multi-criteria financial analysis

STEPS:
- Step 1: Check twin objectives — both plans ensure fund availability (₹2,000 cr raised). But Plan A raises more debt than needed? No, ₹1,500 cr debt + ₹500 cr internal = ₹2,000 cr. Plan B: ₹750+₹750+₹500 = ₹2,000 cr. Both meet quantum.
- Step 2: Idle funds cost — if plant commissioning is phased (₹500 cr/year for 4 years), raising all ₹1,500 cr debt upfront (Plan A) creates idle cash earning ~4% (FD) while paying 9% → negative carry 5% on idle portion. Plan B's equity has no fixed cost, but floatation costs ~5% of issue size = ₹37.5 cr one-time. Debt floatation ~1% = ₹7.5 cr (Plan A) vs ₹7.5 cr (Plan B). Plan A has higher ongoing negative carry.
- Step 3: Financial risk (ICR): Plan A: New Debt=1500, Total Debt=2000 (assume old debt 500). Interest=2000×9%=180 cr. New EBIT=300 + (2000×14%)=580 cr. ICR=580/180=3.22. Plan B: New Debt=750, Total Debt=1250. Interest=112.5 cr. ICR=580/112.5=5.16. Plan B safer.
- Step 4: EPS/Trading on Equity: RoI (14%) > Cost of Debt (9% post-tax 6.3%). Plan A higher leverage → higher EPS if RoI holds. But risk of RoI shortfall (tech, policy) makes Plan A dangerous.
- Step 5: Control: Plan B issues 3.75 cr shares (750/200). If promoter holds 60% of 10 cr shares (6 cr), new holding = 6/13.75 = 43.6% — dilution risk. Plan A no dilution.
- Step 6: Recommendation: Plan B for a NEW TECH plant (high uncertainty, long gestation). Lower financial risk, no negative carry on phased capex. Accept dilution. If plant was brownfield expansion with certain cash flows, Plan A.

ANSWER: Plan B recommended for greenfield solar plant due to lower financial risk (ICR 5.16 vs 3.22), no negative carry on phased investment, and manageable dilution. Plan A only if cash flows are certain and immediate.
