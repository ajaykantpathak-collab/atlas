## DEFINITION
Lipids are a heterogeneous group of organic compounds insoluble in water but soluble in non-polar solvents, primarily composed of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen, classified into simple lipids (triglycerides, waxes), compound lipids (phospholipids, glycolipids), and derived lipids (steroids, fat-soluble vitamins), with fatty acids as their fundamental building blocks determining physical properties and physiological functions.

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to: (1) classify any given lipid into simple, compound, or derived categories with structural justification, and (2) evaluate a dietary scenario for essential fatty acid adequacy and health risk by calculating omega-6:omega-3 ratio and identifying trans-fat sources.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers lipid classification (simple/compound/derived), fatty acid types (SFA/MUFA/PUFA), essential fatty acids (linoleic, alpha-linolenic), their metabolic roles, deficiency symptoms, health implications (CVD, inflammation, brain function), and dietary sources — excluding detailed eicosanoid synthesis pathways, lipoprotein metabolism kinetics, and pharmacological lipid-lowering agents which belong to higher biochemistry/medical nutrition therapy.

## WALKTHROUGH
Start by asking: "Why does ghee solidify in winter but mustard oil stays liquid — and why does your nutritionist insist on both omega-3 and omega-6?" This hooks the student to the real-world puzzle of lipid physical properties and essentiality. Begin with the Class 11 biomolecules foundation: fatty acid structure (chain length, saturation, cis/trans). Build classification stepwise: glycerol backbone → simple lipids (triglycerides for energy storage); phosphate/sugar head groups → compound lipids (membrane architecture); cyclopentanoperhydrophenanthrene ring → derived lipids (signaling). Introduce essential fatty acids as "the two the body cannot make" — linoleic (omega-6) and alpha-linolenic (omega-3) — and trace their conversion to arachidonic acid, EPA, DHA. End with the key insight: the omega-6:omega-3 ratio in modern Indian diets (often 20:1 to 50:1 vs. recommended 5:1 to 10:1) drives inflammatory disease risk, making lipid quality — not just quantity — the clinical nutrition lever.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Define essential fatty acids and name the two parent essential fatty acids with their omega designations.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Essential fatty acids (EFAs) are polyunsaturated fatty acids that humans cannot synthesize de novo due to lack of Δ-12 and Δ-15 desaturases and must obtain from diet. The two parent EFAs are linoleic acid (LA, 18:2 n-6, omega-6) and alpha-linolenic acid (ALA, 18:3 n-3, omega-3). A wrong answer (e.g., naming oleic acid or arachidonic acid as essential) reveals confusion between "essential" (dietary requirement) and "important" (physiological role) — a classic board exam trap where students memorize lists without understanding the enzymatic basis of essentiality.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why triglycerides are classified as simple lipids while phospholipids are compound lipids, using structural criteria.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Simple lipids yield only fatty acids and alcohol (glycerol) on hydrolysis; triglycerides (triacylglycerols) fit this — three fatty acids esterified to glycerol. Compound lipids yield fatty acids, alcohol, and an additional non-lipid component (phosphate, nitrogenous base, carbohydrate) on hydrolysis; phospholipids contain a phosphoric acid group (often with choline/ethanolamine) making them phosphoglycerides. A wrong answer focusing only on "complexity" or "function" (e.g., "phospholipids are in membranes so they're compound") misses the definitional hydrolysis criterion used in CBSE marking schemes.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A 35-year-old male consumes 30 g visible fat/day (15 g refined soybean oil, 10 g vanaspati, 5 g ghee). Calculate his approximate omega-6:omega-3 ratio and evaluate against ICMR recommendations. (Assume: soybean oil = 50% LA, 7% ALA; vanaspati = 20% LA, 1% ALA, 30% trans; ghee = 3% LA, 1% ALA).

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Calculate LA intake = (15×0.50) + (10×0.20) + (5×0.03) = 7.5 + 2.0 + 0.15 = 9.65 g. Step 2: Calculate ALA intake = (15×0.07) + (10×0.01) + (5×0.01) = 1.05 + 0.10 + 0.05 = 1.20 g. Step 3: Ratio = 9.65 / 1.20 ≈ 8.0:1. Step 4: ICMR recommends 5:1 to 10:1 — this ratio is within range BUT vanaspati contributes 30% trans fats (3 g/day), exceeding WHO limit (<1% energy = ~2.2 g/day for 2000 kcal). Step 5: Evaluation — ratio acceptable but trans-fat risk high; replace vanaspati with mustard oil or canola oil. A wrong answer ignoring trans fats or miscalculating ratio (e.g., using total fat not fatty acids) shows inability to apply compositional data to dietary assessment — a core competency for CUET case-based questions.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the metabolic fates of linoleic acid (omega-6) and alpha-linolenic acid (omega-3) in humans, and explain why their ratio influences inflammatory status.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Both undergo Δ-6 desaturation (rate-limiting) → elongation → Δ-5 desaturation. LA → γ-linolenic acid (GLA) → dihomo-γ-linolenic acid (DGLA) → arachidonic acid (AA, 20:4 n-6). ALA → stearidonic acid → eicosatetraenoic acid → eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA, 20:5 n-3) → docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, 22:6 n-3). AA yields pro-inflammatory eicosanoids (PGE₂, LTB₄, TXA₂); EPA/DHA yield less inflammatory/resolving eicosanoids (PGE₃, LTB₅, resolvins). High omega-6:omega-3 shifts substrate competition toward AA pathway, promoting thrombosis, vasoconstriction, inflammation. A wrong answer stating "omega-6 is bad, omega-3 is good" oversimplifies — both are essential; the error is missing competitive enzyme kinetics and eicosanoid balance, which is the mechanistic basis for dietary guidelines.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Critique the statement: "Coconut oil is unhealthy because it is 90% saturated fat; replace it entirely with sunflower oil for heart health." Use lipid biochemistry and Indian dietary context.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is reductionist. Coconut oil's saturated fats are predominantly medium-chain (lauric 48%, myristic 16%, caprylic 7%, capric 6%) — absorbed directly to portal vein, less prone to adipose storage, raise HDL more than LDL. Sunflower oil is high-LA (omega-6, ~65%), negligible ALA — exclusive use worsens omega-6:omega-3 ratio, promotes oxidative stress (LA peroxidation). Indian diets already omega-6 heavy (soybean, sunflower, safflower oils). Better strategy: rotate oils (coconut for high-heat, mustard/canola for omega-3, groundnut/rice bran for MUFA), limit total visible fat to 25-30 g/day. A wrong answer endorsing the statement shows "saturated = bad, PUFA = good" binary thinking — ignoring fatty acid chain length, metabolic handling, and dietary pattern context tested in CBSE case-study questions.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a one-day lipid-modified menu for a 60-year-old post-MI patient with dyslipidemia (LDL 160 mg/dL, TG 220 mg/dL, HDL 35 mg/dL) using ICMR 2020 guidelines, specifying oil choices, visible fat quantity, and omega-3 sources.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Menu principles: Total fat 20-25% energy (~45-55 g/day for 1800 kcal), visible fat 20 g/day. SFA <7% energy, MUFA 10-15%, PUFA 8-10% (omega-6:omega-3 5:1). Oils: 10 g rice bran oil (MUFA+PUFA, oryzanol), 5 g mustard oil (ALA 12%), 5 g flaxseed oil (ALA 55%, cold use only). Visible fat = 20 g. Omega-3 sources: 30 g roasted flaxseed powder (5 g ALA), 100 g fatty fish (rohu/mackerel, 1.5 g EPA+DHA) 2×/week, walnuts 15 g/day. Sample: Breakfast — oats porridge with 5 g flaxseed powder, 100 mL skim milk; Lunch — brown rice, dal, mustard-oil sautéed greens, fish curry (rice bran oil); Snack — walnuts 15 g; Dinner — multigrain roti, rajma, salad with 5 g flaxseed oil dressing. A poor answer giving generic "low fat" without quantitative oil allocation, omega-3 specification, or cooking method shows inability to translate guidelines into personalized prescription — the core skill of clinical nutrition.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students believe "all saturated fats raise LDL equally" and "all polyunsaturated fats are heart-healthy." They write in exams: "Saturated fat from ghee and coconut oil clogs arteries; sunflower oil is best because it's polyunsaturated." This is wrong because: (1) SFA chain length matters — lauric acid (C12) raises HDL more than LDL; stearic acid (C18) is neutral; only palmitic (C16) and myristic (C14) consistently raise LDL. (2) Omega-6 PUFA (linoleic acid) in excess promotes oxidation and inflammation when omega-3 is low — sunflower/safflower oil exclusive use worsens omega-6:omega-3 ratio. (3) Trans fats (vanaspati, reused frying oil) are more atherogenic per gram than any SFA. This misconception persists because textbooks oversimplify "SFA ↑ cholesterol, PUFA ↓ cholesterol" without nuances of fatty acid-specific effects and dietary pattern — a trap in both board "justify" questions and CUET assertion-reason items.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A 25-year-old female athlete (60 kg, 2500 kcal/day) uses 25 g visible fat daily: 10 g groundnut oil, 10 g sunflower oil, 5 g ghee. Calculate her omega-6:omega-3 ratio and determine if she meets ICMR EFA recommendations (LA 3% energy, ALA 0.5% energy). Fatty acid composition: groundnut oil (LA 32%, ALA 0.5%), sunflower oil (LA 65%, ALA 0.5%), ghee (LA 3%, ALA 1%).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate total energy from fat = 25 g × 9 kcal/g = 225 kcal. LA requirement = 3% of 2500 kcal = 75 kcal = 8.33 g. ALA requirement = 0.5% of 2500 kcal = 12.5 kcal = 1.39 g.
- Step 2: Calculate LA intake = (10×0.32) + (10×0.65) + (5×0.03) = 3.2 + 6.5 + 0.15 = 9.85 g. Calculate ALA intake = (10×0.005) + (10×0.005) + (5×0.01) = 0.05 + 0.05 + 0.05 = 0.15 g.
- Step 3: Compare to requirements: LA 9.85 g > 8.33 g (adequate). ALA 0.15 g < 1.39 g (severely deficient — only 11% of requirement).
- Step 4: Omega-6:omega-3 ratio = 9.85 / 0.15 ≈ 65.7:1 (far above recommended 5:1-10:1).
- Step 5: Interpretation: Despite adequate total fat and LA, ALA deficiency and extreme ratio indicate high inflammatory risk. Recommendation: Replace 5 g sunflower oil with 5 g canola oil (ALA 11%) or add 5 g flaxseed oil (ALA 55%) cold.
REASONING: This example demonstrates that meeting total fat and LA requirements does not guarantee EFA adequacy — ALA is the limiting essential fatty acid in typical Indian diets high in omega-6 oils. The calculation uses energy-percentage-based ICMR RDAs (not absolute grams) because requirements scale with total energy intake, a nuance often missed in board answers.
ANSWER: LA intake = 9.85 g (adequate), ALA intake = 0.15 g (deficient), Omega-6:Omega-3 ratio = 65.7:1 (pro-inflammatory).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A food label lists "partially hydrogenated vegetable oil" as ingredient. The nutrition facts show 0 g trans fat per 10 g serving. Explain the regulatory loophole and calculate maximum possible trans fat intake if a person consumes 4 servings/day. (Indian FSSAI regulation: <0.2 g trans fat per serving can be labeled "0 g").

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the loophole — FSSAI allows rounding down: if trans fat <0.2 g per serving, label can declare "0 g trans fat" or "trans fat free".
- Step 2: Maximum trans fat per serving = 0.19 g (just below 0.2 g threshold).
- Step 3: For 4 servings/day = 4 × 0.19 g = 0.76 g trans fat/day.
- Step 4: Compare to WHO limit: <1% of total energy. For 2000 kcal diet, 1% = 20 kcal = 2.22 g/day. 0.76 g is 34% of WHO limit — significant but not exceeding.
- Step 5: However, "partially hydrogenated" implies industrial trans fats (elaidic acid) which have no safe intake level per WHO 2023 guidance. Even 0.76 g/day from multiple processed foods accumulates.
- Step 6: Practical advice: Check ingredient list for "partially hydrogenated" or "shortening" — avoid regardless of nutrition label.
REASONING: This worked example connects lipid chemistry (hydrogenation converts cis to trans double bonds) to food regulation (labeling thresholds) and public health (cumulative exposure). Students often miss that "0 g" on label ≠ zero trans fat — a critical consumer literacy skill tested in CUET case-based questions.
ANSWER: Maximum trans fat = 0.76 g/day from 4 servings; regulatory loophole allows <0.2 g/serving to be labeled "0 g"; avoid products with "partially hydrogenated oil" ingredient.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Design a therapeutic lipid prescription for a 45-year-old male with familial hypercholesterolemia (LDL 220 mg/dL), triglycerides 180 mg/dL, BMI 28 kg/m², on statin therapy. Total energy 1800 kcal. Specify: (a) total visible fat (g), (b) SFA/MUFA/PUFA distribution (g), (c) cholesterol limit (mg), (d) omega-3 dose (g EPA+DHA), (e) plant sterol dose (g), with oil selection and meal distribution.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
STEPS:
- Step 1: Total fat 25% energy = 450 kcal = 50 g total fat. Visible fat = 25 g (50% of total fat from invisible sources). SFA <5% energy = <10 g; MUFA 12-15% = 24-30 g; PUFA 8-10% = 16-20 g (omega-6:omega-3 ≤5:1).
- Step 2: Cholesterol limit: <200 mg/day (therapeutic for FH). Eliminate organ meats, egg yolk limit 2/week, shellfish restrict.
- Step 3: Omega-3: 2-4 g EPA+DHA/day for TG lowering (per AHA/ACC). Since dietary fish provides ~1 g/100 g fatty fish, prescribe 2 g EPA+DHA supplement + 100 g fish 3×/week.
- Step 4: Plant sterols: 2 g/day (proven 10-15% LDL reduction) — use fortified spread/yogurt.
- Step 5: Oil selection (25 g visible fat): 10 g rice bran oil (MUFA 38%, PUFA 37%, oryzanol), 8 g canola oil (MUFA 62%, ALA 11%, omega-6:3=2:1), 7 g olive oil (MUFA 73%). Total SFA = (10×0.20)+(8×0.07)+(7×0.14) = 2.0+0.56+0.98 = 3.54 g (<10 g ✓). MUFA = (10×0.38)+(8×0.62)+(7×0.73) = 3.8+4.96+5.11 = 13.87 g (✓). PUFA = (10×0.37)+(8×0.19)+(7×0.11) = 3.7+1.52+0.77 = 5.99 g — wait, too low. Adjust: increase canola to 12 g, reduce rice bran to 6 g, olive 7 g. Recalculate: PUFA = (6×0.37)+(12×0.19)+(7×0.11) = 2.22+2.28+0.77 = 5.27 g — still low. Add 5 g flaxseed oil (ALA 55% = 2.75 g ALA) cold. Total PUFA = 8.02 g. Omega-6 = rice bran LA (6×0.33=1.98) + canola LA (12×0.19=2.28) = 4.26 g. Omega-3 = canola ALA (12×0.11=1.32) + flaxseed ALA (2.75) = 4.07 g. Ratio = 1.05:1 (excellent).
- Step 6: Meal distribution: Breakfast — 5 g canola oil in cooking + 2 g plant sterol spread; Lunch — 6 g rice bran oil + 5 g flaxseed oil in salad; Snack — walnuts 15 g (2.5 g ALA); Dinner — 7 g olive oil + 7 g canola oil in cooking; Bedtime — EPA/DHA 2 g supplement.
REASONING: This multi-constraint optimization mirrors clinical dietetics practice — balancing NCEP ATP III guidelines, ICMR RDAs, drug-nutrient interaction (statin + plant sterol additive effect), and Indian oil availability. The iterative oil adjustment shows how dietitians use fatty acid composition tables to hit multiple targets simultaneously.
ANSWER: Visible fat 25 g (rice bran 6 g, canola 19 g, olive 7 g, flaxseed 5 g cold); SFA 3.5 g, MUFA 28 g, PUFA 8 g (omega-6:3 ≈1:1); Cholesterol <200 mg; EPA+DHA 2 g supplement + dietary; Plant sterols 2 g fortified spread.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A mother asks you: "My 10-year-old son has been diagnosed with ADHD. I read that omega-3 supplements help. Should I give him fish oil capsules or just add flaxseed powder to his rotis? He is vegetarian." Analyze the biochemical basis for omega-3 in neurodevelopment, compare ALA vs EPA/DHA efficacy, and give a practical vegetarian recommendation with dosage.

COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to biochemical reasoning and evidence-based dietary prescription

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the core issue — ADHD neurodevelopment link to DHA (structural component of neuronal membranes, 40% brain PUFA).
- Step 2: Explain conversion inefficiency — ALA → EPA (5-10%) → DHA (<1%) in humans; vegetarians have lower DHA status.
- Step 3: Evaluate evidence — meta-analyses show modest benefit of EPA+DHA (≥500 mg/day) on ADHD symptoms; ALA alone does not reliably raise DHA.
- Step 4: Vegetarian options — algal DHA (vegan, 200-300 mg/capsule), flaxseed/chia/walnuts for ALA (but insufficient for DHA), fortified foods.
- Step 5: Prescription — Algal DHA 250 mg/day + 10 g roasted flaxseed powder daily (1.5 g ALA) + walnuts 10 g/day. Monitor 3 months.
ANSWER: Fish oil not vegetarian. ALA from flaxseed has <1% conversion to DHA — insufficient for therapeutic neurodevelopmental needs. Recommend algal DHA supplement (250 mg/day) plus dietary ALA sources (flaxseed 10 g, walnuts 10 g daily) to support endogenous conversion. Explain to mother that DHA is structural brain lipid; ALA is precursor but conversion is rate-limited by Δ-6 desaturase competition with high omega-6 intake.
DIFFICULTY: medium

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A hospital kitchen uses 50 kg vanaspati/month for deep-frying (reused 5 times). The dietitian proposes switching to high-oleic sunflower oil (HOSO, 82% oleic acid, 8% LA, 0.5% ALA). Calculate annual trans fat reduction (assume vanaspati 30% trans, 5% trans generated per reuse cycle) and evaluate impact on patient meal omega-6:omega-3 ratio. Current patient meal uses 15 g vanaspati/day; proposed uses 15 g HOSO/day.

COMPETENCY: Quantitative risk assessment and lipid quality optimization in institutional food service

STEPS:
- Step 1: Current trans fat from vanaspati = 50 kg/month × 0.30 = 15 kg trans fat/month initially. Each reuse adds 5% of remaining oil as trans: Cycle 2: 50×0.95×0.05=2.375 kg; Cycle 3: 50×0.95²×0.05=2.26 kg; Cycle 4: 2.14 kg; Cycle 5: 2.03 kg. Total/month = 15+2.375+2.26+2.14+2.03 = 23.8 kg. Annual = 285.6 kg trans fat.
- Step 2: HOSO trans fat = negligible (<0.5% initially, minimal generation due to high MUFA oxidative stability). Annual reduction ≈ 285 kg trans fat.
- Step 3: Patient meal omega-6:omega-3 current: Vanaspati LA 20%, ALA 1% → 15 g gives LA 3 g, ALA 0.15 g. Ratio = 20:1 (plus other dietary omega-6).
- Step 4: Proposed HOSO: LA 8%, ALA 0.5% → 15 g gives LA 1.2 g, ALA 0.075 g. Ratio = 16:1 — still high but LA absolute intake reduced 60%.
- Step 5: Recommendation — HOSO reduces trans fat dramatically but omega-6:3 ratio remains suboptimal. Add 2 g flaxseed oil per meal cold to bring ratio toward 5:1.
ANSWER: Annual trans fat reduction ≈ 285 kg. Patient meal omega-6:omega-3 improves from 20:1 to 16:1 (LA reduced 60%), but still exceeds 10:1 — requires supplemental ALA (flaxseed oil 2 g/meal) for optimal ratio.
DIFFICULTY: hard
