## DEFINITION
A distance-time graph is a graphical representation plotting distance travelled (on the y-axis) against time elapsed (on the x-axis) for an object moving along a straight line. The slope of the graph at any point gives the speed of the object at that instant, with SI units of metres per second (m/s).
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
analyze
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can interpret any distance-time graph to determine whether motion is uniform or non-uniform, calculate instantaneous and average speeds from slopes, compare speeds of multiple objects on the same graph, and explain why a distance-time graph cannot represent direction of motion.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers interpretation of distance-time graphs for rectilinear motion including: straight lines (uniform motion), curves (non-uniform acceleration/deceleration), horizontal lines (rest), and comparison of multiple objects. Does NOT cover velocity-time graphs, displacement-time graphs, or motion in two dimensions.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are standing at a railway crossing in Delhi and watch a train pass by. You note its distance from you every 10 seconds: at 0 s it is 0 m, at 10 s it is 200 m, at 20 s it is 400 m, at 30 s it is 600 m. Plot these points on graph paper with time on the horizontal axis and distance on the vertical axis. You will see the points lie on a straight line passing through the origin. This straight line tells you the train moves with constant speed — a concept you studied in Class 7 as uniform motion (c7_physics_ch04_008). Now calculate the slope: rise over run = (600-0) m / (30-0) s = 20 m/s. This slope is the speed. If the train had slowed down, the line would curve, becoming less steep. The steeper the line, the greater the speed. This visual connection between slope and speed is the key principle of distance-time graphs.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What physical quantity does the slope of a distance-time graph represent, and what are its SI units?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The slope represents speed, with SI units of metres per second (m/s). A wrong answer such as "velocity" reveals the student confuses scalar distance with vector displacement; a wrong answer such as "acceleration" reveals confusion with velocity-time graphs where slope gives acceleration.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
A distance-time graph for a car shows a straight line passing through the origin with a slope of 15 m/s. After 20 seconds, the line becomes horizontal. Explain what the horizontal section means physically and why the graph cannot tell us whether the car is facing north or south.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The horizontal section means the car is at rest (speed = 0 m/s) because distance does not change with time. The graph cannot indicate direction because distance is a scalar quantity — it only measures path length, not displacement. A wrong answer stating "the car moves backwards" reveals the student thinks a flat line means negative speed, confusing distance-time with displacement-time graphs.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Two cyclists, A and B, start from the same point at the same time. Cyclist A's distance-time graph is a straight line with slope 5 m/s. Cyclist B's graph is a curve that starts steeper than A's but becomes less steep after 10 s. At t = 15 s, both have travelled 75 m. Calculate the average speed of each cyclist over 15 s and explain which cyclist had a higher initial speed.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Average speed = total distance / total time = 75 m / 15 s = 5 m/s for both. Cyclist B had higher initial speed because the curve starts steeper than A's straight line (slope > 5 m/s initially). A wrong answer giving different average speeds reveals the student does not understand average speed depends only on total distance and total time, not the path taken.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
A student draws a distance-time graph for a ball thrown vertically upward and claims the graph goes up, then down, forming an inverted V shape. Identify the error in this graph and explain what the correct graph should look like.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The error: distance travelled never decreases — it always increases or stays constant. The graph cannot go down. The correct graph curves upward with decreasing slope (ball slows down), becomes horizontal at the top (momentarily at rest), then curves upward with increasing slope (ball speeds up downward). A wrong answer accepting the inverted V reveals the student confuses distance with displacement or height.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate this statement: "If two objects have the same distance-time graph, they must have the same velocity." Is it true or false? Justify your answer.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
False. Distance-time graphs show speed (magnitude only), not velocity (magnitude and direction). Two objects moving in opposite directions at the same speed will have identical distance-time graphs but opposite velocities. A student answering "true" reveals confusion between speed and velocity, and between distance and displacement.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple experiment using a toy car, a metre scale, and a stopwatch to collect data for plotting a distance-time graph of non-uniform motion. Describe how you would ensure the motion is non-uniform and what shape you expect the graph to have.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Place the toy car on an inclined ramp (e.g., a wooden plank raised at one end) so it accelerates down. Mark positions at 0 cm, 20 cm, 40 cm, 60 cm, 80 cm, 100 cm. Release from rest and measure time to reach each mark. Plot distance vs time. The graph will curve upward with increasing slope (getting steeper) because the car speeds up. A poor answer using a flat surface with constant push reveals the student does not know how to produce measurable non-uniform motion.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students believe a distance-time graph can have a negative slope (line going downwards) when an object "returns" or "moves backwards". They confuse distance (scalar, always non-decreasing) with displacement (vector, can decrease). For example, a student draws a graph for a person walking 10 m forward, then 10 m back to start, showing a line going up then down to zero. This is wrong — the correct graph goes up to 10 m, then continues up to 20 m (total path length).
## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Have the student walk 5 m forward, stop, then walk 5 m back to start. Ask: "Did the distance you travelled decrease when you turned back?" (No, it increased from 5 m to 10 m). Step 2: Plot the actual distance-time data: t=0 s, d=0 m; t=5 s, d=5 m; t=10 s, d=10 m. Step 3: Contrast with a displacement-time graph for the same walk (goes up to +5 m, then down to 0 m). Step 4: Emphasise: "Distance never decreases — odometer in a car never rolls backward."

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A school bus leaves the depot at 7:00 AM. The following data is recorded: at 7:00 AM (t=0 s), distance = 0 km; at 7:10 AM (t=600 s), distance = 6 km; at 7:20 AM (t=1200 s), distance = 12 km; at 7:30 AM (t=1800 s), distance = 18 km. Plot the distance-time graph and determine the speed of the bus in m/s.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given quantities — time values: 0 s, 600 s, 1200 s, 1800 s; distance values: 0 km, 6 km, 12 km, 18 km. Convert distances to metres: 0 m, 6000 m, 12000 m, 18000 m. Need to find speed in m/s.
- Step 2: The formula is speed = slope = Δd/Δt. Since points lie on a straight line through origin, choose any two points. Use (0, 0) and (1800 s, 18000 m). Check units: distance in m, time in s, so speed will be in m/s.
- Step 3: Substitute values: Δd = 18000 m - 0 m = 18000 m; Δt = 1800 s - 0 s = 1800 s. Speed = 18000 m / 1800 s.
- Step 4: Compute: 18000 / 1800 = 10. Units: m/s. The bus travels at constant speed 10 m/s.
- Step 5: Verify reasonableness: 10 m/s = 36 km/h, a typical city bus speed. The straight line confirms uniform motion. Answer: 10 m/s.

REASONING: The graph is a straight line through origin, indicating uniform motion. The slope (rise/run) gives the constant speed. Converting km to m and minutes to seconds ensures SI unit consistency. The speed 10 m/s (36 km/h) is physically reasonable for a school bus in urban traffic.

ANSWER: 10 m/s
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A girl cycles from home to school. Her distance-time graph shows: from t=0 to t=300 s, a straight line with slope 4 m/s; from t=300 s to t=600 s, a horizontal line; from t=600 s to t=900 s, a straight line with slope 6 m/s. Calculate (a) distance from home to school, (b) total time of journey, (c) average speed for the whole journey.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given segments: Segment 1 (0-300 s): slope = 4 m/s. Segment 2 (300-600 s): horizontal, slope = 0. Segment 3 (600-900 s): slope = 6 m/s. Need total distance, total time, average speed.
- Step 2: For each segment, distance = speed × time (since slope = speed). Use formula d = v × t for constant speed segments. For horizontal segment, distance = 0.
- Step 3: Segment 1: d₁ = 4 m/s × 300 s = 1200 m. Segment 2: d₂ = 0 m/s × 300 s = 0 m. Segment 3: d₃ = 6 m/s × 300 s = 1800 m. Total distance = 1200 + 0 + 1800 = 3000 m.
- Step 4: Total time = 900 s (from 0 to 900 s). Average speed = total distance / total time = 3000 m / 900 s.
- Step 5: Compute: 3000/900 = 3.33 m/s. Verify: average speed (3.33 m/s) lies between the two non-zero speeds (4 and 6 m/s) but is lowered by the rest period. Physically reasonable for a cycle journey with a stop.

REASONING: The horizontal segment represents a rest (maybe a red light). Distance covered during rest is zero. Average speed considers total path length over total time including stops. The graph's piecewise linear nature allows calculating each segment's distance using slope × time interval.

ANSWER: (a) 3000 m, (b) 900 s, (c) 3.33 m/s
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Two autos, Auto A and Auto B, start from the same auto stand at the same time. Auto A's distance-time graph is a straight line with slope 8 m/s. Auto B starts from rest and accelerates uniformly; its distance-time graph is a curve passing through (0,0), (5 s, 50 m), (10 s, 200 m), (15 s, 450 m). At what time does Auto B overtake Auto A? What is the distance from the stand at that moment?

STEPS:
- Step 1: Auto A: uniform motion, d_A = 8t. Auto B: non-uniform motion. Need to find t when d_B = d_A. First, find pattern for Auto B's distances: at t=5, d=50; t=10, d=200; t=15, d=450.
- Step 2: Check if distances follow d = ½at² (uniform acceleration from rest). For t=5: ½a(25)=50 → a=4 m/s². For t=10: ½(4)(100)=200 ✓. For t=15: ½(4)(225)=450 ✓. So d_B = 2t².
- Step 3: Set d_A = d_B: 8t = 2t² → 2t² - 8t = 0 → 2t(t - 4) = 0. Solutions: t=0 (start) and t=4 s.
- Step 4: At t=4 s, distance = d_A = 8 × 4 = 32 m. Check d_B = 2(4)² = 32 m. Both match.
- Step 5: Verify: Auto B starts slower (initial speed 0) but accelerates at 4 m/s². At t=4 s, its speed = at = 16 m/s > 8 m/s, so it overtakes. Distance 32 m is reasonable for autos in 4 seconds.

REASONING: This combines distance-time graph interpretation (recognizing uniform acceleration gives quadratic distance-time relation) with algebraic solution. The curve's data points reveal the acceleration, allowing equation formulation. Overtaking occurs when distances equal (not speeds equal).

ANSWER: Auto B overtakes Auto A at t = 4 s, at a distance of 32 m from the stand.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A distance-time graph for a train journey shows three distinct sections: a straight line with positive slope, a horizontal line, and another straight line with a smaller positive slope. The train travels on a straight track. Explain what each section represents in terms of the train's motion, and explain why the third section's smaller slope does NOT necessarily mean the train is moving in the opposite direction.

COMPETENCY: Interpreting physical situations using graphs
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the three sections and their slopes
- Step 2: Relate slope to speed (magnitude only)
- Step 3: Explain horizontal line means zero speed (stationary)
- Step 4: Explain smaller positive slope means lower speed, same direction (distance still increasing)
- Step 5: Clarify that distance-time graphs cannot show direction
ANSWER: Section 1: Train moves at constant speed (straight line, constant slope). Section 2: Train is stationary at a station (horizontal line, distance constant, speed = 0). Section 3: Train moves again at constant but lower speed (straight line, smaller positive slope). The smaller slope means lower speed, NOT opposite direction, because distance continues to increase — it never decreases. Distance is a scalar; it only accumulates. Direction requires a displacement-time graph.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A student records the motion of a ball rolling down an inclined plane. The distance-time data is: t=0 s, d=0 m; t=1 s, d=0.5 m; t=2 s, d=2.0 m; t=3 s, d=4.5 m; t=4 s, d=8.0 m. The student plots this and claims the graph is a straight line, so the motion is uniform. Evaluate this claim. If incorrect, determine the acceleration of the ball.

COMPETENCY: Analyzing data to identify motion type and calculate acceleration
STEPS:
- Step 1: Check if distance is proportional to time (uniform motion) or time squared (uniform acceleration)
- Step 2: Calculate d/t for each point: 0.5/1=0.5, 2/2=1, 4.5/3=1.5, 8/4=2 → not constant, so not uniform
- Step 3: Calculate d/t² for each point: 0.5/1=0.5, 2/4=0.5, 4.5/9=0.5, 8/16=0.5 → constant!
- Step 4: For uniform acceleration from rest, d = ½at², so d/t² = a/2 = 0.5 → a = 1 m/s²
- Step 5: Conclude the graph is a parabola (curved), not a straight line. The student's claim is wrong.
ANSWER: The claim is incorrect. The distances are proportional to t² (d/t² = 0.5 constant), indicating uniform acceleration. The graph is a curve (parabola), not a straight line. Acceleration a = 2 × 0.5 = 1 m/s².
DIFFICULTY: hard
