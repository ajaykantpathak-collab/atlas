## DEFINITION
Cognitive Behaviour Therapy (CBT) is a structured, time-limited, evidence-based psychotherapy that integrates cognitive therapy (identifying and restructuring maladaptive thoughts and core schemas) with behavioural techniques (modifying overt behaviours through conditioning principles), grounded in a bio-psychosocial framework addressing biological arousal, psychological cognitions, and social-environmental contingencies.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can: (1) Explain how CBT's bio-psychosocial model integrates biological (relaxation), cognitive (restructuring), and behavioural (exposure, skills training) components for a given disorder; (2) Apply the ABC model and identify cognitive distortions in a case vignette; (3) Design a basic CBT intervention plan including hierarchy construction, behavioural experiments, and homework assignments.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers: CBT's theoretical integration (bio-psychosocial), ABC analysis, cognitive distortions, core schemas, behavioural techniques (exposure, activity scheduling, behavioural experiments), relaxation training, and application to anxiety/depression. Excludes: Detailed protocol manuals for specific disorders (e.g., CBT-E for eating disorders), third-wave therapies (ACT, DBT, MBCT), and neurobiological mechanisms — these are beyond NCERT scope.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine a Class 12 student, Rohan, who freezes during board exam mock tests — heart racing, thoughts like "I'll fail, my future is ruined," leading to avoidance of practice papers. How would a CBT therapist help? Start by recognising this as a bio-psychosocial problem: biological (arousal), psychological (catastrophic thoughts), social (exam pressure). First, map the ABC: Activating event (mock test) → Beliefs ("I must score 95% or I'm a failure") → Consequences (panic, avoidance). Then introduce behavioural techniques: graded exposure to timed practice, relaxation training for arousal. Next, cognitive restructuring: identify distortions (all-or-nothing, catastrophising), challenge evidence, develop balanced thoughts. Finally, assign homework — thought records, behavioural experiments. The key insight: CBT doesn't just "talk about feelings" — it actively changes the maintaining cycles across all three domains simultaneously.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What are the three domains addressed by CBT's bio-psychosocial approach, and name one technique used for each domain?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The three domains are: (1) Biological — addressed via relaxation procedures (e.g., progressive muscular relaxation, diaphragmatic breathing) to reduce physiological arousal; (2) Psychological — addressed via cognitive therapy techniques (e.g., cognitive restructuring, ABC analysis, identifying negative automatic thoughts) and behavioural techniques (e.g., exposure, activity scheduling); (3) Social — addressed via environmental manipulations (e.g., social skills training, modifying reinforcement contingencies in family/school). A wrong answer that lists only "thoughts and behaviours" misses the biological (relaxation) and social (environmental) components, revealing a reductionist view of CBT as merely "cognitive + behavioural" without the bio-psychosocial integration central to NCERT.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
In Ellis's Rational Emotive Therapy (RET), the ABC model proposes that Activating events lead to Consequences (emotional/behavioural) through Beliefs. A student writes: "The activating event (failing a test) directly causes my depression." Why is this statement inconsistent with RET, and what does it reveal about the student's understanding?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The statement is inconsistent because RET posits that Activating events (A) do not directly cause Consequences (C); rather, Beliefs (B) — specifically irrational beliefs characterised by "musts" and "shoulds" — mediate the A→C link. The student's statement reflects the common-sense but incorrect "A causes C" model, revealing a fundamental misunderstanding of cognitive mediation. It shows the student has not grasped that the therapeutic target in RET is the irrational belief system (B), not the activating event (A), and that emotional distress stems from the interpretation, not the event itself.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A client with social anxiety avoids speaking in class. The CBT therapist constructs an anxiety hierarchy: (1) imagining speaking (20/100 anxiety), (2) speaking to a friend (40), (3) asking a question in small group (60), (4) giving a 2-min presentation (80), (5) giving a 10-min presentation (100). The client practices relaxation, then imagines item 1 until anxiety drops below 20, then moves to item 2. Identify the technique and explain the underlying principle.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Technique: Systematic Desensitisation (Wolpe). Principle: Reciprocal Inhibition — a relaxation response (dominant, incompatible with anxiety) inhibits the weaker anxiety response when both are elicited simultaneously. The hierarchy ensures gradual exposure (graded exposure) while maintaining relaxation. A wrong answer naming "flooding" or "exposure therapy" without mentioning relaxation and reciprocal inhibition reveals confusion between systematic desensitisation (graded + relaxation) and other exposure variants (flooding = intense exposure without relaxation; exposure therapy = may not use hierarchy/relaxation). Also, missing "reciprocal inhibition" shows lack of theoretical grounding.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare Beck's cognitive therapy and Ellis's RET on: (a) origin of psychological distress, (b) role of the therapist, (c) therapeutic process. Which approach is more collaborative and why?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
(a) Origin: Beck — childhood experiences form core schemas (e.g., "I am unlovable") triggered by critical incidents → negative automatic thoughts → cognitive distortions → anxiety/depression. Ellis — irrational beliefs (rigid "musts/shoulds") mediate A→C; philosophy of life is central. (b) Therapist role: Beck — collaborative empiricist, gentle Socratic questioning to test beliefs as hypotheses. Ellis — more directive, actively disputes irrational beliefs through logical/empirical/pragmatic challenges. (c) Process: Beck — identify NATs → identify distortions → examine evidence → develop alternative thoughts → behavioural experiments. Ellis — ABC analysis → identify irrational beliefs → dispute (D) → effective new philosophy (E) → new feelings/behaviours (F). Beck is more collaborative because the therapist and client work as co-investigators ("collaborative empiricism"), whereas Ellis's therapist takes a more expert, disputing stance. A wrong answer claiming they are "the same" or "both just challenge thoughts" misses the structural differences in theory of aetiology and therapeutic stance.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate this claim: "CBT is superior to all other psychotherapies because research shows it has the highest effect sizes for anxiety and depression." Consider: (i) what "superior" means in psychotherapy research, (ii) the role of common factors (therapeutic alliance, expectancy), (iii) limitations of RCT methodology for complex therapies.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The claim overstates evidence. (i) "Superior" in RCTs typically means statistically significant difference in symptom reduction on specific measures at post-treatment — not necessarily clinically meaningful, durable, or generalisable to real-world settings. (ii) Common factors (alliance, empathy, expectancy, client factors) account for 30-70% of variance in outcomes across therapies; CBT's specific techniques add incremental value but are not the sole active ingredient. (iii) RCTs use manualised protocols, highly selected samples, expert therapists — limiting ecological validity. Many trials lack active control groups (e.g., pill placebo, supportive therapy), inflating effect sizes. Also, publication bias favours positive CBT trials. NCERT notes CBT is "short and efficacious" but does not claim superiority over all therapies. A wrong answer accepting the claim uncritically reveals lack of research literacy and confusion between statistical significance and clinical superiority.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a mini CBT case formulation for a 17-year-old student, Priya, who experiences panic attacks before chemistry practical exams. Include: (a) bio-psychosocial predisposing/precipitating/perpetuating factors, (b) a vicious cycle diagram description (thoughts → feelings → behaviours → physical), (c) two specific CBT interventions with rationale.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
(a) Predisposing: Biological — family history of anxiety, high trait neuroticism; Psychological — perfectionistic core schema ("I must be perfect or I'm inadequate"); Social — high parental expectations, competitive school environment. Precipitating: Failed a chemistry practical in Class 11, public criticism by teacher. Perpetuating: Biological — hyperventilation during panic maintains physical symptoms; Psychological — catastrophic misinterpretation of bodily sensations ("heart racing = heart attack"); Behavioural — avoidance of chemistry lab, safety behaviours (carrying water, sitting near exit); Social — parental reassurance reinforces illness role. (b) Vicious cycle: Activating event (entering lab) → Thoughts ("I'll faint, everyone will laugh") → Feelings (terror, shame) → Physical (tachycardia, dyspnea, dizziness) → Behaviours (escape, avoidance) → reinforces Thoughts ("See, I couldn't handle it"). (c) Interventions: 1) Interoceptive exposure + cognitive restructuring: deliberately induce feared sensations (spinning, hyperventilation) in session while testing catastrophic beliefs ("Does dizziness mean heart attack? No, it's hyperventilation") — breaks misinterpretation cycle. 2) Behavioural experiment: graded exposure to lab (visit empty lab → watch peer → assist → perform simple task) with dropout of safety behaviours — tests belief "I cannot cope" and builds self-efficacy. A poor answer listing generic "relaxation and positive thinking" misses case-specific formulation, vicious cycle mechanics, and evidence-based intervention selection.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often equate CBT with "positive thinking" or "replacing negative thoughts with positive ones." This is incorrect. CBT does not encourage unrealistic positive affirmations (e.g., "Everything will be great!"); instead, it teaches **realistic, evidence-based thinking** — evaluating the validity of automatic thoughts, identifying cognitive distortions (all-or-nothing, catastrophising, mind-reading), and generating balanced alternative thoughts (e.g., "I'm anxious about this exam, but I've prepared and can handle whatever happens"). The error arises because "cognitive restructuring" sounds like "making thoughts positive," and pop psychology oversimplifies CBT. In exams, students write "CBT makes you think positively" instead of "CBT helps identify and modify distorted cognitions through collaborative empiricism and behavioural experiments."

## ON_FAILURE
If a student writes "CBT = positive thinking," do this 3-step remediation: (1) Show a thought record from a real CBT session (or NCERT Box 5.1 style) where the "Alternative Thought" column is balanced, not positive (e.g., Situation: "Friend didn't reply to text" | NAT: "She hates me" | Distortion: Mind-reading | Evidence for/against | Balanced: "She might be busy; I'll ask later"). (2) Contrast with "Positive Thinking" version: "She loves me and will reply soon!" — ask: "Which is more believable? Which reduces anxiety long-term?" (3) Assign: Complete a 3-column thought record (Situation | Automatic Thought | Balanced Thought) for 3 personal stressors this week. Review together — check for realistic, not positive, alternatives. This directly targets the "positive thinking" misconception by experiential learning.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Rahul, 17, experiences intense anxiety before Physics board exam. He thinks: "If I don't get 95%, I'm a total failure and my parents will hate me." He avoids studying Physics, feels nauseous, heart races. Using CBT's bio-psychosocial framework, formulate his case and design a 3-session intervention plan.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Bio-psychosocial assessment — Biological: high trait anxiety, autonomic hyperreactivity (nausea, tachycardia); Psychological: perfectionistic core schema ("Must be perfect → failure"), cognitive distortions (all-or-nothing, catastrophising, mind-reading); Social: high parental expectations, comparative school environment.
- Step 2: ABC analysis — A: Physics exam preparation; B: "Must get 95% or I'm worthless + parents will reject me"; C: Anxiety (8/10), avoidance (procrastination), physical symptoms.
- Step 3: Vicious cycle — Avoidance → temporary relief (negative reinforcement) → increased belief "I can't handle Physics" → more anxiety → more avoidance.
- Step 4: Session 1 intervention — Psychoeducation (bio-psychosocial model), relaxation training (diaphragmatic breathing, PMR) for biological arousal, activity scheduling (15-min Physics study + 5-min break) for behavioural activation.
- Step 5: Session 2 intervention — Cognitive restructuring: Thought record for "95% or failure" → identify distortions → examine evidence (past scores, parental love not grade-dependent) → balanced thought: "I want to do well, but my worth isn't defined by one exam."
- Step 6: Session 3 intervention — Behavioural experiment: Timed practice test (exposure) without safety behaviours; test prediction "I'll blank out" vs. actual outcome; homework: daily thought records + graded Physics study.

REASONING: This follows CBT's structured, collaborative approach: assessment → formulation → psychoeducation → skills training (relaxation/behavioural) → cognitive restructuring → behavioural experiments. Each step targets one bio-psychosocial domain while maintaining the cycle's interconnections. The 3-session plan mirrors CBT's time-limited nature (10-20 sessions total; here condensed for illustration).

ANSWER: Case formulation covers all three domains; intervention plan includes relaxation (bio), cognitive restructuring (psycho), activity scheduling/exposure (behavioural), with homework for generalisation.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: In a CBT session for social anxiety, the therapist and client construct this anxiety hierarchy for "speaking in class": 1) Imagine raising hand (SUDS 30), 2) Raise hand in empty classroom (SUDS 45), 3) Ask teacher question after class (SUDS 60), 4) Answer teacher's question in class (SUDS 75), 5) Give 2-min presentation to class (SUDS 90). SUDS = Subjective Units of Distress Scale (0-100). The client practices PMR, then imagines item 1 until SUDS drops to 15, then moves to item 2 in vivo. Explain why: (a) hierarchy is graded, (b) relaxation precedes exposure, (c) in vivo exposure is used for higher items, (d) SUDS tracking is essential.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Graded hierarchy (a) — ensures client succeeds at each step (mastery), prevents flooding (overwhelming anxiety that strengthens fear), builds self-efficacy incrementally. Based on shaping principle (successive approximations).
- Step 2: Relaxation before exposure (b) — reciprocal inhibition (Wolpe): relaxation response physiologically inhibits anxiety response; client learns anxiety is manageable. Without relaxation, exposure may sensitise (increase fear).
- Step 3: In vivo for higher items (c) — imaginal exposure has limited generalisation; in vivo provides realistic sensory cues, tests catastrophic predictions in real context, builds stronger inhibitory learning.
- Step 4: SUDS tracking (d) — quantifies habituation (within-session: SUDS drops 50%+; between-sessions: peak SUDS decreases across sessions); guides pacing (stay until habituation); provides data for client/therapist to evaluate progress.

REASONING: Systematic desensitisation relies on three pillars: (1) graded exposure (hierarchy), (2) reciprocal inhibition (relaxation), (3) habituation (SUDS decline). Skipping any pillar reduces efficacy. In vivo exposure is superior to imaginal for social anxiety because social cues (faces, voices) are complex and hard to fully imagine.

ANSWER: (a) Graded hierarchy enables mastery and prevents flooding; (b) Relaxation enables reciprocal inhibition of anxiety; (c) In vivo exposure ensures realistic cue processing and stronger inhibitory learning; (d) SUDS tracking quantifies habituation and guides treatment pacing.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Anaya, 16, has depression. Core schema: "I am unlovable." Negative automatic thoughts (NATs): "Nobody cares," "I'm a burden." Cognitive distortions: personalising, emotional reasoning, discounting positives. She isolates, stops attending dance class (formerly enjoyed), sleeps 12 hrs/day. Design a CBT intervention integrating behavioural activation (BA) and cognitive restructuring (CR) for first 4 weeks. Include specific homework.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Week 1 — Assessment + Psychoeducation + Behavioural Activation (BA) baseline. Activity monitoring log (hourly: activity, mood 0-10, mastery/pleasure 0-10). Identify "depression cycle": low activity → low mood → negative thoughts → lower activity. Rationale: BA breaks inertia before cognitive work (depressed cognition impairs CR).
- Step 2: Week 2 — BA: Activity scheduling. Target: 1 mastery (e.g., 15-min dance stretch) + 1 pleasure (e.g., call friend) daily. Use "action precedes motivation" principle. Graded task assignment: break dance into micro-steps (put on shoes → play music → 5-min stretch). Homework: Complete scheduled activities, rate mood/mastery/pleasure.
- Step 3: Week 3 — Introduce Cognitive Restructuring (CR). Thought record: Situation → NAT → Distortion → Evidence For/Against → Balanced Thought. Start with behavioural experiment data from BA (e.g., "I called friend → she was happy → contradicts 'nobody cares'"). Rationale: Concrete behavioural evidence makes CR more credible than abstract disputation.
- Step 4: Week 4 — Integrated BA+CR. Behavioural experiment: Attend dance class for 30 min. Prediction: "Everyone will ignore me" (SUDS 80). Test: Go, observe actual reactions. Post-experiment: Thought record using real data. Homework: Continue activity scheduling + 2 thought records/day. Review core schema "unlovable" using continuum technique (0-100% lovable: place self, then place "average teen," "friend," "parent" — usually reveals double standard).

REASONING: This sequence follows CBT-for-depression protocol (Beck et al.): BA first (behavioural activation precedes cognitive change in severe depression), then CR using behavioural evidence, then schema work. The integration is key — BA provides data for CR; CR reduces avoidance maintaining depression. Continuum technique directly targets core schema (Beck), not just NATs.

ANSWER: 4-week integrated plan: Week 1 — monitoring/psychoeducation; Week 2 — graded activity scheduling; Week 3 — thought records using BA data; Week 4 — behavioural experiment + continuum technique for core schema. Homework each week builds skills progressively.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A Class 12 student, Vikram, presents with exam anxiety. He says: "CBT is just about changing thoughts. Why do I need to do behavioural experiments and exposure? Can't I just think positively?" As a trainee therapist, explain to Vikram: (a) Why behavioural experiments are necessary in CBT, not optional; (b) How behavioural experiments test thoughts differently than cognitive restructuring alone; (c) Why "positive thinking" is not the goal of CBT.

COMPETENCY: Translating theoretical concepts to clinical rationale and psychoeducation

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the misconception — Vikram equates CBT with cognitive-only approach and "positive thinking."
- Step 2: Explain behavioural experiments as "hypothesis testing" — thoughts are treated as hypotheses, not facts; experiments provide experiential evidence that cognitive disputation alone cannot.
- Step 3: Contrast cognitive restructuring (verbal/logical level) vs. behavioural experiments (experiential/emotional level) — the latter engages "hot" cognition in real contexts.
- Step 4: Clarify CBT's goal: realistic, balanced thinking based on evidence, not positive affirmations; cite Beck's "collaborative empiricism."
- Step 5: Give a concrete example relevant to exam anxiety (e.g., experiment: "Study 1 hour without notes → test prediction 'I'll remember nothing'").

ANSWER: (a) Behavioural experiments are necessary because anxiety/depression are maintained by avoidance and safety behaviours that prevent disconfirmation of catastrophic beliefs; only behavioural action generates new learning. (b) Cognitive restructuring works at the "cold" verbal level; behavioural experiments engage "hot" cognition in the actual feared situation, producing stronger belief change (e.g., "I handled it" vs. "I think I can handle it"). (c) "Positive thinking" ignores evidence and feels inauthentic; CBT seeks accurate, balanced appraisals (e.g., "I'm anxious but prepared") that reduce distress because they are believable.

DIFFICULTY: medium

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2

A 15-year-old, Meera, has panic disorder with agoraphobia. She fears crowded markets (social), experiences tachycardia/dizziness (biological), and thinks "I'll faint and nobody will help" (psychological). Her mother accompanies her everywhere (social maintaining factor).
Design a comprehensive CBT case formulation and 6-session treatment plan explicitly mapping each intervention to the bio-psychosocial domain it targets. Justify the sequencing.

COMPETENCY: Integrating multi-domain formulation with treatment planning and justifying clinical decisions

STEPS:
- Step 1: Formulation — 4Ps (Predisposing, Precipitating, Perpetuating, Protective) across bio-psycho-social domains.
- Step 2: Vicious cycle — Interoceptive cues (bio) → Catastrophic misinterpretation (psycho) → Avoidance/safety behaviours (behavioural) → Social restriction (social) → Increased sensitivity.
- Step 3: Session 1-2: Psychoeducation (all domains), PMR/diaphragmatic breathing (bio), Activity monitoring (behavioural).
- Step 4: Session 3-4: Interoceptive exposure (bio+psycho), Cognitive restructuring of "fainting" belief using evidence (psycho), Graded in vivo exposure hierarchy for markets (behavioural+social).
- Step 5: Session 5: Social skills/assertiveness for asking help (social), Mother involved: reduce accommodation (social maintaining factor).
- Step 6: Session 6: Relapse prevention, booster plan, schema work if needed (psycho).

ANSWER: Formulation: Predisposing (bio: anxiety sensitivity; psycho: high anxiety sensitivity index; social: overprotective parenting). Precipitating: First panic attack in market. Perpetuating: Bio — hyperventilation maintains symptoms; Psycho — catastrophic misinterpretation; Behavioural — avoidance + safety behaviour (mother); Social — restricted world, mother's accommodation. Protective: Motivation, supportive father. Treatment: Sessions 1-2 target bio (relaxation) + psycho (psychoeducation) + behavioural (monitoring) to build skills before exposure. Sessions 3-4 target core maintaining cycle: interoceptive exposure (bio+psycho), cognitive restructuring (psycho), graded in vivo exposure (behavioural+social). Session 5 targets social maintaining factor (mother's accommodation) via psychoeducation + behavioural contract. Session 6 consolidates. Sequencing follows CBT principle: skills before exposure, behavioural before cognitive for panic (interoceptive exposure provides evidence for CR), social/environmental last.
