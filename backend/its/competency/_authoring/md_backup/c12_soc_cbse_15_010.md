## DEFINITION
The Workers' Movement in India refers to the organized collective struggles of industrial labour — beginning with spontaneous protests in colonial factories, evolving into structured trade unionism (AITUC, INTUC, and later political-party-affiliated unions), and culminating in major confrontations like the Bombay Textile Strike (1981-82) and contemporary struggles of informal/contract workers against neoliberal labour reforms.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
analyze

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to: (1) trace the historical trajectory from spontaneous worker protests to institutionalized trade unionism and explain how each phase was shaped by colonial industrial policy, nationalist politics, and post-Independence state-labour relations; (2) analyze the Bombay Textile Strike as a turning point that revealed the limits of traditional unionism and the emergence of new labour struggles in the informal sector.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the Workers' Movement from colonial factory origins (1860s) through the formation of AITUC (1920), the INTUC split (1947), the 1974 railway strike, the Bombay Textile Strike (1981-82), and the shift to new labour struggles (informalisation, contract labour, neoliberal reforms). It does NOT cover peasant movements (c12_soc_cbse_15_004), tribal movements (c12_soc_cbse_15_007), or environmental movements (c12_soc_cbse_15_009) — those are separate nodes.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are a textile mill worker in Bombay in 1982: you haven't been paid for months, your union leaders are negotiating behind closed doors, and 2.5 lakh workers like you are on strike — but the mills never reopen. Why did the longest industrial strike in Indian history end in defeat? Start by mapping the three phases: colonial spontaneity (pre-1920), institutionalized unionism (1920-1980), and the crisis of traditional unions (post-1981). Connect each phase to the political economy: colonial export-oriented industrialization, nationalist mobilization, post-Independence state-led development, and finally neoliberal restructuring. The key insight is that the Bombay strike wasn't just a wage dispute — it was the moment the old worker-union-state compact collapsed, giving rise to today's fragmented, informalised labour struggles.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What was the first trade union established in India, who founded it, and in which year?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The first trade union in India was established in April 1918 in Madras by B.P. Wadia, a social worker and member of the Theosophical Society. A wrong answer (e.g., naming AITUC as the first union, or attributing it to Gandhi) reveals the student conflates the first union with the first *national* federation, or confuses the Textile Labour Association (founded by Gandhi in 1918) with Wadia's Madras Labour Union.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why the All India Trade Union Congress (AITUC) split in 1947 and what new organisation was formed as a result.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The AITUC split in 1947 because the Indian National Congress, uncomfortable with growing communist control over AITUC (led by S.A. Dange and M.N. Roy), formed a rival federation — the Indian National Trade Union Congress (INTUC) — in May 1947. A wrong answer (e.g., attributing the split to ideological differences alone, or saying INTUC was formed by communists) shows the student misses the political-party-driven nature of Indian trade unionism: unions have historically been extensions of political parties, not autonomous worker organisations.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
The Bombay Textile Strike of 1981-82 involved approximately 2.5 lakh workers and lasted over 18 months. Apply the concept of 'political-party-driven unionism' to explain why the strike failed despite massive participation.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The strike failed because: (1) The Rashtriya Mill Mazdoor Sangh (RMMS), the recognised union affiliated to Congress/INTUC, was seen as colluding with mill owners; (2) Rival unions (communist-affiliated, socialist-affiliated) fragmented worker unity; (3) The state used the Essential Services Maintenance Act (ESMA) and police repression; (4) Mill owners, facing structural unviability, used the strike as pretext for permanent closure. A wrong answer focusing only on "worker disunity" or "state repression" misses the structural shift: the strike marked the end of the post-Independence social compact where the state mediated labour-capital relations through recognised unions.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the colonial-era worker protests (pre-1920) with the post-1947 institutionalised trade union phase on THREE dimensions: organisation, ideology, and relationship with the nationalist movement.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
| Dimension | Colonial-era (pre-1920) | Post-1947 Institutionalised Phase |
|-----------|------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| Organisation | Spontaneous, localised, no formal structure | Formal federations (AITUC, INTUC, HMS, UTUC), recognised unions, collective bargaining |
| Ideology | Economic grievances (wages, conditions), no coherent ideology | Explicitly ideological: communist, socialist, nationalist, Gandhian — each tied to a political party |
| Nationalist movement | Nationalist leaders (Gandhi, Tilak) *drew workers in* (e.g., 1917 Bombay strikes, Ahmedabad 1918) | Unions *became arms of political parties*; INTUC = Congress, AITUC = CPI, HMS = Socialists — worker autonomy subordinated to party politics |

A wrong answer that treats both phases as "same but bigger" misses the qualitative shift from *moral economy* protests to *political-party-controlled* interest-group politics.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate the claim: "The 1974 railway strike was the last major offensive by the organised working class in India; after that, the workers' movement entered a defensive, fragmented phase." Support your evaluation with evidence from the trajectory of trade unionism.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The claim is largely accurate. The 1974 railway strike (led by George Fernandes, 1.7 million workers) was a high-water mark of organised labour's offensive capacity — it challenged the state directly during the Emergency prelude. After 1974: (1) The 1981-82 Bombay Textile Strike ended in catastrophic defeat and deindustrialisation; (2) Liberalisation (1991) accelerated informalisation — today >90% workforce is informal, outside union coverage; (3) Unions fragmented further along party lines, unable to organise contract/casual workers; (4) New labour struggles (e.g., Maruti Suzuki 2011-12, gig worker protests) are spontaneous, leaderless, and lack legal recognition. A wrong answer that says "unions are still strong" ignores the structural decline in union density, collective bargaining coverage, and the shift from 'class-based' to 'identity/fragmented' struggles.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a conceptual map showing how the Bombay Textile Strike (1981-82) connects to THREE contemporary 'new labour struggles' in India. For each connection, name the struggle, the structural cause linking them, and the new form of organisation (if any).

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A strong answer maps:
1. **Maruti Suzuki Workers' Struggle (2011-12, Manesar)** — Structural cause: *Contractualisation replacing permanent jobs* (same as Bombay's 'badli' system); New organisation: *Worker-led committees bypassing recognised unions*.
2. **Gig Workers' Protests (2021-24, Swiggy/Zomato/Ola/Uber)** — Structural cause: *Platform capitalism creating 'algorithmically managed' informal labour*; New organisation: *App-based collectives (IFAT, TWU) using digital mobilisation*.
3. **Scheme Workers' Movements (ASHA, Anganwadi, Mid-day Meal workers)** — Structural cause: *State outsourcing core welfare functions to 'volunteers' at honorariums*; New organisation: *Federations demanding 'worker' status and minimum wage*.
A poor answer lists random strikes without identifying the *structural continuity* (informalisation, state withdrawal, contractualisation) or the *organisational innovation* (bypassing traditional unions).

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think the Bombay Textile Strike failed *only* because of "worker disunity" or "bad leadership." The deeper error is missing the **political economy of deindustrialisation**: mill owners *wanted* closure to sell prime Mumbai land for real estate; the state *allowed* it because textile was no longer a priority sector; and the union (RMMS) *collaborated* because its political patrons (Congress) had shifted to pro-business policies. The strike wasn't lost on the picket line — it was lost in the boardroom and the legislature decades earlier.

## ON_FAILURE
If the student reduces the strike to "disunity," do this 3-step remediation: (1) Show the 1982 *Economic & Political Weekly* editorial "The Bombay Textile Strike: A Post-Mortem" — highlight the land-value argument; (2) Have them compare the 1982 strike demands (wage parity, bonus, job security) with the 2022 *Industrial Relations Code* provisions (fixed-term employment, easier retrenchment) — draw the line from defeat to legalised precarity; (3) Assign a 200-word answer: "Why did the Indian state, which once mediated labour-capital conflict through tripartism, become an enabler of informalisation after 1981?" This forces structural analysis over moralistic narratives.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A Class 12 student is asked: "The Bombay Textile Strike of 1981-82 is often called the 'grave of the Indian working class movement.' Analyse this statement by identifying THREE structural factors that made the strike's defeat inevitable, and explain how each factor relates to the political economy of Indian industrialisation after 1947."

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the three structural factors from the political economy of post-Independence industrialisation: (a) State-led import-substitution industrialisation (ISI) created a protected, inefficient textile sector dependent on subsidies; (b) The 'licence-permit raj' tied mill licenses to political patronage, making mill owners politically connected and unions politically dependent; (c) The 1980s fiscal crisis and subsequent liberalisation shifted state priority from employment protection to capital profitability.
- Step 2: Link each factor to the strike's defeat: (a) Mills were already unviable — owners used the strike as excuse to close permanently and monetise land; (b) The recognised union (RMMS) was Congress-affiliated; when Congress (state and Centre) prioritised industrial peace for investment, RMMS could not militant; (c) The state invoked ESMA, deployed police, and refused mediation — signalling that labour militancy would no longer be accommodated.
- Step 3: Contrast with the 1920s-1970s period: earlier, the state *needed* labour peace for nation-building and mediated via tripartism (Indian Labour Conference); post-1981, the state *needed* capital accumulation and saw labour as a cost.
- Step 4: Synthesise: The strike was not just a lost wage dispute — it was the moment the post-colonial social contract (state mediates labour-capital) ruptured, replaced by a neoliberal contract (state facilitates capital, labour disciplined by market).
- Step 5: Verify: Does this explain why *no* major industrial strike has succeeded since? Yes — the structural conditions (global competition, informalisation, anti-union labour codes) make traditional strike leverage obsolete.

REASONING: This analysis moves beyond 'leadership failure' to show how the strike was embedded in a shifting accumulation regime. The three factors — sectoral unviability, party-union dependence, state reorientation — are not contingent but structural, making the defeat overdetermined.

ANSWER: The statement is analytically sound. The three structural factors are: (1) Textile sector's structural unviability under ISI, making closure profitable for owners; (2) Political-party control of unions (RMMS-Congress) preventing autonomous militancy; (3) State's shift from developmentalist mediator to neoliberal facilitator of capital. Together, they ended the era of 'organised working class' as a political force.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: "Trace the evolution of trade union ideology in India from the formation of AITUC (1920) to the split forming INTUC (1947) to the regional party unions (post-1960s). Present this as a three-stage typology with the dominant ideology, key leaders, and relationship to the nationalist/post-colonial state at each stage."

STEPS:
- Step 1: Define Stage 1 (1920-1947): AITUC as a broad united front. Dominant ideology: *Anti-colonial nationalism + emergent class consciousness*. Key leaders: Lala Lajpat Rai (President 1920), C.R. Das, Jawaharlal Nehru, later S.A. Dange (Communist). Relationship to state: *Adversarial* — colonial state repressed unions (Trade Union Act 1926 was concessional but restrictive); unions aligned with Congress for independence.
- Step 2: Define Stage 2 (1947-1960s): Split into INTUC (Congress), AITUC (CPI), HMS (Socialists), UTUC (Revolutionary Socialists). Dominant ideology: *Party-line class struggle within parliamentary democracy*. Key leaders: V.V. Giri (INTUC), S.A. Dange (AITUC). Relationship to state: *Incorporated* — state created tripartite bodies (ILC, SLC), recognised unions, enacted labour laws (ID Act 1947, ESI Act 1948); unions gained legal recognition but lost autonomy.
- Step 3: Define Stage 3 (1960s-present): Proliferation of regional party unions (Shiv Sena's BMS in Maharashtra, DMK's LPF in Tamil Nadu, TMC unions in West Bengal). Dominant ideology: *Regional/ethnic identity + patronage politics*. Key leaders: Bal Thackeray (BMS), regional satraps. Relationship to state: *Transactional* — unions deliver votes/mobilisation; state delivers licences, protection, patronage; class ideology replaced by identity politics.
- Step 4: Identify the continuous thread: *Unions never became autonomous worker organisations* — always mediated by political parties, whether nationalist, communist, or regional.
- Step 5: Explain the consequence: This structural dependence explains why unions could not defend workers against liberalisation — their political patrons *became* the agents of liberalisation (Congress 1991, regional parties 2000s).

REASONING: The typology reveals a trajectory from *anti-colonial unity* to *party-based fragmentation* to *identity-based transactionalism* — each stage deepening the separation between unions and the working class they claim to represent.

ANSWER: 
Stage 1 (1920-47): United front nationalism — Leaders: Lajpat Rai, Nehru, Dange — State: Adversarial colonial.
Stage 2 (1947-60s): Party-federation pluralism — Leaders: V.V. Giri (INTUC), Dange (AITUC) — State: Incorporative tripartism.
Stage 3 (1960s+): Regional identity patronage — Leaders: Thackeray (BMS), regional bosses — State: Transactional clientelism.
Continuity: Political-party mediation prevents union autonomy.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: "The shift from 'workers' movement' to 'new labour struggles' reflects a fundamental transformation in the composition and conditions of the Indian working class. Using the concepts of *formal/informal economy* (Class 11), *contractualisation*, and *platform labour*, construct an argument explaining why traditional trade union strategies (collective bargaining, legal recognition, tripartism) are ineffective for today's majority workforce."

STEPS:
- Step 1: Establish the demographic shift: Cite NSSO/PLFS data — ~91% of India's workforce is informal (self-employed, casual, contract); only ~9% have formal contracts with social security. The 'working class' of the trade union era (permanent factory workers) is now a minority.
- Step 2: Define contractualisation: The *Contract Labour (Regulation & Abolition) Act, 1970* was meant to regulate but became a tool for *permanent contractualisation* — core production outsourced to contractors; workers have no employer-employee relationship with the principal employer, so no collective bargaining rights under the Industrial Disputes Act.
- Step 3: Define platform labour: Gig workers (delivery, ride-hail) are classified as 'partners' not employees; algorithmic management replaces supervisory authority; no fixed workplace, no fixed hours, no union recognition possible under current law (Code on Social Security 2020 gives 'social security' but not 'labour rights').
- Step 4: Map union strategy failure: (a) *Collective bargaining* requires a recognised employer and recognised union — absent in both contractual and platform work; (b) *Legal recognition* (registration under Trade Unions Act) requires 10% or 100 workers — impossible for dispersed, atomised workers; (c) *Tripartism* (ILC/SLC) only includes *recognised* unions of *formal* sector — informal workers have no seat at the table.
- Step 5: Identify emerging alternatives: (a) *Worker-led committees* (Maruti Suzuki) bypassing registered unions; (b) *App-based collectives* (IFAT, TWU) using digital tools for flash mobilisation; (c) *Scheme worker federations* demanding 'worker' status via courts (Supreme Court ASHA judgment 2024); (d) *Legal strategy* — pushing for 'employee' classification via judicial interpretation (e.g., UK Supreme Court Uber judgment cited in Indian PILs).
- Step 6: Conclude: The 'workers' movement' was built for Fordist factories; 'new labour struggles' are responses to post-Fordist, platform, and informal capitalism — requiring new organisational forms, legal categories, and political imaginaries.

REASONING: This answer integrates Class 11 concepts (formal/informal economy) with Class 12 historical trajectory (trade unionism's rise and crisis) and contemporary political economy (platform capitalism, labour codes) — exactly the multi-concept synthesis the board and CUET demand.

ANSWER: Traditional union strategies fail because they presuppose a Fordist employment relation (permanent, identifiable employer, fixed workplace) that now covers <10% of workers. Contractualisation severs the legal employer-employee link; platform labour replaces it with algorithmic control. Without a recognised employer, collective bargaining has no counterparty; without a concentrated workforce, union registration thresholds are unmeetable; without formal sector status, tripartite bodies exclude them. New struggles therefore experiment with: direct action (wildcat strikes), digital mobilisation, legal reclassification litigation, and state-welfare claims — moving beyond the industrial relations framework entirely.

The strike's defeat signals the victory of the second model."

(b) Explain how the *Industrial Disputes Act, 1947* embodied the first model, and how the *Industrial Relations Code, 2020* embodies the second.

(a) Model 1: Nehruvian ISI / developmental state (1950s-80s) — labour as partner. Model 2: Neoliberal liberalisation (1991+) — labour as cost.
(b) ID Act 1947: Compulsory adjudication, Chapter V-B retrenchment protection, tripartism (ILC). IR Code 2020: Fixed-term contracts, retrenchment threshold 300, 60-day strike notice, diluted standing orders.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A gig worker for a food-delivery platform in Bengaluru works 12 hours a day, earns Rs. 15,000/month after fuel costs, has no health insurance, no paid leave, and can be 'deactivated' (fired) by algorithm without hearing. She wants to organise for better conditions.

(a) Why can she not form a trade union under the *Trade Unions Act, 1926* as it stands? Cite two specific legal barriers.
(b) The *Code on Social Security, 2020* provides for 'gig and platform workers' — what does it actually guarantee, and what does it *not* guarantee?
(c) Propose ONE innovative organisational form she could use, drawing on a real Indian example (2020-2024), and explain how it bypasses the legal barriers in (a).

COMPETENCY: Legal-institutional analysis and creative problem-solving — applying labour law knowledge to a contemporary worker category and designing contextually grounded organisational innovation.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Legal barriers under TU Act 1926: (i) Section 2(g) defines 'workmen' as persons employed in 'trade or business' — gig workers classified as 'partners/independent contractors' fall outside; (ii) Section 4 requires 10% or 100 workers *of an establishment* — no single 'establishment' exists for platform workers (dispersed, algorithmically managed).
- Step 2: Code on Social Security 2020: Guarantees — registration on e-Shram portal, access to *some* social security schemes (PM-SYM, PMJAY, ESIC *if* state notifies), life/disability cover. Does NOT guarantee: Labour rights (minimum wage, working hours, collective bargaining, protection from arbitrary deactivation, employer-employee relationship).
- Step 3: Innovative form: *Indian Federation of App-based Transport Workers (IFAT)* model — not a registered trade union but a *federation of worker collectives* using: (i) WhatsApp/Telegram groups per city for rapid mobilisation; (ii) 'Log-off' protests (digital strike) targeting algorithmic metrics; (iii) Strategic litigation (filing PILs for 'employee' status citing UK Uber judgment); (iv) Alliances with formal unions (AITUC, NTUI) for legal/political cover. Bypasses TU Act by not seeking registration — operates as *unregistered association* under Article 19(1)(c), using constitutional freedom of association, not statutory trade union rights.

ANSWER: 
(a) Barriers: (1) Gig workers excluded from 'workman' definition (independent contractor classification); (2) No identifiable 'establishment' for 10%/100-member threshold.
(b) Guarantees: e-Shram registration, access to select social security schemes. Excludes: Labour rights, collective bargaining, minimum wage, protection from algorithmic termination.
(c) IFAT model: Unregistered federation using digital mobilisation (log-off protests), strategic litigation (employee status), and formal union alliances — operates under Article 19(1)(c) constitutional right, bypassing TU Act registration requirements.

DIFFICULTY: hard
