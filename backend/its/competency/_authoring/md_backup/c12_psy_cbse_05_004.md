## DEFINITION
Behaviour therapy is a therapeutic approach based on learning theory principles that identifies malfunctioning behaviours, their antecedent triggers, and consequent maintaining factors, then applies antecedent operations (modifying triggers) and consequent operations (modifying reinforcements/punishments) to replace faulty behaviours with adaptive ones.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to: (1) conduct a behavioural analysis (identify target behaviour, antecedents, and maintaining consequences) for a given case vignette, and (2) design a treatment package specifying at least one antecedent operation and one consequent operation with justification from learning principles.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers behavioural analysis (ABC model), antecedent operations (establishing operations), consequent operations (reinforcement/punishment types), and core techniques (systematic desensitisation, aversive conditioning, token economy, differential reinforcement, modelling). It does NOT cover cognitive therapy techniques (RET, Beck's cognitive restructuring) or CBT integration — those are in subsequent nodes.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine a Class 12 student, Rohan, who procrastinates on studying by scrolling social media until 2 AM, then feels anxious and sleeps poorly. Where would a behaviour therapist start? Not with "why" but with "what, when, and what happens after." First, we define the target behaviour (procrastination/social media use). Next, we identify antecedents: fatigue after school, phone on desk, notification sounds. Then consequences: immediate dopamine relief from boredom, avoidance of difficult subjects. The therapist then designs antecedent operations (phone in another room during study hours, scheduled breaks) and consequent operations (reward for 45-min focused study, removal of phone privilege if exceeded). This ABC analysis is the engine of behaviour therapy — every technique flows from it. Let's break down each component systematically.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Define "malfunctioning behaviour," "antecedent factors," and "maintaining factors" in behavioural analysis. Give a one-sentence example for each.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Malfunctioning behaviour: any overt or covert action causing distress to the client (e.g., smoking 20 cigarettes daily). Antecedent factors: environmental or internal events that precede and trigger the behaviour (e.g., exam anxiety before smoking). Maintaining factors: consequences that reinforce and sustain the behaviour (e.g., temporary anxiety relief after smoking). A wrong answer typically confuses antecedents with maintaining factors (e.g., saying "anxiety relief" is an antecedent) or labels the behaviour as a "symptom" rather than a measurable action.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
A client bites nails when waiting for exam results. The therapist asks the client to wear a bitter-tasting nail polish. Identify whether this is an antecedent operation or a consequent operation, and explain the learning principle involved.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
This is an antecedent operation (specifically, an establishing operation that alters the sensory properties of the behaviour before it occurs). The bitter taste acts as a punisher contingent on the behaviour, but because it is applied beforehand to change the stimulus conditions, it is classified as antecedent manipulation. The principle is punishment (positive punishment) — adding an aversive stimulus to reduce the behaviour. A common error is calling it a consequent operation because the bitter taste follows the bite; however, the polish is applied *before* the behaviour, making it antecedent.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Design a token economy for a 14-year-old who refuses to do homework. Specify: (a) target behaviour, (b) token type, (c) backup reinforcers (minimum 3), (d) exchange rate, and (e) one response-cost rule.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
(a) Target: completing homework within 60 minutes of returning from school, with ≥80% accuracy. (b) Tokens: coloured star stickers on a chart. (c) Backup reinforcers: 30 min extra video game time (5 tokens), choice of weekend dessert (10 tokens), trip to bookstore (20 tokens). (d) Exchange rate: 1 token per completed homework session; bonus 2 tokens for 5 consecutive days. (e) Response cost: lose 2 tokens if homework not attempted by 7 PM. A weak answer omits measurable criteria for target behaviour, uses vague reinforcers ("praise"), or sets exchange rates so high the student never accesses backup reinforcers.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare systematic desensitisation and aversive conditioning on: (i) underlying principle, (ii) type of problem each treats, (iii) role of relaxation, (iv) ethical concern unique to each.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
(i) Systematic desensitisation uses reciprocal inhibition (relaxation inhibits anxiety); aversive conditioning uses classical conditioning (pairing unwanted behaviour with aversive stimulus). (ii) Desensitisation treats phobias/anxiety; aversive conditioning treats addictions/habits (alcohol, smoking). (iii) Relaxation is central to desensitisation (hierarchy + relaxation); in aversive conditioning, relaxation is not used — discomfort is intended. (iv) Ethical concern: desensitisation may cause temporary anxiety during hierarchy exposure; aversive conditioning inflicts pain/discomfort (electric shock, nausea) raising consent and human rights issues. A poor answer conflates the principles (e.g., says both use operant conditioning) or misses that relaxation is absent in aversive conditioning.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A therapist uses differential reinforcement of alternative behaviour (DRA) for a child who screams to get attention. The therapist instructs parents to ignore screaming and praise polite requests. Evaluate this plan: identify one strength and one limitation, and suggest a modification to address the limitation.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Strength: combines positive reinforcement for adaptive behaviour (polite request) with extinction for maladaptive behaviour (screaming), reducing side effects of punishment. Limitation: extinction burst — screaming may initially increase in intensity/duration, causing parents to give in, which reinforces worse screaming. Modification: add a brief "wait 10 seconds, then prompt polite request" rule so the child learns the new response before extinction takes full effect, and warn parents about the burst so they persist. A weak evaluation only praises the plan without foreseeing extinction burst or suggests adding punishment (time-out) which contradicts DRA's non-aversive philosophy.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a novel 5-step behavioural analysis and treatment plan for a college student who compulsively checks phone notifications during lectures, missing key content. Use the exact sequence: target behaviour → antecedents → maintaining consequences → antecedent operation → consequent operation.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
1. Target behaviour: unlocking phone and viewing notifications during lecture (count per 50-min class). 2. Antecedents: phone visible on desk, vibration/sound alerts, boredom during dense slides, social anxiety (fear of missing out). 3. Maintaining consequences: immediate relief from FOMO (negative reinforcement), dopamine from social validation (positive reinforcement), avoidance of difficult lecture material (negative reinforcement). 4. Antecedent operation: phone on silent + inside bag under seat (removes visual/tactile cues); pre-lecture 2-min "notification check" scheduled (satiates FOMO). 5. Consequent operation: self-monitoring log — each lecture with ≤1 check earns a point; 10 points = preferred coffee (positive reinforcement); if >3 checks, lose 2 points (response cost). A poor answer lists vague antecedents ("stress"), omits maintaining consequences, or proposes only punishment (e.g., "give phone to friend") without reinforcement for the alternative.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe "antecedent operation" and "consequent operation" refer only to what happens *before* and *after* the behaviour in a temporal sense, leading them to misclassify interventions. For example, they label "giving a reward after homework" as antecedent because the promise was made before, or "removing phone before study" as consequent because the removal follows the decision to study. The correct distinction is functional: antecedent operations alter the *stimulus conditions that evoke the behaviour* (establishing/abolishing operations), while consequent operations alter the *outcome that follows the behaviour* (reinforcement/punishment). This error appears in board exams when students are asked to classify a technique and they rely on chronological order instead of functional role.

## ON_FAILURE
If a student misclassifies operations, follow these steps: (1) Present a clear 2x2 table: rows = antecedent/consequent, columns = increases/decreases behaviour; fill with examples (e.g., antecedent+increase = establishing operation like food deprivation; antecedent+decrease = remove cue; consequent+increase = positive reinforcement; consequent+decrease = punishment). (2) Give 5 mini-scenarios (e.g., "teacher puts up 'quiet' sign before class", "parent takes away toy after hitting") and ask the student to identify the operation type using the functional definition: "Does this change what triggers the behaviour, or what follows it?" (3) Have the student re-do Diagnostic Question 2 and 3, explaining their reasoning aloud. (4) Re-assess with a fresh case: "A student wears noise-cancelling headphones to study better. Is this antecedent or consequent?"

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A 16-year-old, Priya, pulls her hair (trichotillomania) while studying for board exams. She reports an urge when she sees split ends, feels tension before pulling, and relief after. Her parents scold her, which temporarily stops it but she resumes later. Conduct a full behavioural analysis and propose one antecedent and one consequent operation.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Define target behaviour operationally — "pulling out hair strands from scalp/eyebrows, counted per 30-min study session."
- Step 2: Identify antecedents — visual cue (split ends in mirror), tactile cue (running fingers through hair), internal cue (rising tension/anxiety before difficult topic), environmental cue (sitting at study desk with mirror).
- Step 3: Identify maintaining consequences — immediate sensory relief (negative reinforcement: tension reduction), tactile stimulation (positive reinforcement), parental scolding (attention may function as positive reinforcement despite being negative in intent).
- Step 4: Design antecedent operation — remove mirror from study area (abolishing operation for visual cue); wear a soft headband or gloves during study (physical barrier alters tactile cue); schedule 5-min "fidget break" with stress ball every 25 min (competing response).
- Step 5: Design consequent operation — differential reinforcement of other behaviour (DRO): if Priya completes a 30-min session with zero pulls, she earns a token; 5 tokens = choice of weekend activity (positive reinforcement). Response cost: if >2 pulls in a session, lose 1 token (mild punishment). Parents instructed to ignore pulling (extinction of attention) and praise token earnings.
REASONING: The analysis follows the ABC model exactly: target behaviour must be measurable; antecedents include both external stimuli and internal states (tension); maintaining factors explain why behaviour persists despite scolding (attention reinforces). Antecedent operations reduce the evocative power of triggers; consequent operations make adaptive behaviour more rewarding than hair-pulling. The DRO schedule avoids reinforcing the pulling itself while building an alternative repertoire.
ANSWER: Target: hair-pulling frequency per 30-min session. Antecedents: split-end visual, tactile cues, pre-study tension. Maintaining: tension relief (negative reinforcement), tactile stimulation (positive reinforcement), parental attention (positive reinforcement). Antecedent op: remove mirror, wear headband/gloves, scheduled fidget breaks. Consequent op: DRO token system (5 tokens = weekend reward) + response cost for >2 pulls + extinction of parental attention.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A 10-year-old, Arjun, refuses to eat vegetables. He pushes the plate away, cries, and parents eventually give him dal-rice only. He eats that happily. Using behavioural analysis, explain why the parents' strategy maintains the problem and design a treatment using antecedent and consequent operations.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Target behaviour — "pushing plate away and crying when vegetables served" (frequency per meal).
- Step 2: Antecedents — vegetables on plate (visual/olfactory cue), parental instruction "eat your vegetables", hunger level (low if snacked before dinner).
- Step 3: Maintaining consequences — escape from vegetables (negative reinforcement: removal of aversive stimulus), access to preferred dal-rice (positive reinforcement), parental attention during crying (positive reinforcement).
- Step 4: Antecedent operations — (a) serve vegetables when hunger is high (no snacks 2 hrs before dinner — establishing operation increases food reinforcement value); (b) mix vegetables into preferred dal-rice (stimulus fading: reduce aversive properties); (c) offer choice: "carrots or beans?" (increases perceived control).
- Step 5: Consequent operations — (a) differential reinforcement: praise + token for each spoon of vegetable eaten (positive reinforcement); (b) extinction: ignore crying/pushing plate (no attention, no replacement with dal-rice until vegetable portion finished); (c) response cost: if plate pushed away, meal ends for 10 min (loss of eating opportunity).
REASONING: The parents' current response (giving dal-rice after crying) is a textbook negative reinforcement trap: the child's crying removes the vegetables (aversive) and gains preferred food. The analysis shows three maintaining consequences, so treatment must address all. Antecedent operations increase motivation (hunger) and reduce aversion (mixing, choice). Consequent operations flip contingencies: vegetables now produce praise/tokens; crying produces nothing and delays meal.
ANSWER: Problem maintained by escape from vegetables (negative reinforcement), access to preferred food (positive reinforcement), and parental attention (positive reinforcement). Antecedent ops: no pre-dinner snacks, mix veggies into dal-rice, offer choice. Consequent ops: token per spoon eaten, ignore crying, 10-min meal pause if plate pushed.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A 22-year-old engineering student, Vikram, has social anxiety. He avoids group discussions, sits at the back, and drinks alcohol before college events. He wants to participate in a upcoming hackathon team. Design a systematic desensitisation hierarchy (5 steps) and specify the relaxation training and two antecedent operations for the hackathon day.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Teach relaxation — progressive muscular relaxation (PMR) daily 15 min for 1 week until Vikram can achieve deep relaxation on cue (validated by self-rating ≤2/10 tension).
- Step 2: Construct anxiety hierarchy (0-100 SUDS) with Vikram: 1) Imagining reading hackathon announcement (20), 2) Imagining registering online (35), 3) Imagining walking into venue alone (50), 4) Imagining introducing self to one stranger (70), 5) Imagining contributing idea in team discussion (90).
- Step 3: Desensitisation sessions — start at step 1; Vikram visualises scene while relaxed; if SUDS >10, stop visualisation, return to relaxation; repeat until SUDS ≤10 for that step; move to next step next session. Total 8-10 sessions.
- Step 4: Antecedent operation 1 (hackathon day) — pre-event "coping rehearsal": 10-min PMR + mental walkthrough of steps 3-5 with coping self-statements ("I can handle this, I've practised").
- Step 5: Antecedent operation 2 — environmental: arrive early with a known peer (safety signal), choose seat near exit (perceived control), carry water bottle (discrete coping behaviour).
- Step 6: Consequent operation — self-monitoring: after each social interaction, record SUDS and coping used; each successful interaction (≥5 min conversation) earns ₹200 transfer to "rewards fund" for tech gadgets (positive reinforcement).
REASONING: Systematic desensitisation requires three components: relaxation training, hierarchy construction, and graded exposure with relaxation. The hierarchy must be client-generated and graded (20-90 SUDS). Antecedent operations on the target day prepare the physiological state (PMR) and modify the environment (peer, seat) to reduce anxiety triggers. Consequent operation reinforces approach behaviour, not just outcome. This integrates classical conditioning (counterconditioning anxiety with relaxation) and operant conditioning (reinforcing social approach).
ANSWER: PMR training 1 week. Hierarchy: (1) reading announcement 20, (2) registering 35, (3) entering venue 50, (4) introducing self 70, (5) contributing idea 90. Sessions: visualise + relax until SUDS ≤10 per step. Hackathon day antecedents: coping rehearsal (PMR + mental walkthrough), arrive early with peer, seat near exit. Consequent: ₹200 per ≥5-min interaction into rewards fund.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Read the case and answer: Riya, 17, has OCD contamination fears. She washes hands 50+ times daily. Her mother buys expensive scented soaps hoping Riya will "enjoy washing less." Her father locks the bathroom for 1 hour after each wash. Riya now washes at school, avoids touching doorknobs, and her hands are bleeding. (a) Identify the maladaptive behaviour, two antecedents, and two maintaining consequences. (b) Explain why the parents' strategies failed using behavioural terminology. (c) Propose one antecedent operation and one consequent operation a behaviour therapist would use instead.
COMPETENCY: Case-based clinical formulation and treatment planning
STEPS:
- Step 1: Parse case for target behaviour, antecedents, consequences.
- Step 2: Analyse parents' interventions as behavioural operations (what principle do they use? why do they backfire?).
- Step 3: Design evidence-based antecedent and consequent operations for OCD (exposure + response prevention principles).
- Step 4: Justify each proposal with learning theory.
ANSWER: (a) Target: hand-washing frequency (>50/day). Antecedents: tactile sensation of "contamination" (e.g., touching doorknob), intrusive thought "germs on hands". Maintaining consequences: immediate anxiety reduction after washing (negative reinforcement), avoidance of feared outcome (negative reinforcement), parental attention (father's intervention, mother's soaps) may function as positive reinforcement. (b) Mother's scented soaps: adds positive reinforcement (pleasant scent) to the compulsive ritual, strengthening it. Father's bathroom lock: prevents the compulsive response (response prevention) but without prior exposure hierarchy or relaxation — causes panic, displacement to school (generalisation), and self-injury. Both ignore the anxiety-maintaining function. (c) Antecedent operation: graded exposure hierarchy — start with touching "low contamination" object (e.g., own pen) while preventing washing (response prevention); use PMR before exposure. Consequent operation: token economy — each successful exposure (touch object + delay wash 5 min, then 10, then 30) earns token; 10 tokens = chosen reward; self-monitoring log reviewed weekly with therapist.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A school implements a token economy for "on-task behaviour" in Class 9. Tokens: paper stars. Backup reinforcers: 5 stars = 10 min free time, 10 stars = homework pass, 20 stars = canteen voucher. After 3 weeks, on-task behaviour increases in Week 1, plateaus in Week 2, and drops below baseline in Week 3. Students complain stars are "childish." Teachers admit they sometimes forget to give stars, and the principal cancelled canteen vouchers due to budget. Analyse the failure using behavioural principles and redesign the system with three specific fixes.
COMPETENCY: Evaluating and improving applied behavioural programmes
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify which behavioural principles explain the initial increase, plateau, and drop (acquisition, satiation, extinction, reinforcer value).
- Step 2: Pinpoint implementation failures (integrity, schedule, reinforcer quality).
- Step 3: Propose three fixes addressing: (i) reinforcement schedule, (ii) reinforcer age-appropriateness, (iii) system integrity.
- Step 4: Explain how each fix maps to a behavioural principle.
ANSWER: Failure analysis: Week 1 increase = continuous reinforcement (CRF) acquisition. Week 2 plateau = satiation (stars lose value) + thin schedule (teachers forget = variable ratio with long intervals). Week 3 drop = extinction (missed reinforcements) + loss of backup reinforcer (canteen voucher cancelled = reinforcer devaluation). "Childish" complaint = lack of social validity for adolescents. Redesign fixes: (1) Shift to variable-interval schedule with minimum 3 stars/hour guaranteed, plus bonus stars for streaks (maintains responding, resists extinction). (2) Replace paper stars with digital badges on class app + age-appropriate backups: 5 badges = choose seat for week, 10 = "skip one assignment" pass, 20 = group project topic choice (increases reinforcer value, social validity). (3) System integrity: automated badge delivery via teacher app (removes forgetting), weekly audit by student council (accountability), backup reinforcer contract signed by principal (prevents cancellation). Each fix targets a specific behavioural mechanism: schedule thinning resistance, reinforcer potency, and treatment fidelity.
DIFFICULTY: hard
