## DEFINITION
The peasant movements in India refer to collective agrarian struggles by cultivators against exploitative land tenure systems, revenue demands, and feudal oppression. The Tebhaga movement (1946–47) in Bengal was a sharecroppers' demand for two-thirds of the produce instead of half, while the Telangana movement (1946–51) in Hyderabad was a revolutionary armed struggle against feudal landlordism and the Nizam's autocracy. Post-1970s "new farmers' movements" in Punjab, Tamil Nadu, and elsewhere shifted focus to market-oriented demands like remunerative prices, input subsidies, and loan waivers, organizing as non-party, regionally rooted pressure groups.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY

It does NOT cover tribal movements (Santhal, Jharkhand, Northeast), Dalit movements, workers' movements, or environmental movements — each has a separate node. It also does not cover pre-1920s peasant uprisings (e.g., Indigo Revolt, Deccan Riots) except as brief context.

## WALKTHROUGH
Start with a relatable scenario: "Imagine you are a sharecropper in 1946 Bengal — you till the land, sow the seeds, and harvest the crop, but the jotedar (landlord) takes half the produce as rent. You hear neighbors whispering about a movement demanding two-thirds share. Would you join? What risks would you face?" This hooks students into the lived reality behind abstract categories. Then guide them to identify the three movements on a timeline (colonial Bengal, princely Hyderabad, post-Green Revolution Punjab/TN). Next, have them fill a comparison matrix: (a) who participated (sharecroppers vs. peasants vs. market-involved farmers), (b) against whom (jotedars, Nizam/feudal lords, state/market forces), (c) demands (share of produce, land redistribution, price parity), (d) leadership (Kisan Sabha/CPI, CPI, non-party farmer leaders), (e) methods (mass mobilization, armed guerrilla, road/rail blockades). Finally, discuss the conceptual puzzle: why do sociologists call the 1970s movements both "class-based" (old) and "new social movements" (identity/ecology)? This builds analytical depth for board and competitive exams.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Name the two most "classical" peasant movements at the time of Independence as identified in the NCERT textbook, and state the region and year for each.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Correct answer: (1) Tebhaga movement — Bengal (North Bihar region), 1946–47; (2) Telangana movement — princely state of Hyderabad, 1946–51. A wrong answer (e.g., naming Bardoli or Champaran) reveals the student cannot distinguish between pre-independence Gandhian peasant satyagrahas (which were part of the nationalist movement) and the post-war "classical" movements led by Kisan Sabha/CPI that the textbook explicitly labels as the two classical cases at Independence.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain in your own words why the Tebhaga movement is classified as a "sharecroppers' movement" while the Telangana movement is described as a "peasant movement against feudal conditions." What is the key difference in the agrarian structure each confronted?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Correct answer: Tebhaga involved bargadars (sharecroppers) who cultivated land owned by jotedars and demanded a change in the share ratio from 50:50 to 66:33 in their favor — it was a struggle over the terms of tenancy within a zamindari/jotedari system. Telangana involved peasants (including landless laborers) fighting against the entire feudal structure of the Nizam's Hyderabad: forced labor (vetti), illegal exactions, land alienation, and the autocratic state — it aimed at overthrowing feudal relations, not just adjusting shares. A wrong answer that treats both as "peasants vs landlords" misses the structural distinction: Tebhaga was a tenancy dispute in a colonial province; Telangana was a revolutionary challenge to a princely state's feudal order.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A student writes: "The new farmers' movements of the 1970s were led by rich farmers and ignored the problems of small peasants and landless laborers." Using the NCERT description, evaluate this statement. Is it fully accurate, partially accurate, or inaccurate? Justify with two specific points from the text.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Correct evaluation: Partially accurate. The NCERT says these movements involved "farmers rather than peasants (farmers are said to be market-involved as both commodity producers and purchasers)" and were "regionally organised, non-party" with demands on "price and related issues." This suggests a broader base than only rich farmers. However, the text also notes they were "strongly anti-state and anti-urban" and used methods like "blocking roads and railways, refusing politicians' and bureaucrats' entry to villages" — tactics that require organizational capacity often associated with surplus-producing farmers. The statement is partially accurate because the movements did focus on market-oriented demands (procurement prices, input costs) that primarily benefit commercial farmers, but the NCERT does not explicitly say they excluded small peasants; it says they broadened agenda to include environment and women's issues later. A fully "inaccurate" judgment would ignore the class character; a fully "accurate" would ignore the text's nuance.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the role of the Communist Party of India (CPI) in the Tebhaga and Telangana movements. How did the CPI's strategy differ in each, and what does this reveal about the relationship between political parties and peasant movements in the 1940s?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Correct answer: In Tebhaga, the CPI supported the movement through the Kisan Sabha but the movement remained largely a mass mobilization for a specific demand (two-thirds share) within the constitutional framework of a colonial province. In Telangana, the CPI led an armed guerrilla struggle aimed at seizing land and establishing parallel administration, directly challenging the sovereignty of the Nizam's state. The difference reveals that the CPI adapted its strategy to the political context: in British India, it worked through legal mass organizations (Kisan Sabha); in a princely state with no democratic space, it opted for revolutionary armed struggle. This shows peasant movements' strategies are shaped by the political regime they confront — a key analytical point for board exams.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
"New farmers' movements are a continuation of old peasant movements because both are class-based struggles of rural producers." Critique this statement using the NCERT's distinction between "old" and "new" social movements. Provide two arguments for and two against.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Arguments FOR continuity: (1) Both involve rural producers mobilizing collectively against state/market exploitation. (2) Both use disruptive protest methods (bandhs, blockades, non-cooperation). Arguments AGAINST continuity: (1) Old movements (like Tebhaga/Telangana) were led by political parties (CPI/Kisan Sabha) and sought structural redistribution of land/power; new movements are explicitly non-party and focus on price parity, input subsidies, loan waivers — market terms, not land reform. (2) Old movements united poor peasants and landless laborers across caste; new movements are often dominated by dominant castes (Jats, Patidars, Vokkaligas) and initially ignored landless laborers' demands, later broadening to environment/women. The NCERT says: "old social movements clearly saw reorganisation of power relations as a central goal... new social movements were not about changing the distribution of power in society but about quality-of-life issues." This statement oversimplifies by ignoring the shift from power redistribution to market negotiation.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often conflate "peasant" and "farmer" as synonyms and assume all agrarian movements are the same. They write: "Tebhaga and Telangana were farmer movements demanding better prices." This is wrong because: (1) NCERT explicitly distinguishes "peasants" (subsistence cultivators, often tenants/sharecroppers) from "farmers" (market-involved commodity producers/purchasers). (2) Tebhaga/Telangana demanded changes in land relations (share, ownership, feudal dues), not crop prices. (3) Price demands are characteristic of post-1970s new farmers' movements. This error costs marks in board exams (where precise terminology is required) and in competitive exams (where classification questions appear). Students make this error because textbook language in earlier classes uses "peasant" loosely, and media today uses "farmer protests" for all agrarian agitations.

2. Ask them to write one-sentence definitions: "A peasant is... A farmer is..." using the market-involvement criterion.
3. Give them three demand statements: (a) "Give us 2/3 of the crop we grow." (b) "Give us ₹2000/quintal for wheat." (c) "Abolish vetti forced labor." Have them match each to Tebhaga, New Farmers, Telangana.
4. Re-diagnostic: Ask them to explain why the 2020-21 farm laws protests are a "new farmers' movement" and not a "peasant movement" using the market-involvement criterion.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A CUET-style assertion-reason question:
Assertion (A): The new farmers' movements of the 1970s are classified as "new social movements" because they focus on quality-of-life issues like environment and gender.
Reason (R): Unlike old class-based movements, new farmers' movements are non-party, regionally organized, and unite participants across class boundaries.
Choose the correct option: (a) Both A and R true, R explains A; (b) Both true, R does not explain A; (c) A true, R false; (d) A false, R true.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Evaluate Assertion (A). NCERT says: "new social movements were not about changing the distribution of power in society but about quality-of-life issues such as having a clean environment." It also says farmers' movements "broadened their agenda and ideology and include environment and women's issues. Therefore, they can be seen as a part of the worldwide 'new social movements'." So A is TRUE.
- Step 2: Evaluate Reason (R). NCERT says: "These movements were regionally organised, were non-party, and involved farmers rather than peasants... The basic ideology... was strongly anti-state and anti-urban." It also says: "In a social movement, questions of social inequality can occur alongside other, equally important issues... the women's movement includes urban, middle-class feminists as well as poor peasant women." This cross-class unity is a feature of new social movements. So R is TRUE.
- Step 3: Does R explain A? R describes organizational form (non-party, regional) and class composition (cross-class). A describes thematic focus (quality-of-life, environment, gender). The NCERT links them by saying the movements *broadened* agenda to include environment/women *and* can be seen as new social movements. The non-party, cross-class character (R) enables the shift to quality-of-life issues (A) because they are not constrained by party ideology focused solely on class redistribution. So R DOES explain A.
- Step 4: Select option (a).

REASONING: This example trains students to dissect assertion-reason questions by verifying each statement against the text and then checking the causal/explanatory link. The NCERT explicitly connects the organizational features (non-party, regional) and expanded agenda (environment, women) to the classification as new social movements.

ANSWER: (a) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A.
