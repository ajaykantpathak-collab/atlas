## DEFINITION
Substitute goods are products that satisfy a similar want, so a rise in the price of one increases demand for the other; complementary goods are products used together, so a rise in the price of one decreases demand for the other. The relationship is captured by the cross-demand function: Dx = f(Py), where Py is the price of the related good.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
analyze
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can correctly predict the direction of the demand curve shift for a good when given a price change in a related good, justify whether the goods are substitutes or complements using real-world reasoning, and distinguish this shift from a movement along the demand curve caused by the good's own price change.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers the definition, graphical representation (rightward/leftward shift of the demand curve), and real-world identification of substitute and complementary goods. Does NOT cover cross-price elasticity of demand calculations, income effects, or strategic pricing by firms (oligopoly behavior).
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you run a small tea stall near a Delhi Metro station. Your regular customers buy tea and biscuits together every morning. One day, the price of biscuits jumps from ₹10 to ₹15 per pack because of a wheat shortage. Observe what happens: fewer customers buy biscuits, and because they prefer tea with biscuits, they also buy less tea. This shows tea and biscuits are complements — a price rise in one reduces demand for the other. Now suppose the price of coffee (a substitute for tea) rises. Customers switch to your tea, increasing its demand even though your tea price hasn't changed. This is a rightward shift of the tea demand curve. The key principle: a change in the price of a related good shifts the entire demand curve for the original good, unlike a change in its own price which causes movement along the curve.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Define substitute goods and complementary goods in terms of how the price of one good affects the demand for the other.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Substitute goods: an increase in the price of good Y leads to an increase in demand for good X (positive relationship). Complementary goods: an increase in the price of good Y leads to a decrease in demand for good X (negative relationship). A wrong answer (e.g., saying complements move in the same direction) reveals the student confuses the direction of the cross-price effect, likely because they think "used together" means "demand moves together" without considering the price mechanism.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If the price of petrol increases significantly, what happens to the demand for cars? Explain whether cars and petrol are substitutes or complements, and describe the shift of the car demand curve.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Cars and petrol are complements. A rise in petrol price increases the cost of using a car, so demand for cars decreases — the demand curve for cars shifts leftward. A wrong answer (e.g., demand for cars increases or curve shifts right) reveals the student misidentifies the relationship as substitutes, perhaps thinking people buy more cars when petrol is expensive to "save fuel," ignoring that higher running costs reduce overall car demand.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
The price of Samsung smartphones falls from ₹25,000 to ₹20,000. Using a demand curve diagram for Xiaomi smartphones, show and explain the effect on the demand for Xiaomi phones. Assume the two brands are close substitutes. Initial demand for Xiaomi at its own price of ₹18,000 is 1,000 units per month. After the Samsung price cut, demand for Xiaomi at the same ₹18,000 falls to 700 units.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Identify Samsung and Xiaomi as substitutes. Step 2: Samsung price falls → Samsung becomes relatively cheaper. Step 3: Consumers switch from Xiaomi to Samsung. Step 4: At every price of Xiaomi (including ₹18,000), quantity demanded decreases — demand curve shifts leftward. Step 5: The shift is from D1 (1,000 units at ₹18,000) to D2 (700 units at ₹18,000). A wrong answer showing a rightward shift or movement along the curve reveals confusion between substitute/complement logic or between shift vs. movement.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare two scenarios: (A) Price of milk rises → demand for cornflakes falls. (B) Price of tea rises → demand for coffee rises. Identify the relationship in each case. Then analyze: if the price of cornflakes itself falls, how does that affect the demand for milk differently than scenario A?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Scenario A: milk and cornflakes are complements (price of milk up → demand for cornflakes down). Scenario B: tea and coffee are substitutes (price of tea up → demand for coffee up). If cornflakes' own price falls, quantity demanded of cornflakes increases (movement along its demand curve), which *increases* demand for milk (rightward shift of milk's demand curve) because they are complements. This is the opposite direction of scenario A where milk's price rose. A wrong answer that says cornflakes' price fall reduces milk demand reveals confusion between own-price effect (movement along) and cross-price effect (shift).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A government imposes a high tax on sugary aerated drinks, raising their price by 20%. Health experts argue this will increase demand for fresh fruit juices. Evaluate this claim using substitute/complement logic. Consider whether the policy might have unintended effects on complementary goods.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The claim is valid only if aerated drinks and fruit juices are substitutes — a price rise in aerated drinks should shift demand for juices rightward. However, if consumers view them as distinct categories (e.g., aerated drinks for parties, juices for health), the cross-effect may be weak. Unintended effect: if aerated drinks are often consumed with fast food (complements), higher aerated drink prices could reduce fast food demand, hurting that industry. A wrong answer that ignores complementarity with fast food or assumes perfect substitution reveals incomplete analysis of market interdependencies.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a real-life example from your neighborhood involving three goods: one pair of substitutes and one pair of complements. Draw a rough demand curve diagram for one good showing the shift when the price of its related good changes. Label axes, curves, and shift direction.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Example: Substitutes — bus travel and metro travel (price of bus fare up → metro demand up). Complements — printers and ink cartridges (price of printers down → ink demand up). Diagram for ink cartridges: vertical axis = price of ink, horizontal axis = quantity of ink. Initial demand D1. Printer price falls → demand for printers rises → demand for ink shifts right to D2. A poor answer uses vague goods (e.g., "food and water") or draws a movement along the curve instead of a shift, showing inability to apply the concept independently.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think that if two goods are complements, a rise in the price of one *increases* demand for the other because "they go together, so you need more of the other." For example, they argue: "If petrol becomes expensive, people will buy more cars to stock up before prices rise further." This confuses the *usage* relationship with the *demand* response. The correct logic: higher petrol price raises the total cost of owning/operating a car, making car ownership less attractive, so car demand falls.
## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Use the "total cost of usage" framework. For complements (car + petrol), calculate total monthly cost = (car EMI) + (petrol cost). Show with numbers: if petrol cost rises from ₹3,000 to ₹4,000, total cost rises, so fewer people can afford the bundle → car demand falls. Step 2: Contrast with substitutes (tea vs coffee): if coffee price rises, tea becomes relatively cheaper *without* changing tea's own price → tea demand rises. Step 3: Practice with a table: list 5 pairs, identify relationship, predict shift direction. Step 4: Draw side-by-side diagrams for own-price change (movement along) vs related-price change (shift) to cement the visual difference.

## THIS_FORMULA
Dx = f(Py) where Dx is demand for good X, Py is price of related good Y. For substitutes: ∂Dx/∂Py > 0. For complements: ∂Dx/∂Py < 0.
## FORMULA_VARIABLES
Dx = quantity demanded of good X (units per period), Py = price of related good Y (rupees per unit).
## FORMULA_UNITS
Dx in units (e.g., smartphones per month), Py in rupees (₹ per unit).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: In a Mumbai housing society, the price of LPG cylinders (subsidized) increases from ₹900 to ₹1,100. Induction stoves are a substitute for LPG for cooking. The demand curve for induction stoves is initially D1: at price ₹3,000, quantity demanded is 50 units/month. After the LPG price hike, at the same induction stove price of ₹3,000, quantity demanded rises to 80 units/month. Analyze the effect.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the goods: LPG and induction stoves are substitutes (both used for cooking).
- Step 2: Identify the change: Price of LPG (related good) rises by ₹200. Own price of induction stoves unchanged at ₹3,000.
- Step 3: Apply substitute logic: Higher LPG price makes LPG cooking relatively more expensive → consumers switch to induction stoves.
- Step 4: Determine shift direction: Demand for induction stoves increases at every price → rightward shift of demand curve from D1 to D2.
- Step 5: Quantify: At ₹3,000, quantity demanded increases from 50 to 80 units. The shift magnitude is 30 units at this price.
- Step 6: Interpret economically: The cross-price effect is positive. Society's total cooking cost may rise or fall depending on electricity tariffs, but the substitution effect dominates in the short run.
REASONING: The price of a substitute good (LPG) rose, making the good in question (induction stove) relatively more attractive. Since the induction stove's own price did not change, this is a pure shift of the demand curve, not a movement along it. The rightward shift indicates higher demand at each possible price of induction stoves.
ANSWER: Demand curve for induction stoves shifts rightward from D1 to D2. At ₹3,000, quantity demanded increases from 50 to 80 units/month.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A popular coffee chain "BrewHub" sells coffee at ₹150/cup and croissants at ₹80 each. They are complements. Due to a coffee bean crop failure, BrewHub raises coffee price to ₹200/cup. Croissant price stays ₹80. Initially, at ₹80, croissant demand is 200 units/day. After coffee price hike, croissant demand at ₹80 falls to 120 units/day. Show the effect on the croissant market.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify relationship: Coffee and croissants are complements (typically consumed together).
- Step 2: Identify the shock: Price of coffee (complement) rises by ₹50. Own price of croissants unchanged at ₹80.
- Step 3: Apply complement logic: Higher coffee price increases the total cost of the "coffee + croissant" bundle → fewer consumers buy the bundle → demand for croissants falls.
- Step 4: Determine shift: Demand curve for croissants shifts leftward from D1 to D2.
- Step 5: Quantify shift: At ₹80, quantity demanded drops from 200 to 120 units/day (a decrease of 80 units).
- Step 6: Business implication: BrewHub's croissant revenue falls from ₹16,000/day (200×80) to ₹9,600/day (120×80), a loss of ₹6,400/day, even though croissant price didn't change.
REASONING: Complementary goods have a negative cross-price relationship. The increase in the price of coffee reduces the demand for the joint consumption bundle, shifting the entire demand curve for croissants leftward. This is a classic example of derived demand — demand for croissants is derived from demand for the coffee-croissant combo.
ANSWER: Croissant demand curve shifts leftward. At ₹80, quantity demanded falls from 200 to 120 units/day. Daily croissant revenue falls by ₹6,400.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Combine with prior concept (Determinants of Demand - Income). In a tier-2 city, demand for two-wheelers (bikes) is affected by: (a) price of petrol (complement), (b) price of electric scooters (substitute), (c) consumer income. Initially, bike demand at ₹80,000 is 1,000 units/month. Petrol price rises ₹10/litre. Electric scooter price falls ₹5,000. Average income rises 5%. Bikes are normal goods. Predict the net shift direction of the bike demand curve and explain which effect dominates if the income effect is +150 units, petrol effect is -200 units, and e-scooter effect is -100 units.
STEPS:
- Step 1: List all three determinants acting simultaneously: price of complement (petrol), price of substitute (e-scooter), income.
- Step 2: Determine individual shift directions: Petrol price ↑ → bike demand ↓ (leftward). E-scooter price ↓ → bike demand ↓ (leftward). Income ↑ (normal good) → bike demand ↑ (rightward).
- Step 3: Use given magnitudes: Petrol effect = -200 units (at ₹80,000). E-scooter effect = -100 units. Income effect = +150 units.
- Step 4: Calculate net shift: -200 -100 +150 = -150 units. Net leftward shift of 150 units at the given price.
- Step 5: New quantity demanded at ₹80,000 = 1,000 - 150 = 850 units/month.
- Step 6: Dominance: The combined negative cross-price effects (-300) outweigh the positive income effect (+150). Petrol complement effect is the single largest driver.
- Step 7: Policy insight: If government wants to support bike industry, reducing petrol tax (complement) is more impactful than income subsidies, given these magnitudes.
REASONING: This example integrates multiple non-price determinants taught in c9_eco_ch2_4 and c9_eco_ch2_6. The demand curve shifts are additive (horizontal summation of individual shifts). The net effect is the algebraic sum. Students must recognize that cross-price effects can be larger than income effects for certain goods, especially when the complementary good (petrol) is a significant recurring cost.
ANSWER: Net leftward shift of 150 units. New demand at ₹80,000 is 850 units/month. Negative cross-price effects dominate positive income effect.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: During the 2022 festive season, an e-commerce platform offered a 30% discount on smartphones. At the same time, the price of mobile data plans (4G/5G) remained unchanged. However, the platform observed a 25% increase in the sale of mobile data recharge vouchers. Using substitute/complement logic, explain this observation. Is the relationship between smartphones and data plans substitutes or complements? What would happen to data voucher demand if data plan prices *also* fell by 20%?
COMPETENCY: Analyzing market relationships
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the two goods: smartphones and mobile data plans.
- Step 2: Observe the change: Smartphone price fell (discount). Data plan price unchanged initially.
- Step 3: Observe the outcome: Data voucher demand increased.
- Step 4: Infer relationship: Lower smartphone price → more smartphones bought → more need for data → data demand rises. This is a complement relationship (negative cross-price elasticity).
- Step 5: Predict combined effect: If data plan prices also fall, two effects reinforce: (a) more smartphones bought (complement effect), (b) data becomes cheaper (own-price effect, law of demand). Both shift/increase data voucher demand further.
ANSWER: Smartphones and data plans are complements. The smartphone discount increased smartphone purchases, which increased the need for data (complement effect), shifting data voucher demand rightward. If data plan prices also fall, data voucher demand increases due to both the complement-driven shift and the own-price movement along the new demand curve.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: The government is considering two policies to reduce petrol consumption: (Policy A) Increase petrol tax by ₹10/litre. (Policy B) Subsidize electric vehicles (EVs) by ₹50,000 per vehicle. Assume petrol and EVs are substitutes in the long run, but petrol cars and petrol are complements. Analyze the impact of each policy on: (i) demand for petrol, (ii) demand for petrol cars, (iii) demand for EVs. Which policy is more effective for reducing petrol consumption in the short run vs long run?
COMPETENCY: Evaluating policy impacts
STEPS:
- Step 1: Map relationships: Petrol & petrol cars = complements. Petrol & EVs = substitutes (long run). EVs & petrol cars = substitutes.
- Step 2: Analyze Policy A (petrol tax ↑): Petrol price ↑ → (i) petrol demand ↓ (law of demand, movement along). (ii) Petrol car demand ↓ (complement effect, leftward shift). (iii) EV demand ↑ (substitute effect, rightward shift).
- Step 3: Analyze Policy B (EV subsidy → EV price ↓): EV price ↓ → (iii) EV demand ↑ (law of demand, movement along). (i) Petrol demand ↓ (substitute effect, leftward shift). (ii) Petrol car demand ↓ (substitute effect via EVs, leftward shift).
- Step 4: Compare short run: Policy A directly raises petrol price → immediate reduction in petrol consumption (movement along). Policy B takes time for EV adoption to grow (shift). Policy A more effective short run.
- Step 5: Compare long run: Policy B accelerates EV stock accumulation, permanently shifting demand away from petrol. Policy A's effect may diminish if demand is inelastic. Policy B more effective long run.
- Step 6: Synthesize: Both reduce petrol demand, but through different mechanisms and timeframes.
ANSWER: Policy A (petrol tax) reduces petrol demand directly via own-price effect and indirectly via complement (petrol cars) and substitute (EVs) shifts. Most effective short run. Policy B (EV subsidy) reduces petrol demand via substitute shift (EVs replace petrol cars) and own-price effect on EVs. More effective long run as EV fleet grows. Both reduce petrol car demand. A combined approach is optimal.
DIFFICULTY: hard
