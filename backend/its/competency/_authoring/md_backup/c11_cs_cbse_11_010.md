## DEFINITION
Cyber safety refers to the practices, measures, and awareness required to protect individuals, systems, and networks from cyber threats such as unauthorised access, data theft, malware, phishing, and identity theft, while ensuring ethical, legal, and responsible use of digital technologies.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to classify a given online scenario into the correct cyber threat category (hacking, phishing, identity theft, ransomware, cyber bullying, plagiarism, copyright infringement) and justify the classification using at least two specific indicators from the scenario. The student must also be able to recommend two appropriate preventive measures citing relevant sections of the IT Act 2000 (amended 2008).

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers foundational concepts of cyber safety: digital footprints (active/passive), netizen etiquettes (net, communication, social media), intellectual property rights (copyright, patent, trademark), violations (plagiarism, copyright/trademark infringement), open source licensing (CC, GPL, FOSS), major cyber crimes (hacking, phishing, identity theft, ransomware), the Indian IT Act 2000 framework, and health/ergonomic impacts. It does NOT cover technical implementation of firewalls, antivirus mechanisms, encryption algorithms, or advanced forensic investigation techniques (covered in subsequent nodes).

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you receive an email claiming to be from your bank asking you to "verify your account" by clicking a link and entering your PIN. The logo looks perfect, the language is professional, but the sender address is "security@bank-verification-services.xyz" instead of your bank's actual domain. This is a classic phishing attempt — but how do you prove it? Start by examining the URL structure: legitimate banks use their primary domain (e.g., hdfcbank.com), not subdomains on unrelated domains. Next, check for HTTPS and valid security certificates. Then, recall that no legitimate bank ever asks for PIN via email. This scenario connects your prior knowledge of URLs (c11_cs_cbse_11_007) and HTTPS (c11_cs_cbse_11_009) to cyber safety. The key insight: cyber threats exploit trust and urgency; defence requires verifying technical indicators (domain, protocol, certificate) before taking action.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Define "digital footprint" and distinguish between active and passive digital footprints with one example each.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
A digital footprint is the trail of data created by a user's online activities. Active digital footprint: data intentionally submitted (e.g., posting a photo on Instagram). Passive digital footprint: data collected without explicit user action (e.g., website recording IP address and browsing history via cookies). A wrong answer that confuses the two (e.g., calling cookies "active" because user "chose" to visit the site) reveals misunderstanding of intentionality — passive footprints are generated by system-level tracking, not user consent.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
A student downloads a 2-minute educational video from YouTube, adds a title slide "Prepared by [Student Name]", and submits it as their own project work. Is this plagiarism, copyright infringement, both, or neither? Explain using the definitions from the NCERT textbook.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
This is both plagiarism and copyright infringement. Plagiarism: presenting someone else's work (the video content) as one's own by adding a false attribution. Copyright infringement: using the video without permission from the copyright holder (YouTube's Terms of Service and the creator's rights), even if modified. A wrong answer claiming "fair use" or "only plagiarism" reveals confusion between ethical attribution (plagiarism) and legal permission (copyright) — the NCERT explicitly states that giving reference does not make copyrighted material free to use.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Your friend receives an SMS: "Your ATM card ending in 4567 will be blocked. Update KYC immediately: http://sbi-kyc-update.tk". They are about to click the link. List three specific technical indicators in this message that confirm it is a phishing attempt, and state the immediate action they should take.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Three indicators: (1) URL uses .tk (free Tokelau domain) instead of sbi.co.in or onlinesbi.com; (2) URL path "kyc-update" is not a standard SBI endpoint; (3) SMS creates urgency ("immediately", "will be blocked") — a social engineering tactic. Immediate action: Do not click; delete the SMS; report to bank's official helpline (not the number in SMS) and forward to 1930 (India's cyber crime helpline). A wrong answer focusing only on "spelling errors" or "unknown sender" misses the technical domain analysis that is definitive proof.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare ethical hacking and non-ethical hacking across four dimensions: intent, authorisation, outcome, and legal status. Provide one real-world example of each.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Ethical hacking: intent = improve security; authorisation = explicit written permission; outcome = vulnerabilities reported and fixed; legal = legal under IT Act. Example: Company hires certified ethical hacker (CEH) to penetration-test their web app. Non-ethical hacking: intent = steal data/disrupt services; authorisation = none; outcome = data breach, financial loss; legal = criminal offence under IT Act Sections 43, 66. Example: Attacker exploits SQL injection to steal customer database. A wrong answer that equates "technical skill" with ethics reveals the misconception that hacking is defined by technique rather than authorisation and intent.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate the statement: "Using a strong password is sufficient to protect your social media account from being compromised." Support your evaluation with at least three distinct attack vectors that bypass password strength.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is false. Three attack vectors bypassing password strength: (1) Phishing: user voluntarily enters password on fake login page; (2) Session hijacking: attacker steals session cookie after legitimate login; (3) SIM swapping: attacker ports victim's phone number to intercept 2FA codes; (4) Keylogger malware: records keystrokes including strong password; (5) Credential stuffing: reused password from another breached site. A wrong answer claiming "strong password prevents all attacks" reveals the misconception that authentication security is single-factor; defence-in-depth (2FA, device trust, phishing awareness) is essential.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a "Digital Safety Checklist" for a Class 11 student that includes: (a) 3 daily habits, (b) 3 weekly habits, (c) 3 monthly habits, and (d) 1 emergency response step for each: phishing email, ransomware infection, and cyber bullying incident. Justify each item with a specific threat it mitigates.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Sample answer: Daily: (1) Verify sender before clicking links (mitigates phishing); (2) Log out of shared devices (mitigates session hijacking); (3) Avoid posting location in real-time (mitigates stalking/physical threat). Weekly: (1) Scan for malware (mitigates ransomware/trojans); (2) Review app permissions (mitigates data leakage); (3) Backup important files offline (mitigates ransomware data loss). Monthly: (1) Change critical passwords (mitigates credential stuffing); (2) Check privacy settings on social media (mitigates oversharing); (3) Update OS and apps (mitigates exploit of known vulnerabilities). Emergency: Phishing — report to 1930, change passwords, enable 2FA; Ransomware — disconnect from network, do not pay, restore from backup, report to cyber cell; Cyber bullying — screenshot evidence, block/report user, inform trusted adult/school, file complaint under IT Act Section 66A/67. A poor answer with generic advice ("be careful") reveals lack of threat-specific procedural knowledge.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students believe that "deleting a post or photo from social media permanently removes it from the internet." This misconception arises because the UI shows the content disappearing from their profile, creating a false sense of control. In reality, the NCERT explicitly states: "once a data trail is generated, even if we later try to erase data about our online activities, the digital footprints still remain" — copies exist in server backups, CDN caches, other users' screenshots/shares, and platform archives. For example, in NCERT Exercise Q8, Preeti deletes her birthday photos but Himanshu had already downloaded/forwarded them — the "deleted" images persist in Gayatri's chat. Students confuse "UI removal" with "data deletion," not understanding that digital data is replicated across multiple systems instantly upon upload.

## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Demonstrate the "View Page Source" and "Inspect Element" browser tools to show that deleted content may still exist in DOM or cached versions. Step 2: Use the Wayback Machine (archive.org) to show historical snapshots of a familiar website — prove deletion is not retroactive. Step 3: Walk through NCERT Exercise Q8 (Preeti-Himanshu-Gayatri scenario) as a case study: trace the data flow from upload → server → Himanshu's device → forward → Gayatri's device → Preeti's deletion only affects her profile view, not the copies. Step 4: Assign a practical: "Find a deleted tweet/post of a public figure that still exists in screenshots/news articles" to cement that replication, not storage, is the core issue.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A Class 11 student, Arjun, receives an email claiming to be from "CBSE Board" with subject "URGENT: Your Class 11 Registration Incomplete". The email contains a link: "http://cbse-registration-verification.ml/form.php". The email body uses the CBSE logo and formal language. Arjun clicks the link, sees a page identical to cbse.gov.in, and enters his roll number, date of birth, and Aadhaar number. Analyse this scenario step-by-step to identify the cyber crime, the technical indicators of the attack, and the specific data compromised.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the cyber crime category — this is phishing (fraudulent email impersonating authority) combined with identity theft (collecting Aadhaar, DOB, roll number for impersonation).
- Step 2: Analyse the URL technical indicators — (a) TLD ".ml" (Mali) is a free/cheap domain favoured by attackers, not ".gov.in"; (b) Subdomain "cbse-registration-verification" mimics legitimate branding but is controlled by attacker; (c) Protocol is HTTP, not HTTPS — no encryption, no certificate validation; (d) Path "/form.php" suggests generic PHP form handler, not CBSE's actual application structure.
- Step 3: Analyse the social engineering indicators — (a) Urgency ("URGENT", "Incomplete") pressures bypassing verification; (b) Authority impersonation (CBSE Board) exploits trust; (c) Logo/form cloning creates visual legitimacy.
- Step 4: Identify data compromised — Roll number + DOB + Aadhaar = complete identity triplet for: (a) Academic fraud (fake certificates); (b) Government service impersonation (Aadhaar-linked services); (c) Financial fraud (KYC bypass using Aadhaar).
- Step 5: Map to IT Act provisions — Section 66C (identity theft), Section 66D (cheating by personation using computer resource), Section 43 (unauthorised access/download of data).
- Step 6: Immediate remediation — (a) Report to 1930 (cyber helpline) and cybercrime.gov.in; (b) Lock Aadhaar biometrics via UIDAI portal; (c) Monitor credit report for unauthorised loans; (d) Change passwords on all accounts using same credentials.

REASONING: This example integrates URL analysis (from c11_cs_cbse_11_007), protocol security (c11_cs_cbse_11_009), social engineering psychology, data sensitivity classification (Aadhaar = biometric/financial KYC key), and legal framework (IT Act). The multi-step reasoning mirrors board exam case-study questions where students must identify crime, evidence, impact, and response.

ANSWER: Cyber crime: Phishing + Identity Theft. Technical indicators: .ml TLD, HTTP not HTTPS, deceptive subdomain, generic PHP path. Data compromised: Roll number, DOB, Aadhaar (full identity triplet). IT Act sections: 66C, 66D, 43. Remediation: Report to 1930, lock Aadhaar biometrics, monitor credit, rotate credentials.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Priya downloads a research paper PDF from a university repository for her Class 11 project on "AI in Healthcare". The paper has a Creative Commons BY-NC-SA 4.0 license. She: (1) includes two paragraphs verbatim in her project with citation; (2) creates a summary infographic using the paper's data and posts it on her public Instagram; (3) prints 50 copies of the infographic to distribute at a school science fair. For each action, determine if it complies with the CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 license. Justify using the license terms: BY (attribution), NC (non-commercial), SA (share-alike).

STEPS:
- Step 1: Decode CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 terms — BY = must give appropriate credit; NC = cannot use for commercial purposes; SA = derivatives must be shared under same license.
- Step 2: Analyse Action 1 (verbatim paragraphs with citation) — Compliant? YES. Verbatim copying is allowed under CC BY if attribution is given. Citation satisfies BY. NC not violated (school project = non-commercial). SA not triggered (no derivative created).
- Step 3: Analyse Action 2 (infographic posted on public Instagram) — Compliant? CONDITIONAL. Infographic is a derivative work (SA triggered) — must license infographic under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. Instagram posting is non-commercial (NC satisfied) IF Priya doesn't monetise. BY requires attribution on the infographic itself. If she posts without CC license notice and attribution → VIOLATION of SA and BY.
- Step 4: Analyse Action 3 (50 printed copies for science fair) — Compliant? CONDITIONAL. Distribution of derivative (infographic) requires SA compliance (same license on prints). Science fair is non-commercial (NC satisfied). BY requires attribution on each copy. If prints lack license notice/attribution → VIOLATION.
- Step 5: Synthesise compliance checklist for Priya — (a) Add "CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 | Source: [Paper Citation]" on infographic; (b) Include same notice on printed copies; (c) Ensure no monetisation (no ads, no sponsored posts); (d) Verify original paper's CC version matches (4.0).
- Step 6: Contrast with copyright infringement — If paper had "All Rights Reserved" (standard copyright), ALL three actions would require explicit permission. CC license pre-grants permissions conditionally.

REASONING: This example tests understanding of open licensing (CC/GPL from NCERT 11.4.3) versus traditional copyright, derivative work obligations (SA), and commercial/non-commercial boundary — a frequent board exam application question. It connects IPR theory to practical student scenarios.

ANSWER: Action 1: Compliant (BY satisfied, NC/SA not triggered). Action 2: Non-compliant unless infographic carries CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 notice + attribution. Action 3: Non-compliant unless each print carries CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 notice + attribution. Key insight: SA requires derivative works to inherit the same license; BY requires attribution on every copy/format.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A school's Wi-Fi network (SSID: "School_WiFi") uses WPA2-PSK with password "School@2024". Students complain of slow internet. The network admin discovers: (1) 40% of connected devices are unknown MAC addresses; (2) bandwidth usage spikes at 2 AM; (3) DNS logs show requests to known malware C2 domains. The admin suspects a neighbour cracked the Wi-Fi password and is using the network for botnet activity. Design a 6-step incident response plan incorporating technical, procedural, and legal actions, referencing specific IT Act sections and network concepts from prior nodes.

STEPS:
- Step 1: CONTAINMENT — Immediately change Wi-Fi password to 16+ character random string (entropy > 100 bits). Enable MAC filtering for known student devices (whitelist). Disable WPS (vulnerable to brute-force). This stops unauthorised access at Layer 2 (data link).
- Step 2: IDENTIFICATION — Analyse router logs (DHCP leases, firewall logs) to identify: (a) Rogue device MAC addresses; (b) Timestamp of first unauthorised connection; (c) Data volume per device. Cross-reference with DNS logs to confirm C2 beaconing (periodic requests to malicious domains).
- Step 3: EVIDENCE PRESERVATION — Create forensic image of router logs (dd command or vendor backup). Hash logs with SHA-256 for integrity. Document chain of custody. This satisfies IT Act Section 65 (tampering with computer source documents) evidentiary requirements.
- Step 4: ERADICATION — Factory reset router, update firmware to latest (patch KRACK/other WPA2 vulns). Reconfigure with WPA3-SAE if hardware supports. Deploy network segmentation: VLAN for students, separate VLAN for admin/IoT.
- Step 5: RECOVERY & LEGAL — File FIR at local cyber cell under IT Act Section 43 (unauthorised access), Section 66 (computer-related offences), Section 66F (cyber terrorism if critical infrastructure affected). Provide logs as evidence. Notify ISP to block C2 domains at upstream.
- Step 6: PREVENTION & AWARENESS — Implement: (a) RADIUS/802.1X authentication (per-user credentials, not shared PSK); (b) Captive portal with AUP acceptance; (c) Monthly student workshops on "Why sharing Wi-Fi password = identity theft risk" (connects to NCERT cyber bullying/identity theft); (d) Automated DNS filtering (block known malware domains via RPZ).

REASONING: This advanced example synthesises network security (WPA2/3, MAC filtering, VLAN, RADIUS — building on c11_cs_cbse_11_004, 11_005, 11_006), incident response lifecycle (NIST SP 800-61), digital forensics (hashing, chain of custody), and IT Act legal mapping. It prepares students for competency questions requiring multi-domain integration — exactly the board exam pattern for 5-6 mark case studies.

ANSWER: 6-step plan: (1) Contain via password rotation + MAC whitelist + WPS disable; (2) Identify rogue devices via DHCP/DNS log correlation; (3) Preserve evidence with SHA-256 hashing + chain of custody; (4) Eradicate via firmware update + WPA3 + VLAN segmentation; (5) Legal: FIR under IT Act 43/66/66F + ISP notification; (6) Prevent: 802.1X + captive portal + DNS filtering + student awareness.
