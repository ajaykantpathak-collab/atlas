## DEFINITION
Promotion of a company is the first stage in company formation where a person or group conceives a business opportunity, analyses its technical, financial, and economic feasibility, assembles resources (men, materials, machinery, managerial ability, finance), and undertakes all preliminary steps—including name approval, fixing signatories to the Memorandum, appointing professionals, and preparing registration documents—to bring a company into legal existence.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to: (1) Define promotion and identify it as the first of three stages in company formation; (2) List and explain all six steps of promotion in correct sequence with their legal requirements; (3) Distinguish a promoter from an entrepreneur, director, or professional advisor using Section 69 criteria; (4) Explain why preliminary contracts do not bind the company but bind the promoter personally.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers: meaning of promotion, six sequential steps (name approval → fixing MoA signatories → appointing professionals → preparing MoA/AoA/consent documents → statutory declaration → filing with ROC), functions of promoter (opportunity identification, feasibility studies, resource assembly), legal position of promoters (fiduciary duty, no secret profits, personal liability for pre-incorporation contracts), and preliminary vs provisional contracts. It does NOT cover: incorporation procedure (node 003), MoA/AoA clause details (nodes 004, 005), certificate of incorporation effects (node 006), capital subscription (node 007), or commencement of business (node 008).

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine Avtar, the automobile engineer from your textbook, who invented a carburettor reducing petrol consumption by 40%. He needs ₹50 crore to manufacture it—too large for a sole proprietorship or partnership. He decides to form a company. Where does he start? He doesn't walk into the Registrar's office tomorrow. First, he must verify if the idea is technically possible (is the special metal available?), financially viable (can he raise ₹50 crore?), and economically profitable (will cars buy it?). Only after positive feasibility studies does he apply for a name, decide who signs the MoA, hire a Company Secretary and Chartered Accountant, and draft documents. This entire pre-registration journey—from idea to ROC filing—is promotion. The key insight: promotion is not merely "starting a business"; it is a legally defined phase with specific steps, and the promoter carries personal liability until incorporation.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Define "promotion of a company" as per the Companies Act, 2013 framework. State the exact stage it occupies in the formation process.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Promotion is the first stage of company formation involving conceiving a business opportunity, conducting feasibility studies (technical, financial, economic), assembling resources, and completing pre-registration formalities (name approval, MoA signatories, professional appointments, document preparation) to incorporate a company. A wrong answer—such as "promotion means advertising the company" or "promotion starts after incorporation"—reveals confusion between business marketing and legal formation stages, or misunderstanding that promotion precedes legal existence.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Avtar has a brilliant carburettor design but skips the feasibility study and directly applies for name approval. His application is rejected because the required rare-earth metal is unavailable in India and import is banned. Which promotion step did he violate, and why is that step legally critical before name approval?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Avtar violated Step 2: Feasibility Studies (technical feasibility). The NCERT explicitly states promoters must undertake detailed feasibility studies—technical, financial, economic—with help of specialists before deciding to launch a company. Name approval (Step 3) comes only after "having decided to incorporate a company," which presumes positive feasibility results. A wrong answer suggesting name approval comes first or that feasibility is optional reveals the misconception that promotion is merely paperwork rather than a substantive investigation phase.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A Chartered Accountant, Ms. Rao, helps Avtar prepare financial projections, draft the MoA, and file incorporation documents. She charges professional fees. Is Ms. Rao a promoter under Section 69 of the Companies Act, 2013? Justify using the exact statutory exclusion.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
No, Ms. Rao is not a promoter. Section 69(c) explicitly excludes "a person who is acting merely in a professional capacity" from the definition of promoter. The CA assists in preparation of documents and feasibility studies but does not conceive the business idea, control the company's affairs, or direct the Board. A wrong answer claiming she is a promoter because she "helps form the company" reveals failure to apply the statutory professional-capacity exclusion—a classic board exam trap.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the legal position of a promoter with that of a director and an entrepreneur using three distinct criteria: (a) existence of company, (b) fiduciary duty, (c) personal liability for pre-incorporation contracts.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
| Criterion | Promoter | Director | Entrepreneur |
|-----------|----------|----------|--------------|
| (a) Company exists? | No (pre-incorporation) | Yes (post-incorporation) | May or may not |
| (b) Fiduciary duty? | Yes, to proposed company | Yes, to existing company | No statutory duty |
| (c) Liability for pre-incorporation contracts? | Personally liable unless ratified | Not applicable (company exists) | Personally liable if sole proprietor |

A wrong answer equating promoter with director (e.g., "promoter becomes director automatically") reveals confusion between promotion phase and post-incorporation governance. The promoter's fiduciary duty arises before the company exists—a unique legal position.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Avtar signs a contract with SteelCo for 10 tonnes of special alloy at ₹2 crore during promotion. The company is incorporated six months later. The Board refuses to ratify the contract. SteelCo sues the company. Who is liable, and why? Evaluate the company's defence that "the contract was signed before incorporation."

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Avtar (promoter) is personally liable; the company is not liable. Preliminary contracts (pre-incorporation contracts) are not binding on the company—even after incorporation—because the company lacked legal personality at the time of contracting. The company cannot ratify a preliminary contract; it can only enter a fresh contract. The company's defence succeeds: the contract is void ab initio against the company. A wrong answer holding the company liable reveals misunderstanding that incorporation validates prior acts—the certificate of incorporation is conclusive evidence of legal birth from its date, not retroactive validation of promoter's acts.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a 6-step promotion plan for a startup proposing to manufacture biodegradable packaging from agricultural waste in Punjab. For each step, specify: (i) the action, (ii) the legal requirement from Companies Act, 2013, (iii) the professional needed, and (iv) the risk if skipped.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A strong answer will map all six NCERT steps to the specific context:
1. Opportunity identification → waste availability study → agricultural expert → risk: raw material shortage
2. Feasibility studies (technical: pulping tech; financial: ₹30 cr capex; economic: margin vs plastic) → engineers, CAs → risk: unviable project
3. Name approval (INC-1, 3 names) → ROC Punjab → CS → risk: rejection, delay
4. Fix MoA signatories (min 7 for public, 2 for private) → first directors, qualification shares → CA/CS → risk: invalid MoA
5. Appoint professionals (merchant banker, auditor, CS) → statutory compliance → risk: defective filings
6. Prepare documents (MoA, AoA, consent, declaration, fee) → Schedule I tables, Section 7 → CS/advocate → risk: registration refusal
A poor answer listing generic steps without legal specifics or context reveals inability to apply the framework to a novel scenario.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students conflate "promoter" with "entrepreneur" or "founder-director." They assume: (1) The person who has the business idea automatically becomes a director; (2) Professionals (CA, CS, lawyers) assisting in incorporation are promoters; (3) Preliminary contracts become binding on the company after incorporation through ratification. The root error is treating promotion as a business activity rather than a legally defined pre-incorporation phase with distinct statutory criteria (Section 69) and consequences (fiduciary duty, personal liability, no ratification of preliminary contracts). For example, in a board question "X, a CA, drafts MoA for a fee. Is X a promoter?" students often answer "Yes, because he helped form the company"—missing the professional-capacity exclusion.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Ramesh, a software engineer, develops an AI-based crop-disease detection app. He estimates ₹12 crore for development, marketing, and scaling. He approaches a VC firm which agrees to fund provided he incorporates a company. Ramesh hires a Company Secretary (CS) to check name availability, draft MoA/AoA, and file incorporation forms. The CS charges ₹80,000. Ramesh also signs a lease for office space at ₹2 lakh/month for 3 years in his own name during promotion. The company is incorporated 4 months later. The Board refuses to take over the lease. Analyse: (a) Is the CS a promoter? (b) Who is liable for the lease? (c) Which promotion steps did Ramesh complete/skip?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the actors—Ramesh (idea originator, fund arranger, lease signer), CS (professional hired for incorporation), VC (funding source).
- Step 2: Apply Section 69 to CS—CS acts in professional capacity (drafting, filing), not conceiving idea, not controlling affairs, not directing Board → CS is NOT a promoter.
- Step 3: Apply preliminary contract rule—Lease signed by Ramesh during promotion is a preliminary contract. Company cannot ratify it. Company can enter fresh lease. Ramesh remains personally liable for ₹2 lakh × 36 months = ₹72 lakh unless fresh contract signed.
- Step 4: Map Ramesh's actions to 6 promotion steps: (1) Opportunity identified ✓ (AI app for farmers); (2) Feasibility studies—technical (app works?), financial (₹12 cr arranged via VC?), economic (profitable?) → VC term sheet suggests financial feasibility but technical/economic not explicitly verified → partially done; (3) Name approval → CS checks availability ✓; (4) Fix MoA signatories → Ramesh + 6 others for public company ✓; (5) Appoint professionals → CS appointed ✓; (6) Prepare documents → CS drafts MoA/AoA, consent, declaration ✓.
- Step 5: Synthesise—Ramesh is promoter (Section 69(a) named in prospectus, 69(b) controls affairs). CS is professional. Lease liability stays with Ramesh.
- Step 6: Board exam answer structure—define promoter per Section 69, state preliminary contract principle, map steps, conclude liability.
REASONING: This example tests the core distinction between promoter and professional (Section 69 proviso), the non-ratifiability of preliminary contracts, and the six-step promotion sequence. The VC term sheet is a proxy for financial feasibility but not a substitute for promoter's own studies. The lease in Ramesh's personal name is a classic preliminary contract trap.
ANSWER: (a) No, CS is not a promoter—Section 69(c) professional-capacity exclusion applies. (b) Ramesh is personally liable for the full lease amount; company is not bound and cannot ratify. (c) Completed: Steps 1, 3, 4, 5, 6. Partially done/skipped: Step 2 (feasibility studies not explicitly documented).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: "GreenEnergy Ltd." is proposed as a public company to manufacture solar inverters. Three individuals—Anita (engineer, conceived the technology), Bhavesh (CA, drafted financial model), and Chirag (industrialist, providing ₹5 crore seed funding)—are involved. The ROC receives incorporation documents with 7 signatories to MoA. During promotion, Anita signed a contract with a German machinery supplier for €2 million equipment. The contract states: "This agreement shall be binding on GreenEnergy Ltd. upon its incorporation." The company incorporates on 15th March. On 20th March, the Board passes a resolution: "We adopt the contract with German supplier dated 10th January." The supplier delivers equipment in April. The company refuses payment citing defective specs. The supplier sues. Determine: (i) Who are the promoters? (ii) Is the Board's resolution valid ratification? (iii) Can the company avoid the contract?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Apply Section 69 to each person. Anita: conceived idea, took initiative → promoter (69(a)/(b)). Bhavesh: CA acting professionally → NOT promoter (69(c) exclusion). Chirag: provides seed funding, may be shareholder/director later; if he controls affairs or directs Board → promoter per 69(b)/(c); if passive investor → not promoter. Assume Chirag is active → promoter.
- Step 2: Analyse the contract clause—"binding upon incorporation" is a preliminary contract. NCERT: "A company cannot ratify a preliminary contract." The Board's resolution is an attempted ratification, which is legally ineffective.
- Step 3: Company's options—It can enter a fresh contract with the supplier on same/different terms. If it does not, the supplier's only recourse is against the promoters (Anita, possibly Chirag) personally.
- Step 4: Defective specs defence—If company enters fresh contract, it can raise quality defences. If no fresh contract, company has no contractual relationship with supplier; supplier sues promoters for breach.
- Step 5: Board exam framing—Quote NCERT: "Preliminary contracts are not legally binding on the company... It cannot ratify a preliminary contract." Distinguish from provisional contracts (post-incorporation, pre-commencement).
- Step 6: Conclude—Promoters: Anita and Chirag (if active). Resolution invalid. Company can avoid contract by not entering fresh agreement; supplier's remedy lies against promoters personally.
REASONING: This tests the "cannot ratify" rule which is a direct NCERT statement often missed. The "binding upon incorporation" clause is a red herring—parties cannot contractually override the statutory rule that a non-existent entity cannot be bound. The distinction between Anita (originator) and Bhavesh (professional) mirrors the Section 69 proviso.
ANSWER: (i) Promoters: Anita (conceiver) and Chirag (if exercising control/direction). Bhavesh is NOT a promoter (professional capacity). (ii) No, the Board's resolution is invalid—preliminary contracts cannot be ratified. (iii) Yes, the company can avoid the contract by not entering a fresh contract; the supplier's remedy is against the promoters personally for breach of preliminary contract.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A group of 5 doctors wants to form a private company "HealthFirst Pvt Ltd" to run a chain of diagnostic clinics. They have ₹3 crore own funds and need ₹7 crore bank loan. They engage a consultant who charges ₹2 lakh for "promotion services" including: (a) market survey, (b) name reservation, (c) MoA/AoA drafting, (d) ROC filing, (e) obtaining DIN for all 5. The consultant also introduces them to a bank for the loan. The bank sanctions ₹7 crore against clinic property mortgage. The company incorporates on 1st July. On 15th July, the consultant sends a bill for ₹2 lakh + ₹50,000 "success fee" for loan arrangement. The doctors refuse the success fee. The consultant claims he is a promoter entitled to remuneration. Meanwhile, one doctor, Dr. Mehta, had signed a 5-year equipment maintenance contract with a vendor on 1st June (pre-incorporation) in the company's proposed name. The vendor now demands payment from the company. Analyse all legal issues.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify promoters—The 5 doctors collectively conceive the idea, arrange funds, sign MoA (min 2 for private company), take initiative → all 5 are promoters per Section 69(a)/(b).
- Step 2: Consultant's status—Performs market survey, name reservation, drafting, filing, DIN—all professional services. Introduces to bank—professional introduction. Section 69(c) exclusion applies: "acting merely in a professional capacity." Consultant is NOT a promoter.
- Step 3: Consultant's remuneration—Promoters are not legally entitled to expenses/remuneration; company may choose to reimburse. Consultant has a contractual claim for agreed fees (₹2 lakh) but no statutory right as promoter. Success fee depends on contract terms, not promoter status.
- Step 4: Dr. Mehta's equipment contract—Signed 1st June, incorporation 1st July → preliminary contract. NCERT: "Promoters remain personally liable to third parties for these contracts." Company cannot ratify. Vendor's remedy: sue Dr. Mehta (and jointly other promoters if joint liability) personally.
- Step 5: Bank loan—Sanctioned against property mortgage. Loan agreement likely signed post-incorporation (15th July) → valid company contract. If signed pre-incorporation, same preliminary contract issue arises.
- Step 6: Board exam synthesis—Distinguish promoter vs professional (Section 69), promoter remuneration (no statutory right, contractual only), preliminary contract liability (personal, non-ratifiable), and private company MoA signatory requirement (2, not 7).
REASONING: This multi-issue problem combines promoter identification (Section 69), professional exclusion, promoter remuneration principles, preliminary contract doctrine, and private vs public company distinctions. The consultant's "success fee" claim tests whether students conflate business introduction with promoter status. Dr. Mehta's contract tests the non-ratification rule in a private company context.
ANSWER: (1) Promoters: All 5 doctors. Consultant is NOT a promoter (professional capacity exclusion). (2) Consultant entitled to contractual fee (₹2 lakh) but not promoter remuneration; success fee depends on contract, not promoter status. (3) Equipment maintenance contract is preliminary contract—company not bound, cannot ratify; Dr. Mehta (and co-promoters) personally liable to vendor. (4) Bank loan likely valid if signed post-incorporation; if pre-incorporation, same preliminary contract issue.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: "InnovateX" is a proposed public company for drone-based agricultural spraying. The founding team includes: Priya (drone engineer, conceived technology), Rohan (MBA, prepared business plan), and a law firm "LegalEdge" (drafted MoA, filed forms). During promotion, Priya signed a contract with a battery supplier for 500 units at ₹1.5 crore, stating "subject to incorporation." The supplier delivered 200 units before incorporation. After incorporation on 10th August, the Board meets on 15th August and passes: "We accept the battery contract for 200 units delivered." The remaining 300 units are delivered in September. The company pays for 200 units but refuses the 300, citing no contract. The supplier sues the company for 300 units and the promoters for 200 units. 

Answer the following:
(a) Identify the promoters under Section 69, justifying each classification.
(b) Analyse the legal status of the battery contract for the 200 pre-incorporation units and the 300 post-incorporation units.
(c) Evaluate the Board's 15th August resolution—does it create liability for the 300 units?
(d) Who is liable for what, and why?

COMPETENCY: Legal reasoning with statutory interpretation—applying Section 69 and preliminary contract doctrine to a multi-phase fact pattern.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Apply Section 69 to each actor—Priya (conceiver, initiator), Rohan (business plan—professional or promoter?), LegalEdge (law firm—professional capacity).
- Step 2: Classify battery contract phases—pre-incorporation delivery (200 units) = preliminary contract; post-incorporation delivery (300 units) = new contract offer/acceptance analysis.
- Step 3: Apply "no ratification" rule to Board resolution for 200 units—resolution ineffective for preliminary contract.
- Step 4: Analyse 300 units—Board resolution + acceptance of delivery may constitute fresh contract formation.
- Step 5: Determine liability matrix—promoters for 200 units; company for 300 units if fresh contract formed.
- Step 6: Structure answer with statutory citations and NCERT principles.

ANSWER: (a) Promoters: Priya (Section 69(a)/(b)—conceiver, controls affairs). Rohan: if MBA acting as consultant → professional capacity exclusion; if co-founder with control → promoter. LegalEdge: law firm → professional capacity exclusion, NOT promoter. (b) 200 units pre-incorporation: preliminary contract, binding on promoters personally, not on company. 300 units post-incorporation: not preliminary contract; Board resolution + acceptance of delivery may form fresh contract. (c) Board resolution for 200 units is invalid ratification (NCERT: "cannot ratify a preliminary contract"). For 300 units, resolution + delivery acceptance = fresh contract. (d) Promoters (Priya, possibly Rohan) liable for 200 units. Company liable for 300 units if fresh contract formed via resolution + conduct. Supplier's claim for 300 units against company succeeds; claim for 200 units against promoters succeeds.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: "AgriTech Solutions" is a proposed private company for precision farming IoT devices. Four founders—Anjali (IoT architect), Bharat (agronomist), Chitra (CA), and Dev (marketing head)—decide to incorporate. They skip detailed technical feasibility, assuming IoT components are easily importable. They apply for name "AgriTech Solutions Pvt Ltd" but ROC rejects it as identical to existing "AgriTech India Pvt Ltd." They reapply with "AgriTech Innovations Pvt Ltd" and get approval. They prepare MoA with 4 signatories (all founders). Chitra drafts MoA/AoA, files forms, obtains DINs. Anjali signs a 3-year cloud server contract with AWS India on 1st May for ₹50 lakh/year "on behalf of AgriTech Innovations Pvt Ltd." The company incorporates on 1st June. On 5th June, the Board (all 4 founders) passes: "We ratify the AWS contract." AWS raises invoice for Year 1. The company refuses payment, claiming: (i) no technical feasibility study was done, so promotion was defective; (ii) the contract was pre-incorporation and cannot be ratified. AWS sues. 

Decide with reasons:
(a) Was the promotion defective due to skipped feasibility study? What is the legal consequence?
(b) Is the Board's ratification of the AWS contract valid?
(c) Who is liable to AWS, and for how much?
(d) If the company had been a public company, how would the MoA signatory requirement differ?

COMPETENCY: Integrated application—feasibility study requirement vs legal validity, preliminary contract non-ratification rule, promoter liability, private vs public company distinction.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Feasibility study—NCERT: "promoters undertake detailed feasibility studies... Only when these investigations throw up positive results, the promoters may decide to actually launch a company." Skipping it is a business judgment error, not a legal defect in promotion. No statutory provision invalidates incorporation for lack of feasibility study.
- Step 2: AWS contract—signed 1st May, incorporation 1st June → preliminary contract. NCERT: "A company cannot ratify a preliminary contract." Board resolution of 5th June is legally ineffective.
- Step 3: Liability—Promoters (all 4 founders) jointly and severally liable to AWS for Year 1 (₹50 lakh) and potentially subsequent years if contract not replaced. Company not liable unless fresh contract entered.
- Step 4: Private vs public MoA signatories—Private company: minimum 2 signatories (they have 4, valid). Public company: minimum 7 signatories (Section 7(1)(a)). They would need 3 more subscribers.
- Step 5: Synthesise—Feasibility study is prudential, not jurisdictional. Preliminary contract doctrine is absolute. Promoter liability is personal and joint.

ANSWER: (a) Promotion is not legally defective—feasibility study is a prudential step for promoters' own decision-making, not a statutory precondition for incorporation. Consequence: business risk, not legal invalidity. (b) No, ratification is invalid—preliminary contracts cannot be ratified per NCERT and Companies Act jurisprudence. (c) All 4 promoters (Anjali, Bharat, Chitra, Dev) are jointly and severally liable to AWS for ₹50 lakh (Year 1) and potentially future years. Company is not liable unless it enters a fresh contract. (d) Public company requires minimum 7 signatories to MoA (Section 7(1)(a)); private company requires 2. Current 4 signatories suffice for private but would be insufficient for public.

DIFFICULTY: hard
