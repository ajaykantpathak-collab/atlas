## DEFINITION
Derivatives are financial instruments whose value is derived from the value of an underlying asset such as shares, commodities, currencies, interest rates, or market indices. The three main types are forwards (customised OTC contracts to buy/sell at a future date), futures (standardised exchange-traded contracts with daily settlement), and options (contracts giving the right but not the obligation to buy/call or sell/put the underlying at a predetermined price).

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine a wheat farmer in Punjab who fears prices may drop by harvest time, and a bread factory in Delhi that fears prices may rise. They could sign a private deal today to fix the price for delivery in three months — that's a **forward**. If they want the safety of a regulated exchange, daily settlement, and no counterparty risk, they'd use a **future**. If the farmer wants protection against a price drop but still wants to benefit if prices rise, he buys a **put option** paying a premium. Start by listing the three instruments side-by-side; then for each, ask: "Who bears the obligation? Who has the right? Where is it traded? How is risk managed?" This comparative lens builds the mental framework needed for board case-study questions and CUET application questions.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the fundamental difference between a forward contract and a futures contract in terms of standardisation and trading venue?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
A forward contract is a customised, bilateral agreement traded over-the-counter (OTC) with no standardised terms, while a futures contract is standardised in terms of quantity, quality, delivery date, and traded on a recognised stock exchange. A wrong answer (e.g., "forwards are exchange-traded") reveals confusion about the OTC vs exchange distinction — a classic board exam trap.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why a futures contract requires both initial margin and maintenance margin, whereas a forward contract typically does not.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Futures are exchange-traded with daily marking-to-market; margins act as performance bonds to eliminate counterparty risk. Forwards are private OTC contracts settled only at maturity, so counterparty risk remains and no formal margin system exists. A wrong answer (e.g., "forwards also have margin") shows the student hasn't grasped the role of the clearing corporation in futures.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A jeweller expects gold prices to rise in two months. He enters a long futures contract at ₹60,000 per 10g. At expiry, the spot price is ₹62,000. Calculate his profit per 10g and explain whether he would have been better off with a call option (premium ₹1,500) instead.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Futures profit = ₹62,000 – ₹60,000 = ₹2,000 per 10g. Call option payoff = max(0, 62,000 – 60,000) – 1,500 = ₹500 per 10g. Futures yield higher profit here because the price moved favourably beyond the premium cost. If the student ignores the premium or misapplies the option payoff formula, they miss the cost-benefit trade-off central to competitive exams.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the risk profiles of a long forward, a long future, and a long call option. Which instrument limits the maximum loss for the buyer, and why?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Long forward/future: unlimited loss potential if price moves against the position. Long call option: maximum loss limited to the premium paid. The option's asymmetric payoff (right, not obligation) caps downside. A student who says "futures limit loss because of margin" confuses margin calls with loss limitation — a critical conceptual error.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
"Options are always better than futures for hedging because they limit downside." Evaluate this statement with reference to the cost of hedging and the obligation to perform.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is false. Options require an upfront premium, increasing hedging cost. Futures have no premium but create a binding obligation — if the hedge is no longer needed, exiting a futures position may incur losses. The choice depends on risk appetite, cost sensitivity, and certainty of exposure. A student who agrees without mentioning premium cost or obligation shows superficial understanding.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a hedging strategy for an Indian importer who must pay $1 million in 90 days. The importer wants to lock in the exchange rate but also benefit if the rupee appreciates. Specify the instrument, position, and key parameters.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Buy a USD-INR call option (right to buy dollars) with strike near current spot, expiry ≥ 90 days. If rupee depreciates, exercise and buy at strike; if rupee appreciates, let option lapse and buy at spot, losing only premium. A good answer identifies the correct option type (call on USD), strike selection, and trade-off. A poor answer suggests futures (no upside participation) or put option (wrong direction).

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think "futures and forwards are the same thing — both fix a future price." They miss that futures are exchange-traded, standardised, marked-to-market daily, and cleared by a clearing corporation, while forwards are private, customised, and carry counterparty risk. This leads to errors in board questions asking for "two differences" and in CUET case studies where margin calls or daily settlement are central.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A cotton farmer expects to harvest 500 quintals in 3 months. Current spot price is ₹6,000/quintal. He enters a forward contract with a textile mill to sell at ₹6,200/quintal. At harvest, spot price is ₹5,800. Calculate the farmer's gain/loss from the forward vs selling in spot market. Also state the mill's gain/loss.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the forward contract terms — farmer sells 500 quintals at ₹6,200/quintal in 3 months.
- Step 2: Determine spot price at maturity — ₹5,800/quintal.
- Step 3: Calculate farmer's revenue under forward: 500 × ₹6,200 = ₹31,00,000.
- Step 4: Calculate farmer's revenue if sold in spot: 500 × ₹5,800 = ₹29,00,000.
- Step 5: Farmer's gain from forward = ₹31,00,000 – ₹29,00,000 = ₹2,00,000.
- Step 6: Mill's loss = same amount (zero-sum before transaction costs).
REASONING: Forwards lock in price, eliminating price risk. The farmer gains because spot fell below contracted price; the mill loses an equivalent amount. This illustrates the zero-sum nature and price certainty of forwards.
ANSWER: Farmer gains ₹2,00,000; mill loses ₹2,00,000.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: An investor buys 1 lot of Nifty 50 futures (lot size 50) at 19,500. Initial margin is 10%, maintenance margin 8%. Next day, Nifty closes at 19,300. Calculate the mark-to-market loss, margin balance, and whether a margin call is triggered.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Contract value at entry = 50 × 19,500 = ₹9,75,000. Initial margin = 10% = ₹97,500.
- Step 2: Daily settlement price = 19,300. Price change = 19,300 – 19,500 = –200 points.
- Step 3: Mark-to-market loss = 50 × 200 = ₹10,000 (debited from margin account).
- Step 4: Margin balance after loss = ₹97,500 – ₹10,000 = ₹87,500.
- Step 5: Maintenance margin required = 8% of current contract value (50 × 19,300) = ₹77,200.
- Step 6: Since ₹87,500 > ₹77,200, no margin call is triggered.
REASONING: Futures are marked-to-market daily; losses reduce margin balance. Margin call occurs only if balance falls below maintenance margin. This example shows the mechanics of daily settlement and margin monitoring.
ANSWER: MTM loss ₹10,000; margin balance ₹87,500; no margin call.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A portfolio manager holds ₹5 crore in Nifty 50 stocks. Nifty is at 19,500. He buys 200 Nifty put options (lot size 50) with strike 19,000, premium ₹300, expiry 1 month. Nifty falls to 18,200 at expiry. Calculate net portfolio protection (gain on options minus premium cost). Compare with shorting futures.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Number of options = 200, each covering 50 units → total underlying = 10,000 Nifty units.
- Step 2: Intrinsic value per unit at expiry = max(0, 19,000 – 18,200) = ₹800.
- Step 3: Gross option payoff = 10,000 × ₹800 = ₹80,00,000.
- Step 4: Total premium paid = 10,000 × ₹300 = ₹30,00,000.
- Step 5: Net gain from options = ₹80,00,000 – ₹30,00,000 = ₹50,00,000.
- Step 6: Portfolio loss without hedge: assume beta=1, Nifty drop = (19,500-18,200)/19,500 ≈ 6.67%. Portfolio loss ≈ ₹33,35,000.
- Step 7: Net protected value = portfolio loss offset by option gain → effective protection with upside participation if Nifty rises.
- Step 8: Short futures alternative: would lock in 19,500, eliminating loss but also eliminating upside.
REASONING: Options provide asymmetric protection — downside capped (minus premium), upside retained. Futures provide symmetric lock-in. The manager chooses based on risk-return trade-off.
ANSWER: Net option gain ₹50,00,000; portfolio effectively protected with upside participation.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A case study describes a small exporter who will receive $100,000 in 60 days. The exporter approaches a bank for a forward cover at ₹83.50/$. The bank offers a forward contract but also suggests a currency put option (right to sell $ at ₹83.00, premium ₹0.50/$). The exporter is risk-averse but wants to benefit if the rupee depreciates further. Analyse both alternatives and recommend one with justification.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to financial instrument choices and justifying with cost-benefit analysis
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the exposure — exporter is long USD, short INR; risk is rupee appreciation (USD depreciation).
- Step 2: Forward cover locks in ₹83.50/$, eliminating risk but also eliminating upside if rupee depreciates (USD strengthens).
- Step 3: Put option (right to sell USD at ₹83.00) costs ₹0.50/$ premium. If rupee appreciates to ₹82, exercise and sell at ₹83.00, net ₹82.50 after premium. If rupee depreciates to ₹85, let option lapse, sell at ₹85, net ₹84.50 after premium.
- Step 4: Compare: Forward guarantees ₹83.50. Option guarantees at least ₹82.50 (strike minus premium) but allows participation above ₹83.50.
- Step 5: Recommendation: Since exporter is risk-averse but wants upside, put option is better despite premium cost. The premium is the price of optional upside.
ANSWER: Recommend put option. It provides a floor of ₹82.50/$ (after premium) while allowing benefit if spot moves above ₹83.50/$. Forward locks at ₹83.50 with no upside.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: "In a futures contract, both parties are obligated to perform. In an option, only the seller is obligated." Critically evaluate this statement. Then, explain how the clearing corporation eliminates counterparty risk in futures but not in forwards, using the concepts of novation and margin.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying with institutional mechanics
STEPS:
- Step 1: Evaluate the statement — it is correct. Futures: both long and short are obligated. Option: buyer has right, seller (writer) has obligation if exercised.
- Step 2: Explain novation — clearing corporation becomes counterparty to both sides, guaranteeing performance.
- Step 3: Explain margin system — initial and maintenance margins, daily marking-to-market, margin calls ensure funds are available.
- Step 4: Contrast with forwards — no clearing corporation, bilateral contract, no daily settlement, counterparty risk remains until maturity.
- Step 5: Conclude that futures' institutional design (novation + margin) transforms bilateral risk into managed systemic risk.
ANSWER: The statement is accurate. Futures create mutual obligation; options create asymmetric obligation. Clearing corporation interposes itself via novation, becoming buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer. Daily margin calls and marking-to-market ensure losses are collected daily, virtually eliminating counterparty default. Forwards lack this mechanism — risk is bilateral and accumulates until settlement.
DIFFICULTY: hard
