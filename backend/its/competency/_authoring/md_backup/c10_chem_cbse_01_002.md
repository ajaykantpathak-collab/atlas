## DEFINITION
A chemical equation is a symbolic representation of a chemical reaction where reactants (starting substances) are written on the left side and products (new substances formed) on the right side, separated by an arrow (→), with physical states indicated in parentheses. Balancing by inspection is the method of adjusting stoichiometric coefficients (numbers placed before formulas) to ensure the number of atoms of each element is identical on both sides, obeying the Law of Conservation of Mass.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can independently convert a word equation into a correct skeleton equation with proper chemical formulas and physical states, then balance it by inspection using the "element-by-element" strategy, and verify atom counts on both sides for combination, decomposition, displacement, and double displacement reactions.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers writing skeleton equations from word descriptions, assigning physical states (s, l, g, aq), and balancing by inspection for reactions involving 2–4 reactants/products with polyatomic ions treated as units. Does NOT cover balancing redox reactions by half-reaction method, algebraic balancing, or balancing ionic equations (covered in higher classes).

## WALKTHROUGH
Observe a magnesium ribbon burning in air — it produces a blinding white light and leaves a white ash (magnesium oxide). This is a chemical change you saw in Class 6 (c6_chemistry_ch05_004) and Class 9 (c9_chem_ch3_1). To represent it chemically, we first write the word equation: Magnesium + Oxygen → Magnesium oxide. Then we convert to formulas using valency rules from Class 9 (c9_chem_ch3_6): Mg + O₂ → MgO. Notice oxygen atoms are unbalanced (2 on left, 1 on right). We balance by placing coefficient 2 before MgO, then 2 before Mg: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. This step-by-step inspection method is the core skill for all reaction types in this chapter.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Write the balanced chemical equation for the reaction: Hydrogen gas burns in oxygen gas to form liquid water. Include physical states.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Correct answer: 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l). A wrong answer like H₂ + O₂ → H₂O reveals the student recalls the reaction but fails to apply the Law of Conservation of Mass — they have not balanced oxygen atoms (2 on left, 1 on right). Another error: writing H₂O(g) instead of (l) shows confusion about water's state at room temperature, a common board exam marking penalty.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
A student writes the skeleton equation for heating calcium carbonate as: CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g). They claim it is already balanced. Is the student correct? Explain why or why not by counting atoms of each element on both sides.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Yes, the student is correct. Left side: Ca=1, C=1, O=3. Right side: Ca=1 (in CaO), C=1 (in CO₂), O=1+2=3. All atoms match. A wrong answer claiming it is unbalanced reveals the student does not recognize that polyatomic ions (CO₃²⁻) break apart in decomposition — they may incorrectly treat CO₃ as a unit that must stay intact, a misconception from not understanding ionic dissociation in thermal decomposition.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Balance the following skeleton equation by inspection: Fe(s) + H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄(s) + H₂(g). Show all steps and verify the final atom count.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Count atoms: Fe: 1 vs 3, H: 2 vs 2, O: 1 vs 4. Step 2: Balance Fe first (appears in one compound each side): place 3 before Fe → 3Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂. Step 3: Balance O: 4 on right, so place 4 before H₂O → 3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂. Step 4: Balance H: 8 on left, so place 4 before H₂ → 3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂. Step 5: Verify: Fe=3, H=8, O=4 on both sides. Balanced: 3Fe(s) + 4H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄(s) + 4H₂(g). A wrong answer like Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂ shows the student balanced O and H but forgot to go back and balance Fe — a classic "start over" error when elements are interdependent.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the balancing approach for these two reactions: (i) Na(s) + Cl₂(g) → NaCl(s) and (ii) P₄(s) + Cl₂(g) → PCl₃(l). Which reaction requires balancing the diatomic element first? Justify your answer by analyzing the element inventory.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Reaction (ii) requires balancing the diatomic element (Cl₂) first. In (i), Na and Cl₂ both appear once; we balance Cl by putting 2 before NaCl, then 2 before Na. In (ii), P₄ has 4 P atoms, so we put 4 before PCl₃, giving 12 Cl on right. Then we balance Cl₂ by putting 6 before it. If a student starts with P₄ in (i) or Cl₂ in (ii) without checking which element appears in multiple compounds, they create fractional coefficients or dead ends. Wrong answer reveals inability to strategize — they follow a rigid "left to right" rule instead of analyzing the equation structure.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student balances the double displacement reaction: BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + NaCl(aq) as: BaCl₂ + Na₂SO₄ → BaSO₄ + 2NaCl. They argue it is balanced because Ba, Cl, Na, S, O counts match. Evaluate this claim and identify the error if any.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The claim is wrong. Atom count: Left: Ba=1, Cl=2, Na=2, S=1, O=4. Right: Ba=1, S=1, O=4, Na=2, Cl=2. Atoms match, BUT the student wrote NaCl instead of 2NaCl in the skeleton. The error is in the skeleton equation: Na₂SO₄ provides 2 Na⁺, so product must be 2NaCl, not NaCl. The student "balanced" by changing the formula of sodium chloride from NaCl to 2NaCl (coefficient) but the skeleton itself was incorrectly written as NaCl (implying 1 Na). This reveals confusion between coefficient (stoichiometric number) and subscript (formula composition) — a critical board exam distinction.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a 3-step experimental procedure to prove that the reaction between iron nails and copper sulphate solution follows the balanced equation: Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s). Your procedure must include how to verify the stoichiometric ratio 1:1:1:1.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Good answer: Step 1: Weigh a clean iron nail (mass m₁). Step 2: Immerse in 50 mL of 0.1 M CuSO₄ solution until reaction stops (blue colour fades, reddish Cu deposits). Step 3: Remove nail, wash, dry, weigh again (mass m₂). Mass loss = m₁ - m₂ = mass of Fe reacted. Filter and dry the Cu deposit, weigh (mass m₃). Calculate moles: n(Fe) = (m₁-m₂)/56, n(Cu) = m₃/63.5. If n(Fe) ≈ n(Cu), 1:1 ratio verified. Poor answer: "Observe colour change" — reveals inability to connect symbolic equation to quantitative experimental verification, a key competency in CBSE practical assessment.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students change subscripts inside chemical formulas to balance equations (e.g., writing H₂ + O₂ → H₂O₂ instead of 2H₂O, or changing Fe₃O₄ to FeO₄). They treat subscripts as adjustable numbers rather than fixed composition identifiers. This stems from not internalizing that a formula represents a specific compound with fixed atom ratios (Class 9, c9_chem_ch3_6), while coefficients represent how many formula units participate. In board exams, writing H₂O₂ for water loses full marks for that equation.

## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Use coloured molecular model kits or paper cutouts — red for O, white for H, black for C. Build reactant molecules physically. Step 2: Count atoms of each colour on left. Step 3: Build product molecules one by one, adding whole units only (never break a molecule). Step 4: If atoms don't match, add another whole reactant or product unit. Step 5: Record how many units of each were used — those are coefficients. Repeat for 5 equations until the student never attempts to change a subscript.

## THIS_FORMULA
General form: aA + bB → cC + dD where a,b,c,d are stoichiometric coefficients (smallest whole numbers) and A,B,C,D are chemical formulas with states. Balancing condition: For each element X, Σ(atoms of X in reactants) = Σ(atoms of X in products).

## FORMULA_VARIABLES
a,b,c,d = stoichiometric coefficients (dimensionless, smallest whole numbers); A,B,C,D = chemical formulas of reactants/products; (s),(l),(g),(aq) = physical states solid, liquid, gas, aqueous.

## FORMULA_UNITS
Coefficients are pure numbers (no units). Atom counts are dimensionless. Molar amounts use mol (later nodes).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Solid potassium chlorate decomposes on heating to give solid potassium chloride and oxygen gas. Write the balanced chemical equation with physical states.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify reactants and products from word equation. Reactant: potassium chlorate (KClO₃). Products: potassium chloride (KCl) and oxygen (O₂). All are solids except oxygen gas.
- Step 2: Write skeleton equation with correct formulas and states: KClO₃(s) → KCl(s) + O₂(g).
- Step 3: Count atoms: Left: K=1, Cl=1, O=3. Right: K=1, Cl=1, O=2. Oxygen unbalanced (3 vs 2).
- Step 4: Balance O by finding LCM of 3 and 2 = 6. Put 2 before KClO₃ (gives 6 O) and 3 before O₂ (gives 6 O): 2KClO₃(s) → KCl(s) + 3O₂(g).
- Step 5: Re-check K and Cl: Left K=2, Cl=2. Right K=1, Cl=1. Balance by putting 2 before KCl: 2KClO₃(s) → 2KCl(s) + 3O₂(g).
- Step 6: Final verification: K=2, Cl=2, O=6 on both sides. Equation balanced.
REASONING: We balanced oxygen first because it appears in only one reactant and one product but with different subscripts (3 vs 2). Using LCM avoids fractions. Then we balanced the metals which were disturbed by the oxygen balancing step. This "balance the most complex element first" strategy prevents back-and-forth.
ANSWER: 2KClO₃(s) → 2KCl(s) + 3O₂(g)

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Aqueous barium chloride reacts with aqueous sodium sulphate to form a white precipitate of barium sulphate and aqueous sodium chloride. Write the balanced chemical equation with states.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify ions: BaCl₂ → Ba²⁺ + 2Cl⁻; Na₂SO₄ → 2Na⁺ + SO₄²⁻. Products: BaSO₄ (insoluble, precipitate) and NaCl (soluble).
- Step 2: Write skeleton with states: BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + NaCl(aq).
- Step 3: Count atoms: Ba=1, Cl=2, Na=2, S=1, O=4 on left. Right: Ba=1, S=1, O=4, Na=1, Cl=1. Sodium and chlorine unbalanced.
- Step 4: Balance Na and Cl together by putting 2 before NaCl: BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq).
- Step 5: Verify: Ba=1, Cl=2, Na=2, S=1, O=4 on both sides. All balanced. Note: Polyatomic ion SO₄²⁻ stays intact (1 on each side) — treat as unit.
REASONING: In double displacement, the cations swap anions. The precipitate (BaSO₄) drives the reaction. Balancing is straightforward because each element appears in only one compound per side except Na and Cl which are linked in NaCl. We balance the "swapped" ions together.
ANSWER: BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq)

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: In the thermite reaction, aluminium powder reacts with iron(III) oxide to produce molten iron and aluminium oxide. The reaction is highly exothermic and used for welding railway tracks. Write the balanced equation. If 5.4 g of Al is used, calculate the mass of Fe produced (molar masses: Al=27, Fe=56, O=16). [Combines balancing with mole concept from Class 9]
STEPS:
- Step 1: Write formulas: Al(s) + Fe₂O₃(s) → Fe(l) + Al₂O₃(s). States: Al and Fe₂O₃ solids, Fe molten (l) due to high heat, Al₂O₃ solid.
- Step 2: Balance by inspection. Fe: 2 on left → put 2 before Fe. Al: 2 on right (in Al₂O₃) → put 2 before Al. O: 3 on left, 3 on right (in Al₂O₃) → balanced. Equation: 2Al(s) + Fe₂O₃(s) → 2Fe(l) + Al₂O₃(s).
- Step 3: Verify atoms: Al=2, Fe=2, O=3 both sides.
- Step 4: Mole calculation. Moles of Al = mass/molar mass = 5.4 g / 27 g mol⁻¹ = 0.2 mol.
- Step 5: Stoichiometric ratio from balanced equation: 2 mol Al → 2 mol Fe, so 1:1. Moles of Fe produced = 0.2 mol.
- Step 6: Mass of Fe = moles × molar mass = 0.2 mol × 56 g mol⁻¹ = 11.2 g.
REASONING: The balanced equation provides the mole ratio (2:2 = 1:1) which is the bridge between mass of reactant and mass of product. Without correct balancing, the ratio would be wrong (e.g., if unbalanced as Al + Fe₂O₃ → Fe + Al₂O₃, ratio would be 1:1 for Al:Fe but Al atoms unbalanced). This is exactly how CBSE board exam questions combine balancing with quantitative chemistry.
ANSWER: Balanced equation: 2Al(s) + Fe₂O₃(s) → 2Fe(l) + Al₂O₃(s); Mass of Fe = 11.2 g

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A student observes that when copper turnings are added to dilute silver nitrate solution, the solution turns blue and a greyish-white deposit forms on the copper. They write the equation: Cu(s) + AgNO₃(aq) → Cu(NO₃)₂(aq) + Ag(s). The teacher says the equation is incorrect in two ways. Identify both errors and write the correct balanced equation with states.
COMPETENCY: Interpreting chemical equations and identifying errors in stoichiometry and formula writing
STEPS:
- Step 1: Analyze the observation — blue solution means Cu²⁺ ions formed (copper nitrate), greyish-white deposit is silver metal. So products are Cu(NO₃)₂ and Ag.
- Step 2: Check reactants: Cu and AgNO₃ correct. Check product formulas: Cu(NO₃)₂ correct (Cu²⁺, NO₃⁻), Ag correct.
- Step 3: Identify first error: Silver nitrate is AgNO₃, but in the skeleton, only one AgNO₃ is written. However, Cu forms Cu²⁺, so it displaces 2 Ag⁺ ions. The skeleton must have 2AgNO₃ to provide 2 Ag and 2 NO₃.
- Step 4: Identify second error: The equation is not balanced. With 2AgNO₃, we get 2Ag on right, so coefficient 2 before Ag. Then N and O balance automatically.
- Step 5: Write correct balanced equation: Cu(s) + 2AgNO₃(aq) → Cu(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2Ag(s).
ANSWER: Error 1: Skeleton equation should have 2AgNO₃ (not AgNO₃) because Cu²⁺ displaces two Ag⁺ ions. Error 2: Equation not balanced — Ag atoms and NO₃ groups unbalanced. Correct: Cu(s) + 2AgNO₃(aq) → Cu(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2Ag(s).
DIFFICULTY: medium

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: During the electrolysis of water, a student collects gases at two electrodes. At the cathode, gas volume is double that at the anode. The student writes the overall reaction as: H₂O(l) → H₂(g) + O₂(g). They claim the volume ratio proves the equation is balanced as written. Evaluate the student's claim using the balanced chemical equation and the mole-gas volume relationship (Avogadro's law).
COMPETENCY: Relating balanced chemical equations to quantitative gas volume data and evaluating scientific claims
STEPS:
- Step 1: Recall Avogadro's law: Equal volumes of gases at same T and P contain equal moles. Volume ratio = mole ratio.
- Step 2: The observed volume ratio V_H₂ : V_O₂ = 2:1 implies mole ratio n_H₂ : n_O₂ = 2:1.
- Step 3: Write the balanced equation for water electrolysis by inspection: Balance O first — 2H₂O → O₂ (2 O each side). Then balance H: 4 H on left → 2H₂ on right. Balanced: 2H₂O(l) → 2H₂(g) + O₂(g).
- Step 4: From balanced equation, stoichiometric mole ratio H₂:O₂ = 2:1, matching the observed volume ratio.
- Step 5: Evaluate claim: The student's equation H₂O → H₂ + O₂ gives mole ratio 1:1, which would predict equal volumes. But observed is 2:1. Therefore, the student's equation is incorrect, and their claim is false. The balanced equation explains the data.
ANSWER: The student's claim is incorrect. Their equation H₂O → H₂ + O₂ predicts a 1:1 volume ratio, but the balanced equation 2H₂O(l) → 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) gives a 2:1 mole (and volume) ratio, which matches the observation. The student failed to balance the equation before interpreting the data.
DIFFICULTY: hard
