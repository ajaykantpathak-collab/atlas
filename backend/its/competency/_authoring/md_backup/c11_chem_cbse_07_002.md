## DEFINITION
Dynamic equilibrium in a chemical process is a state in a reversible reaction where the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction, resulting in no net change in the concentrations of reactants and products over time, though molecular-level transformations continue unabated.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
Student can explain why equilibrium is "dynamic" not "static" using molecular collision theory, predict how a system at equilibrium responds to a concentration change (qualitative Le Chatelier), and distinguish between physical and chemical equilibrium with examples.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers: reversible reactions, dynamic nature, macroscopic constancy vs microscopic activity, equilibrium in homogeneous and heterogeneous systems, and qualitative direction prediction. Does NOT cover: equilibrium constant expressions (Kc/Kp), quantitative calculations, Le Chatelier's principle formal statement, or temperature/pressure effects in depth (these are subsequent nodes).

## WALKTHROUGH
Observe a saturated sugar solution in a glass with undissolved crystals at the bottom — the amount of dissolved sugar appears constant, yet if you add radioactive sugar crystals, radioactivity soon appears in the solution, proving molecules continuously exchange. This mirrors Class 9 learning on saturated solutions (c9_chem_ch1_3) but now we generalize: in a closed vessel, N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) reaches a point where ammonia forms as fast as it decomposes. Start by identifying a reversible reaction (⇌), then track concentration vs time graphs until they plateau — that plateau is dynamic equilibrium.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
In the reversible reaction N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) at equilibrium in a closed container, which statement is correct? (a) The forward reaction has stopped. (b) The concentrations of N₂, H₂, and NH₃ are equal. (c) The rate of formation of NH₃ equals the rate of its decomposition. (d) The total pressure of the system is zero.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Correct: (c) The rate of formation of NH₃ equals the rate of its decomposition. Dynamic equilibrium means both forward and reverse reactions continue at equal rates, so no net change occurs. A wrong choice (a) reveals the static equilibrium misconception — students think "equilibrium" means everything stops. Choice (b) confuses equal rates with equal concentrations; equilibrium concentrations depend on Kc, not stoichiometry. Choice (d) is physically impossible for a gas mixture.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
A student writes: "At equilibrium, the reaction N₂O₄(g) ⇌ 2NO₂(g) has no net change because all N₂O₄ molecules have converted to NO₂." Explain why this is incorrect using the concept of dynamic equilibrium.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The statement is incorrect because at equilibrium, both N₂O₄ and NO₂ coexist; N₂O₄ continuously dissociates into NO₂ while NO₂ simultaneously recombines into N₂O₄ at the same rate. The "no net change" refers to constant macroscopic concentrations, not cessation of reaction. A wrong answer indicates the student views equilibrium as a completion point (like irreversible reactions in Class 10) rather than a dynamic balance. They likely confuse "equilibrium" with "reaction goes to completion" from c10_chem_cbse_01_001.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
For the equilibrium H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g), initially 1.0 mol H₂ and 1.0 mol I₂ are placed in a 10 L vessel. At equilibrium, 0.6 mol of HI is found. Calculate the equilibrium concentrations of all species and determine if the system has reached equilibrium from the forward or reverse direction.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Initial: [H₂] = 0.1 M, [I₂] = 0.1 M, [HI] = 0 M. Change: HI increases by 0.6 mol/10 L = 0.06 M, so H₂ and I₂ each decrease by 0.03 M (stoichiometry 1:1:2). Equilibrium: [HI] = 0.06 M, [H₂] = 0.07 M, [I₂] = 0.07 M. Since HI was zero initially and increased, the net reaction proceeded forward. A wrong answer (e.g., using 0.6 M instead of 0.06 M) reveals mole-to-concentration conversion error. Forgetting the 2:1 stoichiometry for HI formation shows incomplete balancing skill from c10_chem_cbse_01_002.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the equilibrium in a saturated NaCl solution (solid NaCl ⇌ Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)) with the gas-phase equilibrium 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g). Identify two similarities and two differences in their dynamic nature and macroscopic observables.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Similarities: (1) Both are dynamic — ions/crystals and gas molecules continuously interchange at equal rates. (2) Both show constant macroscopic properties (concentration/pressure) at equilibrium. Differences: (1) Heterogeneous vs homogeneous — NaCl equilibrium involves a solid phase (activity = 1), while SO₂/SO₃ is all gas. (2) Observable: saturated solution shows constant conductivity; gas equilibrium shows constant pressure/color intensity (SO₃ is colorless, SO₂ is colorless but often monitored via pressure). A poor answer misses the phase distinction or thinks "dynamic" means visible motion.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student claims: "If I add more H₂ to the equilibrium N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g), the system will immediately form more NH₃ and the equilibrium constant Kc will increase." Evaluate this claim.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The claim is partially correct in outcome but wrong in reasoning. Adding H₂ shifts equilibrium forward (Le Chatelier), producing more NH₃ temporarily, but Kc remains constant at constant temperature — only temperature changes Kc. The student confuses "position of equilibrium" (concentrations) with "equilibrium constant" (ratio at equilibrium). A wrong answer reveals confusion between Kc (thermodynamic constant) and Q (reaction quotient), which is formally taught in c11_chem_cbse_07_005.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple school-lab experiment to demonstrate that chemical equilibrium is dynamic, not static, using the reaction Fe³⁺(aq) + SCN⁻(aq) ⇌ [Fe(SCN)]²⁺(aq) (deep red). List materials, procedure, and the key observation that proves dynamism.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Materials: FeCl₃ solution, KSCN solution, water, test tubes, spectrophotometer or colorimeter (or visual comparison). Procedure: (1) Prepare equilibrium mixture — deep red. (2) Split into two tubes. (3) Add excess Fe³⁺ to tube A (color intensifies). (4) Add excess SCN⁻ to tube B (color intensifies). (5) To a third tube, add radioactive isotope tracer (⁵⁹Fe or ³⁵S) — if available — and track incorporation into complex. Key observation: color change upon adding either reactant proves the reverse reaction (dissociation of complex) was occurring; otherwise, adding more reactant couldn't shift equilibrium. Without isotopes, the color shift itself proves the system responds dynamically. A poor answer describes only initial color formation, missing the perturbation test.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students believe "equilibrium means 50% reactants, 50% products" because they equate "equal rates" with "equal concentrations." In N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) at 400°C, equilibrium may have 85% NH₃ by mole — yet forward rate = reverse rate. The error stems from confusing rate equality (kinetic) with concentration equality (thermodynamic), ignoring stoichiometric coefficients and activation energy differences.

## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Draw a concentration vs time graph for A ⇌ B starting with pure A — show curves approaching different plateau levels. Step 2: Emphasize "rate = k[A]" vs "rate = k'[B]" — at equilibrium k[A] = k'[B], so [B]/[A] = k/k' = Kc, not 1. Step 3: Use the "dance floor" analogy: 10 couples dancing (forward) and 10 couples leaving (reverse) — total on floor constant, but not 50/50 men/women. Step 4: Practice with ICE tables where equilibrium concentrations are clearly unequal.

## THIS_FORMULA
For a general reversible reaction: aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, at equilibrium: Rate_forward = k_f[A]^a[B]^b = Rate_reverse = k_r[C]^c[D]^d

## FORMULA_VARIABLES
k_f = forward rate constant, k_r = reverse rate constant, [A], [B], [C], [D] = equilibrium molar concentrations (mol/L), a,b,c,d = stoichiometric coefficients

## FORMULA_UNITS
k_f: depends on overall order (e.g., L^(n-1) mol^(1-n) s^-1), concentrations: mol L⁻¹, rates: mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹

## COMPUTATION_TYPE
algebraic

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: In a 2.0 L vessel, 0.80 mol N₂ and 1.20 mol H₂ are allowed to react: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g). At equilibrium, 0.40 mol NH₃ is found. Calculate the equilibrium concentrations of all three gases and verify the system reached equilibrium from the forward direction.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Convert initial moles to concentrations: [N₂]₀ = 0.80/2.0 = 0.40 M, [H₂]₀ = 1.20/2.0 = 0.60 M, [NH₃]₀ = 0 M.
- Step 2: Set up ICE table. Change: NH₃ increases by 0.40/2.0 = 0.20 M. From stoichiometry, N₂ decreases by 0.10 M (1:2 ratio), H₂ decreases by 0.30 M (3:2 ratio).
- Step 3: Equilibrium concentrations: [N₂] = 0.40 - 0.10 = 0.30 M, [H₂] = 0.60 - 0.30 = 0.30 M, [NH₃] = 0.20 M.
- Step 4: Verify forward direction: NH₃ formed from zero, so net reaction proceeded forward. Check mass balance: N atoms: 0.30×1 + 0.20×1 = 0.50? Wait — initial N atoms = 0.80 mol in 2L = 0.40 M N₂ → 0.80 M N atoms. Final: 0.30 M N₂ (0.60 M N) + 0.20 M NH₃ (0.20 M N) = 0.80 M N ✓. H atoms: initial 1.20 mol H₂ in 2L = 1.20 M H₂ → 2.40 M H. Final: 0.30 M H₂ (0.60 M H) + 0.20 M NH₃ (0.60 M H) = 1.20 M H? Error! Recalculate: 0.30 M H₂ = 0.60 M H atoms, 0.20 M NH₃ = 0.60 M H atoms, total 1.20 M H atoms. But initial was 2.40 M H atoms. Mistake: 1.20 mol H₂ in 2.0 L = 0.60 M H₂, not 1.20 M. Correct: initial [H₂] = 0.60 M → 1.20 M H atoms. Final: 0.30 M H₂ (0.60 M H) + 0.20 M NH₃ (0.60 M H) = 1.20 M H ✓.
- Step 5: Confirm dynamic equilibrium: all concentrations constant > 0, both forward and reverse reactions occurring at equal rates.
REASONING: ICE tables track stoichiometric changes from initial to equilibrium. The 1:3:2 mole ratio dictates concentration changes. Mass balance verification catches stoichiometry errors. Non-zero equilibrium concentrations of all species confirm dynamic (not complete) reaction.
ANSWER: [N₂] = 0.30 M, [H₂] = 0.30 M, [NH₃] = 0.20 M; equilibrium reached from forward direction.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: For the equilibrium PCl₅(g) ⇌ PCl₃(g) + Cl₂(g), initially 0.50 mol PCl₅ is placed in a 5.0 L flask. At equilibrium, the total pressure is 1.2 atm at 300 K. Calculate the degree of dissociation (α) of PCl₅ and the equilibrium concentrations. (R = 0.0821 L atm mol⁻¹ K⁻¹)
STEPS:
- Step 1: Initial moles: n_PCl₅ = 0.50, n_PCl₃ = 0, n_Cl₂ = 0. Volume = 5.0 L. Let α = fraction dissociated.
- Step 2: At equilibrium: n_PCl₅ = 0.50(1-α), n_PCl₃ = 0.50α, n_Cl₂ = 0.50α. Total moles = 0.50(1-α) + 0.50α + 0.50α = 0.50(1+α).
- Step 3: Use ideal gas law: PV = n_total RT → 1.2 × 5.0 = 0.50(1+α) × 0.0821 × 300. Solve: 6.0 = 0.50(1+α) × 24.63 = 12.315(1+α). → 1+α = 6.0/12.315 = 0.487? Impossible (α negative). Recheck: 0.50 × 0.0821 × 300 = 12.315. 6.0 = 12.315(1+α) → 1+α = 0.487 → α = -0.513. Error: initial pressure if no dissociation: P = nRT/V = 0.50×0.0821×300/5 = 2.463 atm. But equilibrium pressure is 1.2 atm < 2.463? That's impossible for a dissociation reaction (moles increase). Problem data inconsistent. Revise: Assume total pressure at equilibrium is 3.0 atm (typical textbook value). Then 3.0×5 = 0.50(1+α)×24.63 → 15 = 12.315(1+α) → 1+α = 1.218 → α = 0.218.
- Step 4: With α = 0.218: [PCl₅] = 0.50(1-0.218)/5 = 0.0782 M, [PCl₃] = [Cl₂] = 0.50×0.218/5 = 0.0218 M.
- Step 5: Verify: total moles = 0.50(1+0.218) = 0.609 mol. P = 0.609×0.0821×300/5 = 3.00 atm ✓.
REASONING: Degree of dissociation α links stoichiometry to measurable total pressure. Gas-phase equilibria often use pressure data. The ideal gas law converts between moles and pressure. Inconsistent data in problems must be identified — a key exam skill.
ANSWER: α = 0.218 (with corrected P_total = 3.0 atm); [PCl₅] = 0.078 M, [PCl₃] = [Cl₂] = 0.022 M.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: The reaction 2NO₂(g) ⇌ N₂O₄(g) is at equilibrium in a 1.0 L vessel at 298 K with [NO₂] = 0.020 M and [N₂O₄] = 0.040 M. The volume is suddenly halved to 0.50 L (pressure doubled). Predict the immediate effect on concentrations and the direction of net reaction to re-establish equilibrium. (This connects to Le Chatelier's principle in c11_chem_cbse_07_006).
STEPS:
- Step 1: Before volume change: [NO₂] = 0.020 M, [N₂O₄] = 0.040 M. Equilibrium constant Kc = [N₂O₄]/[NO₂]² = 0.040/(0.020)² = 100 M⁻¹.
- Step 2: Volume halved → concentrations instantaneously double (moles same, V halved): [NO₂]' = 0.040 M, [N₂O₄]' = 0.080 M.
- Step 3: Calculate reaction quotient Qc immediately after compression: Qc = [N₂O₄]'/[NO₂]'² = 0.080/(0.040)² = 0.080/0.0016 = 50 M⁻¹.
- Step 4: Compare Qc (50) with Kc (100): Qc < Kc, so net reaction proceeds forward (toward N₂O₄) to reach new equilibrium.
- Step 5: Qualitative Le Chatelier: increasing pressure favors side with fewer gas moles (1 mol N₂O₄ vs 2 mol NO₂) — consistent with forward shift.
- Step 6: New equilibrium will have higher [N₂O₄] and lower [NO₂] than the instantaneous doubled values, but both higher than original due to compression.
REASONING: Volume change instantly alters concentrations (Qc), not Kc. The system responds to restore Qc = Kc. This bridges dynamic equilibrium understanding to quantitative prediction (Q vs K) and Le Chatelier's principle.
ANSWER: Instant concentrations: [NO₂] = 0.040 M, [N₂O₄] = 0.080 M; Qc = 50 < Kc = 100; net reaction shifts forward (toward N₂O₄).

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A student sets up the equilibrium H₂(g) + I₂(g) ⇌ 2HI(g) in a 1 L vessel at 400°C. Initially, 1.0 mol H₂ and 1.0 mol I₂ are present. At equilibrium, the mixture contains 1.56 mol HI. The student concludes: "Since 1.56 mol HI formed, the reaction is 78% complete, so equilibrium lies far to the right." Evaluate this conclusion. Is the reaction "78% complete" a valid description of equilibrium position? Explain using the dynamic equilibrium concept.
COMPETENCY: Interpreting equilibrium position vs reaction completion
STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate equilibrium concentrations: [HI] = 1.56 M, [H₂] = [I₂] = (1.0 - 0.78) = 0.22 M (since 1.56 mol HI requires 0.78 mol H₂ and 0.78 mol I₂).
- Step 2: Note all three species have significant concentrations at equilibrium (0.22 M, 0.22 M, 1.56 M).
- Step 3: "78% complete" implies 22% reactants remain unreacted — but at equilibrium, forward and reverse rates are equal, not zero. The reaction hasn't "stopped" at 78%.
- Step 4: The equilibrium constant Kc = [HI]²/([H₂][I₂]) = (1.56)²/(0.22×0.22) = 50.2. This large Kc indicates products favored, but reactants still present and actively reacting.
- Step 5: Dynamic equilibrium means continuous molecular exchange: H₂ + I₂ → 2HI and 2HI → H₂ + I₂ occur at equal rates. "Percent completion" is a static, irreversible-reaction concept.
ANSWER: The conclusion is misleading. While 78% of limiting reactant converted, equilibrium is not a "completion" state. All species coexist and react dynamically. Kc = 50.2 confirms product-favored but not complete. The term "extent of reaction" (ξ) is better than "% complete."

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Consider two equilibria at 300 K in separate 1 L vessels: (1) N₂O₄(g) ⇌ 2NO₂(g) with Kc = 0.15, starting with 0.10 M N₂O₄. (2) 2NO₂(g) ⇌ N₂O₄(g) with Kc = 6.67, starting with 0.20 M NO₂. Without calculating exact equilibrium concentrations, determine which system has a higher [NO₂] at equilibrium. Justify using dynamic equilibrium principles and the relationship between the two Kc values.
COMPETENCY: Relating equilibrium constants of reverse reactions and predicting relative concentrations
STEPS:
- Step 1: Recognize reaction (2) is the reverse of reaction (1). Kc(reverse) = 1/Kc(forward) = 1/0.15 = 6.67 ✓.
- Step 2: System 1 starts with only N₂O₄ (0.10 M), so net reaction proceeds forward (dissociation) to produce NO₂.
- Step 3: System 2 starts with only NO₂ (0.20 M), so net reaction proceeds reverse (association) to produce N₂O₄, consuming NO₂.
- Step 4: At equilibrium, both systems must satisfy the same concentration ratio: [NO₂]²/[N₂O₄] = 0.15.
- Step 5: System 1: initial [N₂O₄] = 0.10 M, produces some NO₂. System 2: initial [NO₂] = 0.20 M, consumes some NO₂.
- Step 6: Since Kc = 0.15 < 1, equilibrium favors N₂O₄ (reactant in forward direction). System 1 starts with pure favored species (N₂O₄), so little dissociation → low [NO₂]. System 2 starts with pure disfavored species (NO₂), so extensive association → [NO₂] drops significantly but from higher initial value.
- Step 7: Compare: System 2 initial [NO₂] = 0.20 M. Even after shifting left, its equilibrium [NO₂] > System 1's equilibrium [NO₂] (which starts at 0 and increases slightly). Quantitatively: System 1: let x = [NO₂] at eq, then [N₂O₄] = 0.10 - x/2. x²/(0.10 - x/2) = 0.15 → x ≈ 0.11 M. System 2: let 2y = NO₂ consumed, [NO₂] = 0.20 - 2y, [N₂O₄] = y. (0.20-2y)²/y = 0.15 → y ≈ 0.06, [NO₂] ≈ 0.08 M. Wait — System 1 has higher [NO₂]! The intuitive "starts with more" fails because equilibrium ratio is fixed. Must calculate.
- Step 8: Correct conclusion: System 1 (starts with N₂O₄) reaches higher [NO₂] (~0.11 M) than System 2 (~0.08 M) because the equilibrium constant favors N₂O₄, so starting with N₂O₄ allows more NO₂ to form than remains when starting with NO₂.
ANSWER: System 1 (starting with N₂O₄) has higher equilibrium [NO₂] (~0.11 M) than System 2 (~0.08 M). The fixed Kc = 0.15 means [NO₂]²/[N₂O₄] = 0.15 at equilibrium. Starting with the favored species (N₂O₄) yields more product (NO₂) at equilibrium than starting with the disfavored species loses.
DIFFICULTY: hard
