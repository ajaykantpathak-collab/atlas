## DEFINITION
The dipole moment (μ) of a polar covalent bond is the product of the magnitude of the partial charge (δ) on either atom and the distance (r) between the centres of positive and negative charge: μ = δ × r. It is a vector quantity directed from the positive centre to the negative centre by convention, but represented in chemistry by a crossed arrow (➤) pointing from positive to negative end on the Lewis structure, with the cross on the positive end. The SI unit is coulomb metre (C·m); the commonly used unit is the Debye (D), where 1 D = 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m.

## WALKTHROUGH
Observe how a stream of water from a tap bends towards a charged plastic comb rubbed through dry hair — the water molecule acts like a tiny magnet with a positive and negative end. This happens because oxygen pulls the shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen, creating a charge separation. Recall from Class 9 (c9_chem_ch3_4) that covalent bonds involve electron sharing, but here the sharing is unequal. Start by identifying the more electronegative atom in a bond (using periodic trends from Class 10, c10_chem_cbse_05_010). Then determine molecular shape using VSEPR theory (c11_chem_cbse_04_005). Finally, add bond dipoles as vectors — if they cancel by symmetry, net μ = 0 (non-polar); if not, the molecule is polar. This vector addition uses trigonometry (c10_math_cbse_08_001) where the resultant of two equal dipoles at angle θ is μ_net = 2μ_bond cos(θ/2).

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Define dipole moment and state its SI unit. Why is the crossed arrow representation used in chemistry instead of the conventional vector arrow?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Dipole moment (μ) = charge (q) × distance of separation (r); SI unit = C·m. The crossed arrow (➤) points from δ+ to δ− with the cross on the δ+ end, opposite to the physics convention (vector from − to +). A wrong answer (e.g., stating unit as Debye only, or drawing arrow from negative to positive) reveals confusion between physics and chemistry conventions and ignorance of the 1 D = 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m conversion.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why CO₂ has zero net dipole moment while H₂O has a net dipole moment of 1.85 D, even though both contain polar bonds. Use molecular geometry in your explanation.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
CO₂ is linear (O=C=O, 180°); the two equal C=O bond dipoles are opposite and cancel vectorially → μ_net = 0. H₂O is bent (∠HOH ≈ 104.5°); the two O−H bond dipoles add at an angle, giving a resultant along the bisector. A wrong answer (e.g., "CO₂ has no polar bonds" or "H₂O is linear") shows failure to connect VSEPR geometry to vector addition of dipoles.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
The bond dipole moment of H−F is 1.91 D and the H−F bond length is 92 pm. Calculate the partial charge (δ) on each atom in units of elementary charge (e). (1 D = 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m; e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
μ = 1.91 D = 1.91 × 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ = 6.372 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m. r = 92 pm = 92 × 10⁻¹² m. δ = μ/r = 6.372×10⁻³⁰ / 92×10⁻¹² = 6.926×10⁻²⁰ C. In units of e: δ/e = 6.926×10⁻²⁰ / 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ = 0.432 e. A wrong answer (e.g., forgetting unit conversion, using 1 D = 10⁻¹⁸ esu·cm incorrectly) reveals poor dimensional analysis and confusion between Debye and SI units.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the dipole moments of NH₃ (1.47 D) and NF₃ (0.23 D). Both are pyramidal with a lone pair on nitrogen. Explain the large difference using the direction of the lone pair dipole relative to the bond dipoles.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
In NH₃, N is more electronegative than H; each N−H bond dipole points N←H. The lone pair dipole points in the same direction as the resultant of the three N−H dipoles (away from H atoms), adding constructively → large μ. In NF₃, F is more electronegative than N; each N−F dipole points N→F. The lone pair dipole points opposite to the resultant of the three N−F dipoles, partially cancelling → small μ. A wrong answer (e.g., attributing difference only to electronegativity difference) misses the vector opposition of lone pair dipole.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student claims: "BF₃ has polar B−F bonds, so it must have a net dipole moment." Evaluate this statement and correct the error.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is false. BF₃ is trigonal planar (sp², 120°). The three equal B−F bond dipoles are symmetrically arranged at 120°; their vector sum is zero. Polarity of individual bonds does not guarantee molecular polarity — geometry determines whether bond dipoles cancel. A wrong answer accepting the claim shows inability to apply symmetry arguments and vector addition to molecular shape.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple experiment using household items to demonstrate that water is a polar molecule but carbon tetrachloride (CCl₄) is non-polar. Explain the expected observations.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Charge a plastic ruler by rubbing with wool. Bring it near a thin stream of water from a tap — the stream bends toward the ruler. Repeat with CCl₄ (or cooking oil as proxy) — no bending. Water's net dipole aligns with the external field; CCl₄'s tetrahedral symmetry gives μ_net = 0, so no alignment. A poor answer (e.g., "both bend" or "neither bends") reveals no understanding of molecular polarity's macroscopic manifestation.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think that "polar bonds always make a polar molecule" and incorrectly predict NH₃ and NF₃ to have similar dipole moments because both are pyramidal. They also confuse the direction of the dipole moment vector: in chemistry the crossed arrow points from δ+ to δ− (cross on +), but in physics the vector points from − to +. Another error is using bond length in pm without converting to metres when calculating δ in coulombs from μ in Debye.

## THIS_FORMULA
μ = q × r (for a diatomic bond); μ_net = Σ μ_i (vector sum for polyatomic); μ(D) = 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ × μ(C·m); Percent ionic character ≈ (μ_observed / μ_100%_ionic) × 100

## FORMULA_VARIABLES
μ = dipole moment (vector); q = magnitude of partial charge (C); r = distance between charge centres (m); μ_i = individual bond dipole moment vectors; μ_observed = experimental dipole moment; μ_100%_ionic = theoretical dipole moment if bond were fully ionic (q = e, r = bond length).

## FORMULA_UNITS
μ: C·m (SI) or Debye (D); 1 D = 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m; q: coulomb (C) or elementary charge (e); r: metre (m) or picometre (pm); bond angles: degrees (°) or radians.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Calculate the net dipole moment of H₂O given that each O−H bond dipole moment is 1.51 D and the H−O−H bond angle is 104.5°. Express the answer in Debye and in C·m.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the geometry — H₂O is bent (VSEPR: 2 bond pairs + 2 lone pairs → tetrahedral electron geometry, bent molecular shape). The two O−H bond dipoles are equal in magnitude (μ_OH = 1.51 D) and symmetrically arranged at angle θ = 104.5°.
- Step 2: Set up vector addition — place the molecule in the xy-plane with the bisector of the H−O−H angle along the +x axis. Each bond dipole makes an angle of θ/2 = 52.25° with the x-axis.
- Step 3: Resolve each bond dipole into x and y components. For the upper O−H bond: μ_x = μ_OH cos(52.25°), μ_y = μ_OH sin(52.25°). For the lower O−H bond: μ_x = μ_OH cos(52.25°), μ_y = −μ_OH sin(52.25°).
- Step 4: Sum components — μ_net,x = 2 × 1.51 × cos(52.25°) = 2 × 1.51 × 0.611 = 1.845 D; μ_net,y = 1.51 × sin(52.25°) − 1.51 × sin(52.25°) = 0.
- Step 5: Net dipole moment magnitude μ_net = √(μ_net,x² + μ_net,y²) = 1.845 D ≈ 1.85 D. Direction: along the bisector, from H atoms toward O (since O is δ−).
- Step 6: Convert to SI: μ_net = 1.845 D × 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m/D = 6.155 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m.
REASONING: The vector addition uses the symmetry of the bent molecule — y-components cancel, x-components add. The resultant lies along the symmetry axis (bisector). This matches the experimental value of 1.85 D, confirming the model.
ANSWER: μ_net = 1.85 D = 6.16 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m, directed along the bisector of the H−O−H angle toward the oxygen atom.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: The dipole moment of HF is 1.91 D and the bond length is 92 pm. Calculate the percent ionic character of the H−F bond. (Assume 100% ionic character would mean a full charge of +e on H and −e on F separated by 92 pm.)
STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate the theoretical dipole moment for 100% ionic character: μ_ionic = e × r = (1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) × (92 × 10⁻¹² m) = 1.474 × 10⁻²⁹ C·m.
- Step 2: Convert μ_ionic to Debye: μ_ionic = 1.474 × 10⁻²⁹ / 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ = 4.42 D.
- Step 3: The observed dipole moment μ_obs = 1.91 D (given).
- Step 4: Percent ionic character = (μ_obs / μ_ionic) × 100 = (1.91 / 4.42) × 100 = 43.2%.
- Step 5: Interpret — the H−F bond is 43% ionic, 57% covalent. This aligns with Pauling's electronegativity difference (ΔEN = 4.0 − 2.1 = 1.9) predicting ~45% ionic character.
- Step 6: Note that the direction of the dipole is Hδ+ → Fδ−, consistent with fluorine's higher electronegativity.
REASONING: The calculation compares the actual charge separation (from measured μ) to the maximum possible charge separation (full electron transfer). The result is a measure of bond polarity on the covalent-ionic continuum.
ANSWER: Percent ionic character of H−F = 43.2%.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Predict whether the following molecules have a net dipole moment. For polar molecules, indicate the direction of the net dipole. (a) SF₄ (see-saw shape), (b) XeF₄ (square planar), (c) ClF₃ (T-shaped). Use VSEPR to determine geometry first.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Determine VSEPR geometry for each. (a) SF₄: 5 electron domains (4 bonding + 1 lone pair) → trigonal bipyramidal electron geometry, see-saw molecular shape. Lone pair occupies equatorial position. (b) XeF₄: 6 electron domains (4 bonding + 2 lone pairs) → octahedral electron geometry, square planar molecular shape. Lone pairs opposite each other (axial). (c) ClF₃: 5 electron domains (3 bonding + 2 lone pairs) → trigonal bipyramidal electron geometry, T-shaped molecular shape. Lone pairs occupy equatorial positions.
- Step 2: Draw each molecule with bond dipoles. All X−F bonds are polar (F more electronegative), dipoles point toward F.
- Step 3: Analyze symmetry and vector sum. (a) SF₄: Two axial S−F bonds are opposite (180°) → their dipoles cancel. Two equatorial S−F bonds are at ~120° to each other and to the lone pair; their dipoles do not fully cancel because the lone pair distorts angles. Net dipole along the bisector of the equatorial bonds, toward the equatorial F atoms. POLAR.
- Step 4: (b) XeF₄: Four Xe−F bonds in a plane at 90° to each other, symmetric. Each dipole has an opposite counterpart → vector sum = 0. NON-POLAR.
- Step 5: (c) ClF₃: Two axial Cl−F bonds opposite (180°) → cancel. One equatorial Cl−F bond remains; its dipole is not cancelled. Net dipole along the equatorial Cl−F bond, toward F. POLAR.
- Step 6: Verify with known data: SF₄ μ = 0.63 D, XeF₄ μ = 0, ClF₃ μ = 0.60 D. The predictions match.
REASONING: Molecular polarity depends on whether bond dipoles cancel by symmetry. Lone pairs affect geometry and may contribute their own dipole, but the key is vector addition of all bond dipoles in the actual 3D arrangement.
ANSWER: (a) SF₄: polar, net dipole along equatorial bisector toward F atoms. (b) XeF₄: non-polar (μ = 0). (c) ClF₃: polar, net dipole along equatorial Cl−F bond toward F.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: The dipole moment of NH₃ is 1.47 D and that of NF₃ is 0.23 D. Both molecules are pyramidal with bond angles ~107°. Using vector addition and the direction of bond dipoles and lone pair dipole, explain why NF₃ has a much smaller dipole moment despite the greater electronegativity difference in N−F bonds compared to N−H bonds.
COMPETENCY: Explaining molecular polarity using vector addition of bond dipoles and lone pair contributions
STEPS:
- Step 1: Determine the direction of each bond dipole. In NH₃, N (EN = 3.0) > H (EN = 2.1) → dipole points H → N. In NF₃, F (EN = 4.0) > N (EN = 3.0) → dipole points N → F.
- Step 2: Determine the direction of the lone pair dipole. The lone pair on N creates a dipole pointing away from N (negative charge concentrated on the lone pair side).
- Step 3: In NH₃, the three N−H bond dipoles have a resultant pointing along the symmetry axis from the centre of the H triangle toward N. The lone pair dipole points in the SAME direction (away from the H plane). They ADD constructively.
- Step 4: In NF₃, the three N−F bond dipoles have a resultant pointing from N toward the centre of the F triangle (opposite to the lone pair direction). The lone pair dipole points opposite to the bond dipole resultant. They PARTIALLY CANCEL.
- Step 5: The large electronegativity difference in N−F makes individual bond dipoles larger, but the cancellation by the lone pair dipole reduces the net moment drastically.
ANSWER: In NH₃, bond dipoles and lone pair dipole align in the same direction (constructive addition → large μ). In NF₃, bond dipoles point opposite to the lone pair dipole (destructive cancellation → small μ), despite larger individual bond dipoles.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A molecule AB₃ has a net dipole moment of 1.85 D. The A−B bond length is 100 pm and the electronegativity difference suggests each A−B bond has 20% ionic character. The molecule could be trigonal planar (BF₃-type) or trigonal pyramidal (NH₃-type). Determine the geometry and calculate the bond dipole moment of each A−B bond. Justify your answer.
COMPETENCY: Using dipole moment data to deduce molecular geometry and calculate bond polarity
STEPS:
- Step 1: If trigonal planar (120°), three equal bond dipoles at 120° → vector sum = 0. But μ_net = 1.85 D ≠ 0 → geometry CANNOT be trigonal planar.
- Step 2: Therefore geometry is trigonal pyramidal (like NH₃) with bond angle ~107°. The three bond dipoles have a resultant along the symmetry axis.
- Step 3: For pyramidal AB₃ with bond angle θ, the resultant μ_net = 3 μ_bond cos(α), where α is the angle between each bond and the symmetry axis. For tetrahedral electron geometry, α = 180° − 109.5° = 70.5°? Wait — better: the angle between bond and symmetry axis is (180° − θ)/2? Actually for NH₃, the angle between N−H bond and the C₃ axis is about 68°. Let's derive: cos(α) = √(1/3) for perfect tetrahedron? No — use the formula μ_net = μ_bond √(1 + 2 cos θ) for three symmetric vectors at angle θ to each other. For θ = 107°, μ_net = μ_bond √(1 + 2 cos 107°) = μ_bond √(1 + 2(−0.292)) = μ_bond √(0.416) = 0.645 μ_bond.
- Step 4: So μ_bond = μ_net / 0.645 = 1.85 / 0.645 = 2.87 D.
- Step 5: Check ionic character: μ_ionic = e × r = 4.8 D (for 100 pm, 1e charge). 20% of 4.8 D = 0.96 D. But calculated μ_bond = 2.87 D > 0.96 D. Contradiction? Wait — the 20% ionic character refers to the bond, but the net dipole includes the lone pair contribution! In NH₃-type molecules, the lone pair dipole adds to the bond dipole resultant. So μ_bond from step 4 is not the pure bond dipole — it's an "effective" value. The question likely expects: geometry = pyramidal, and μ_bond ≈ 0.96 D (from 20% ionic character), with the rest coming from lone pair. But the net is 1.85 D. Let's recalculate properly.
- Step 6: Actual bond dipole from ionic character: 20% of 4.8 D = 0.96 D. For pyramidal, resultant of three bond dipoles = 0.96 × √(1 + 2 cos 107°) = 0.96 × 0.645 = 0.62 D. The lone pair dipole must contribute the rest: μ_lp ≈ 1.85 − 0.62 = 1.23 D (in same direction). This is consistent with NH₃ where lone pair dipole dominates.
- Step 7: Final answer: Geometry is trigonal pyramidal. Bond dipole moment = 0.96 D (from 20% ionic character). Net dipole = 1.85 D arises from vector sum of bond dipoles (0.62 D) plus lone pair dipole (~1.23 D).
ANSWER: Geometry = trigonal pyramidal (not planar, because planar would give μ = 0). Each A−B bond dipole = 0.96 D (20% ionic character). The net dipole of 1.85 D is the vector sum of the three bond dipoles (resultant ~0.62 D along symmetry axis) plus the lone pair dipole (~1.23 D in the same direction).
