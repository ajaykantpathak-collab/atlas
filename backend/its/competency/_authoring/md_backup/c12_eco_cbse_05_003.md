## DEFINITION
A shift in supply occurs when a non-price determinant of supply (such as input prices, technology, number of sellers, expectations, or government policies) changes, causing the entire supply curve to move rightward (increase in supply) or leftward (decrease in supply) at every price level, leading to a new market equilibrium with a different equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
analyze

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can (1) graphically demonstrate and verbally explain the directional changes in equilibrium price and quantity for both rightward and leftward supply shifts, (2) mathematically solve for new equilibrium price and quantity given linear supply and demand equations after a supply shift, and (3) distinguish supply-shift effects from demand-shift effects and simultaneous-shift scenarios in competitive exam questions.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers only the effect of a **unilateral shift in supply** (ceteris paribus) on equilibrium price and quantity in a perfectly competitive market. It does NOT cover simultaneous shifts in demand and supply (node c12_eco_cbse_05_004), government interventions like taxes/subsidies (node c12_eco_cbse_05_005), or price controls (nodes c12_eco_cbse_05_006, c12_eco_cbse_05_007).

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you run a small wheat flour (atta) mill in Punjab. Last year, you bought wheat at ₹2,200 per quintal; this year, a bumper harvest pushes the wheat price down to ₹1,900 per quintal. Your cost of production falls, so you are willing to supply more flour at every possible selling price — your supply curve shifts **rightward**. Walk through the market: at the old equilibrium price, there is now excess supply (surplus); sellers compete by lowering price; as price falls, quantity demanded expands (movement along demand curve) and quantity supplied contracts (movement along new supply curve) until a new equilibrium is reached. The core principle: **a rightward supply shift lowers equilibrium price but raises equilibrium quantity; a leftward shift raises equilibrium price but lowers equilibrium quantity** — the exact opposite of a rightward demand shift.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Define "increase in supply" and "decrease in supply" using the concept of a shift of the supply curve. How do they differ from "expansion in supply" and "contraction in supply"?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
**Correct answer:** An **increase in supply** is a rightward shift of the entire supply curve — at every price, producers are willing to sell a larger quantity — caused by non-price factors (e.g., lower input costs, better technology). A **decrease in supply** is a leftward shift — at every price, producers offer less — caused by adverse non-price factors (e.g., higher input costs, bad weather). **Expansion in supply** and **contraction in supply** refer to movements *along* a given supply curve due to a change in the good's own price (law of supply), not a shift of the curve.  
**What a wrong answer reveals:** If the student says "increase in supply means quantity supplied goes up when price rises," they are confusing a **movement along the curve** (expansion) with a **shift of the curve** (increase). This is the single most common Class 12 error — treating a change in quantity supplied as a change in supply.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why a rightward shift in supply leads to a **fall in equilibrium price** but a **rise in equilibrium quantity**. Use the concepts of surplus, price adjustment, and movements along the demand curve in your explanation.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
**Correct answer:** At the original equilibrium price, a rightward supply shift creates **excess supply (surplus)** because quantity supplied now exceeds quantity demanded. To clear the surplus, sellers lower the price. As price falls, two things happen simultaneously: (1) **quantity demanded expands** (movement *down* along the downward-sloping demand curve — law of demand), and (2) **quantity supplied contracts** (movement *down* along the new supply curve — law of supply). The price keeps falling until the new quantity demanded equals the new quantity supplied. The new equilibrium has a **lower price** (due to the initial surplus) and a **higher quantity** (because the demand curve is downward sloping: lower price → higher quantity demanded).  
**What a wrong answer reveals:** If the student claims "equilibrium quantity falls because supply increased," they have reversed the direction of the movement along the demand curve. If they say "price rises to clear the surplus," they misunderstand the market-clearing mechanism.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
The market for onions in Maharashtra is described by the following linear equations (quantity in quintals, price in ₹/kg):  
Demand: Qd = 500 – 20P  
Supply: Qs = 100 + 10P  
A new irrigation technology reduces production costs, shifting the supply curve to Qs' = 160 + 10P.  
Calculate the initial equilibrium price and quantity, the new equilibrium price and quantity, and the absolute changes in both.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
**Step-by-step solution:**  
1. **Initial equilibrium:** Set Qd = Qs → 500 – 20P = 100 + 10P → 30P = 400 → P₁ = ₹13.33/kg.  
   Q₁ = 500 – 20(13.33) = 500 – 266.67 = **233.33 quintals**.  
2. **New equilibrium after supply shift:** Set Qd = Qs' → 500 – 20P = 160 + 10P → 30P = 340 → P₂ = ₹11.33/kg.  
   Q₂ = 500 – 20(11.33) = 500 – 226.67 = **273.33 quintals**.  
3. **Changes:** ΔP = P₂ – P₁ = 11.33 – 13.33 = **–₹2.00/kg (price falls)**.  
   ΔQ = Q₂ – Q₁ = 273.33 – 233.33 = **+40 quintals (quantity rises)**.  
**What a wrong answer reveals:** If the student gets ΔP positive, they likely set Qs = Qs' instead of Qd = Qs'. If they get ΔQ negative, they may have used the wrong demand equation or mis-signed the shift. A common competitive-exam trap: forgetting that the demand curve *does not shift* — only the supply intercept changes.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the effects of a **rightward shift in supply** versus a **rightward shift in demand** on equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity. Construct a table showing the direction of change (↑/↓/ambiguous) for each variable in each case. Explain why the price effects are opposite while the quantity effects are in the same direction.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
**Correct answer:**

| Shift Type | Equilibrium Price | Equilibrium Quantity |
|------------|-------------------|----------------------|
| Rightward Supply Shift | ↓ (falls) | ↑ (rises) |
| Rightward Demand Shift | ↑ (rises) | ↑ (rises) |

**Explanation:** Both shifts increase the equilibrium quantity because in each case the *new* intersection occurs at a higher quantity on the horizontal axis. However, a rightward supply shift creates a **surplus at the old price**, forcing price **down**; a rightward demand shift creates a **shortage at the old price**, pulling price **up**. The demand curve slopes downward, so a lower price (supply shift) increases quantity demanded, while a higher price (demand shift) decreases quantity demanded — but the shift itself dominates, so quantity still rises in both cases.  
**What a wrong answer reveals:** If the student says "both shifts raise price" or "both lower quantity," they have not internalised the surplus/shortage mechanism. If they say "quantity change is ambiguous for supply shift," they are confusing this with *simultaneous shifts* (covered in the next node).

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A news report states: "Due to a severe heatwave, the supply of mangoes in Uttar Pradesh has decreased, causing mango prices to rise. The higher prices will encourage farmers to plant more mango trees next year, which will increase supply and bring prices back down." Evaluate this statement using the distinction between a **shift in supply** and a **movement along the supply curve**. Identify the economic error in the reporter's reasoning.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
**Correct answer:** The reporter commits the **"supply response to price" fallacy**. The heatwave causes a **leftward shift in supply** (decrease in supply) — at every price, fewer mangoes are available. This shift raises the equilibrium price (movement *up* along the demand curve). The higher price then induces a **movement along the *new* supply curve** (expansion in quantity supplied), *not* a shift of the supply curve. Planting more trees is a **long-run supply response** (a future rightward shift of supply due to increased capacity), but it occurs in a *future* season, not instantaneously. The reporter incorrectly treats the current price-induced quantity change as a current supply shift that will "bring prices back down" in the same period. In the current market period, supply is fixed by the harvest; only quantity supplied responds to price.  
**What a wrong answer reveals:** If the student agrees with the reporter, they conflate **short-run movements along the supply curve** with **long-run shifts of the supply curve**. This is a classic JEE/NEET trap: confusing the *time horizon* of supply adjustments.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Construct a real-world Indian market scenario (different from wheat, onions, or mangoes) where a **leftward shift in supply** occurs due to a **government policy change**. Draw the initial and new supply curves on a labeled diagram (describe the diagram in words), and trace the step-by-step adjustment from the old equilibrium to the new equilibrium, naming the surplus/shortage at each stage.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
**Expected answer (criteria for a good response):**  
- **Scenario example:** The government imposes a **stricter emission norm (BS-VI to BS-VII)** on two-wheeler manufacturers, raising production costs per unit.  
- **Diagram description:** Downward-sloping demand curve (D). Initial upward-sloping supply curve (S₁). New supply curve (S₂) shifted **leftward** (higher intercept, same slope if per-unit tax/cost increase is constant). Initial equilibrium E₁ (P₁, Q₁). New equilibrium E₂ (P₂ > P₁, Q₂ < Q₁).  
- **Step-by-step adjustment:**  
  1. At P₁, S₂ shows lower quantity supplied than D → **shortage (excess demand)** of (Qd – Qs₂) at P₁.  
  2. Buyers compete, bidding price up.  
  3. As price rises: (a) quantity demanded **contracts** (move up along D), (b) quantity supplied **expands** (move up along S₂).  
  4. Price rises until shortage vanishes at E₂.  
  5. Result: **Higher equilibrium price, lower equilibrium quantity.**  
**What a poor answer reveals:** If the student draws the shift rightward, they misread "stricter norm raises cost" as "increases supply." If they describe a surplus instead of a shortage, they confuse leftward supply shift with rightward supply shift. If they say "demand shifts," they violate ceteris paribus.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
**Misconception:** "When supply increases, price falls, so producers earn less revenue — therefore producers would never voluntarily adopt cost-saving technology."  
**Why it is wrong:** This confuses **equilibrium price** with **producer revenue**. A rightward supply shift *does* lower equilibrium price, but it *raises* equilibrium quantity. Total revenue (P × Q) may rise, fall, or stay the same depending on the **price elasticity of demand**. If demand is elastic (|Ed| > 1), the % increase in quantity outweighs the % fall in price → **total revenue increases**. Producers adopt technology precisely because it lowers *marginal cost*, raising *profit* even if price falls, as long as the new price exceeds the new marginal cost. The error is assuming revenue = price, ignoring the quantity effect.

## THIS_FORMULA
**Equilibrium condition:** Qd(P) = Qs(P)  
**After supply shift:** Qd(P) = Qs'(P) where Qs'(P) = Qs(P) + ΔS (ΔS > 0 for rightward shift, ΔS < 0 for leftward shift, assuming parallel shift)  
**Change in equilibrium:** ΔP* = P*_new – P*_old, ΔQ* = Q*_new – Q*_old

## FORMULA_VARIABLES
Qd = quantity demanded (function of price P), Qs = quantity supplied (function of price P), Qs' = new quantity supplied after shift, P = price (₹ per unit), Q = quantity (units), ΔS = vertical/horizontal shift magnitude (depends on specification), P*_old = initial equilibrium price, P*_new = new equilibrium price, Q*_old = initial equilibrium quantity, Q*_new = new equilibrium quantity.

## FORMULA_UNITS
Price: ₹ per unit (e.g., ₹/kg, ₹/quintal)  
Quantity: physical units (kg, quintals, tonnes, pieces)  
Shift magnitude ΔS: same units as quantity (if horizontal shift) or price (if vertical shift)

## COMPUTATION_TYPE
algebraic

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
**PROBLEM:** The market for **cotton yarn** in Tamil Nadu is initially in equilibrium. The demand curve is Qd = 1,200 – 40P (P in ₹/kg, Q in tonnes). The initial supply curve is Qs = 200 + 20P. The government announces a **subsidy of ₹10 per kg** to cotton yarn producers, effectively lowering their marginal cost by ₹10 at every output level. Assuming the subsidy leads to a parallel rightward shift of the supply curve, find: (a) initial equilibrium price and quantity, (b) new supply equation, (c) new equilibrium price and quantity, (d) the change in consumer surplus (approximate using triangle area).

**STEPS:**
- **Step 1: Identify the initial equilibrium.** Set Qd = Qs: 1,200 – 40P = 200 + 20P → 60P = 1,000 → P₁ = ₹16.67/kg. Substitute into Qd: Q₁ = 1,200 – 40(16.67) = 1,200 – 666.67 = **533.33 tonnes**.
- **Step 2: Determine the new supply equation after the ₹10/kg subsidy.** A per-unit subsidy to producers shifts the supply curve **rightward (downward vertically)** by the subsidy amount. At any given quantity, producers are willing to accept a price ₹10 lower. The inverse supply initially: P = (Qs – 200)/20 = 0.05Qs – 10. After subsidy: P' = 0.05Qs – 20. Convert back to direct form: Qs' = 20(P' + 20) + 200 = 20P' + 600. **New supply: Qs' = 600 + 20P**. (Alternatively: horizontal shift of +200 units at every price: Qs' = (200+200) + 20P = 600 + 20P.)
- **Step 3: Find the new equilibrium.** Set Qd = Qs': 1,200 – 40P = 600 + 20P → 60P = 600 → P₂ = **₹10/kg**. Q₂ = 1,200 – 40(10) = **800 tonnes**.
- **Step 4: Calculate changes.** ΔP = 10 – 16.67 = **–₹6.67/kg** (price falls). ΔQ = 800 – 533.33 = **+266.67 tonnes** (quantity rises).
- **Step 5: Approximate change in consumer surplus (CS).** Initial CS = ½ × (P_max – P₁) × Q₁. P_max (demand intercept) = 1,200/40 = ₹30. CS₁ = ½ × (30 – 16.67) × 533.33 = ½ × 13.33 × 533.33 ≈ **3,555.5 (₹-tonnes)**. New CS₂ = ½ × (30 – 10) × 800 = ½ × 20 × 800 = **8,000 (₹-tonnes)**. ΔCS = +4,444.5 (₹-tonnes) — consumers gain significantly.

**REASONING:** A per-unit subsidy reduces producers' effective marginal cost, shifting supply rightward. The equilibrium price falls by less than the subsidy (₹6.67 < ₹10) because demand is downward sloping — consumers capture part of the subsidy via lower price, producers capture the rest via higher quantity. The algebraic method ensures exact numerical answers required in board exams (4-6 mark questions) and CUET/JEE.

**ANSWER:** (a) P₁ = ₹16.67/kg, Q₁ = 533.33 tonnes. (b) Qs' = 600 + 20P. (c) P₂ = ₹10/kg, Q₂ = 800 tonnes. (d) ΔCS ≈ +4,444.5 ₹-tonnes (consumer surplus increases).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
**PROBLEM:** In the **apple market of Himachal Pradesh**, an unseasonal hailstorm destroys 30% of the standing crop. Before the storm, the market was described by:  
Demand: Qd = 800 – 50P (P in ₹/kg, Q in quintals)  
Supply: Qs = 100 + 25P  
The storm causes a **leftward shift in supply** such that at every price, quantity supplied is 30% lower than before.  
(a) Write the new supply equation.  
(b) Compute the initial and new equilibrium price and quantity.  
(c) Calculate the percentage change in equilibrium price and quantity.  
(d) If the demand curve were perfectly inelastic (vertical), what would happen to equilibrium price and quantity? Explain.

**STEPS:**
- **Step 1: Initial equilibrium.** Qd = Qs → 800 – 50P = 100 + 25P → 75P = 700 → P₁ = ₹9.33/kg. Q₁ = 800 – 50(9.33) = 800 – 466.67 = **333.33 quintals**.
- **Step 2: New supply after 30% crop loss.** "30% lower at every price" means a **multiplicative horizontal shift**: Qs' = 0.7 × Qs = 0.7(100 + 25P) = **70 + 17.5P**. (Note: This is a *rotation* if the shift is proportional, not a parallel shift. The slope changes from 25 to 17.5.)
- **Step 3: New equilibrium.** Qd = Qs' → 800 – 50P = 70 + 17.5P → 67.5P = 730 → P₂ = **₹10.81/kg**. Q₂ = 800 – 50(10.81) = 800 – 540.74 = **259.26 quintals**.
- **Step 4: Percentage changes.** %ΔP = (10.81 – 9.33)/9.33 × 100 = **+15.86%**. %ΔQ = (259.26 – 333.33)/333.33 × 100 = **–22.22%**.
- **Step 5: Perfectly inelastic demand scenario.** If demand is vertical at, say, Qd = 333.33 (initial quantity), then equilibrium quantity **cannot change** — it stays at 333.33 quintals. The leftward supply shift creates a shortage at the old price; price rises until quantity demanded (fixed) equals new quantity supplied. Solve 333.33 = 70 + 17.5P → P = **₹15.05/kg**. **Result: Price rises sharply (+61%), quantity unchanged.** The entire burden falls on consumers.

**REASONING:** This example tests two critical competitive-exam skills: (1) distinguishing **parallel shifts** (additive) from **proportional shifts** (multiplicative) — the latter changes the slope, which many students miss; (2) analyzing **extreme elasticity cases** (perfectly inelastic demand) which are favourite JEE/NEET questions. The percentage change calculation is standard for "percentage change" board questions.

**ANSWER:** (a) Qs' = 70 + 17.5P. (b) Initial: P₁ = ₹9.33/kg, Q₁ = 333.33 quintals; New: P₂ = ₹10.81/kg, Q₂ = 259.26 quintals. (c) %ΔP = +15.86%, %ΔQ = –22.22%. (d) Perfectly inelastic demand: Q* unchanged at 333.33 quintals, P* rises to ₹15.05/kg (+61.3%).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
**PROBLEM:** **Combining supply-shift analysis with elasticity concepts (Class 11 Microeconomics link).**  
The market for **generic paracetamol tablets** in India is highly competitive. Demand: Qd = 50,000 – 5,000P (P in ₹/strip, Q in thousand strips). Initial supply: Qs = 10,000 + 5,000P.  
(a) Compute initial equilibrium P₁, Q₁ and the **price elasticity of demand (Ed)** at equilibrium.  
(b) A new API (Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient) import duty raises marginal cost by ₹2 per strip, shifting supply **leftward parallel by 10,000 units** (i.e., Qs' = Qs – 10,000). Find new equilibrium P₂, Q₂.  
(c) Compute the **percentage change in total revenue (TR = P × Q)** for producers.  
(d) Explain why the **magnitude of the price increase** depends on the **elasticity of demand**, using the formula ΔP/P ≈ (1 / (|Ed| + Es)) × (ΔS/S) for small shifts, where Es is supply elasticity.

**STEPS:**
- **Step 1: Initial equilibrium and Ed.** Qd = Qs → 50,000 – 5,000P = 10,000 + 5,000P → 10,000P = 40,000 → P₁ = **₹4/strip**. Q₁ = 50,000 – 5,000(4) = **30,000 thousand strips**.  
  Ed = (dQd/dP) × (P/Q) = (–5,000) × (4/30,000) = **–0.667** (inelastic demand, |Ed| < 1).  
  Es = (dQs/dP) × (P/Q) = 5,000 × (4/30,000) = **0.667**.
- **Step 2: New supply after ₹2 duty (parallel leftward shift of 10,000).** Qs' = (10,000 – 10,000) + 5,000P = **5,000P**.  
  New equilibrium: 50,000 – 5,000P = 5,000P → 10,000P = 50,000 → P₂ = **₹5/strip**. Q₂ = 5,000(5) = **25,000 thousand strips**.
- **Step 3: Total revenue change.** TR₁ = 4 × 30,000 = **120,000 (₹-thousand strips)**. TR₂ = 5 × 25,000 = **125,000 (₹-thousand strips)**. %ΔTR = (125,000 – 120,000)/120,000 × 100 = **+4.17%**. **Producers' total revenue RISES despite lower quantity because demand is inelastic** — the 25% price rise outweighs the 16.7% quantity fall.
- **Step 4: Elasticity linkage.** The approximate formula for a small supply shift: %ΔP ≈ [Es / (|Ed| + Es)] × %ΔS (where %ΔS is the horizontal shift as % of initial Q). Here, %ΔS = –10,000/30,000 = –33.33%. %ΔP ≈ [0.667 / (0.667 + 0.667)] × (–33.33%) = 0.5 × (–33.33%) = –16.67%? Wait — sign convention: a leftward supply shift (negative %ΔS) causes a positive %ΔP. Correct formula: %ΔP = [Es / (|Ed| + Es)] × |%ΔS|. So %ΔP = 0.5 × 33.33% = 16.67% (actual: (5–4)/4 = 25% — approximation error because shift is large). **Key insight:** If |Ed| were larger (more elastic), the denominator (|Ed| + Es) would be larger → %ΔP smaller. With inelastic demand (|Ed| < Es), price rises more than quantity falls → TR rises.

**REASONING:** This worked example bridges **Class 12 market equilibrium** with **Class 11 elasticity** (c11_eco_cbse_... elasticity nodes) and introduces the **pass-through formula** used in JEE Advanced and CUET PG economics. It shows that the *distribution of the supply-shift burden* between price and quantity depends on relative elasticities — a core competitive-exam concept. The TR calculation tests whether students understand that "price up, quantity down" does not automatically mean "revenue down."

**ANSWER:** (a) P₁ = ₹4, Q₁ = 30,000 thousand strips, Ed = –0.667 (inelastic), Es = 0.667. (b) P₂ = ₹5, Q₂ = 25,000 thousand strips. (c) TR rises by 4.17%. (d) Price rise magnitude is inversely related to |Ed|; with inelastic demand, price bears most of the adjustment, raising producer revenue.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
**QUESTION:** The Government of India imposes a **per-unit tax of ₹5 per kg** on sugar producers. The initial market equilibrium price is ₹40/kg and quantity is 100 lakh tonnes. The price elasticity of demand is –0.5 and price elasticity of supply is 0.8 (both constant near equilibrium).  
(a) Using the **tax incidence formula**, calculate the approximate **increase in price paid by consumers** and the **decrease in price received by producers** (net of tax).  
(b) Determine the new equilibrium quantity.  
(c) Explain why the consumer bears a larger share of the tax burden in this case.  
(d) If the government instead gave a **per-unit subsidy of ₹5/kg**, how would your answers to (a) and (b) change in direction and magnitude?

**COMPETENCY:** Analyzing tax incidence using elasticity — applying supply-shift mechanics to policy evaluation.

**STEPS:**
- **Step 1: Recall the tax incidence formulas.** For a per-unit tax t, the price paid by consumers rises by ΔPd = [Es / (|Ed| + Es)] × t. The price received by producers (net of tax) falls by ΔPs = [|Ed| / (|Ed| + Es)] × t.
- **Step 2: Plug in values.** Es = 0.8, |Ed| = 0.5, t = ₹5.  
  ΔPd = [0.8 / (0.5 + 0.8)] × 5 = (0.8 / 1.3) × 5 = **₹3.08/kg**.  
  ΔPs = [0.5 / 1.3] × 5 = **₹1.92/kg**.  
  Check: ΔPd + ΔPs = 3.08 + 1.92 = ₹5 = t ✓.
- **Step 3: New prices and quantity.** Consumer price Pc = 40 + 3.08 = **₹43.08/kg**. Producer price Pp = 40 – 1.92 = **₹38.08/kg**.  
  %ΔQ ≈ –Es × (ΔPs/P) = –0.8 × (1.92/40) = –0.0384 = –3.84%.  
  New Q = 100 × (1 – 0.0384) = **96.16 lakh tonnes**. (Alternatively use demand elasticity: %ΔQ = Ed × (ΔPd/P) = –0.5 × (3.08/40) = –3.85% ≈ same.)
- **Step 4: Explain burden sharing.** Consumer share = ΔPd/t = 3.08/5 = 61.6%. Producer share = 38.4%. Consumers bear more because **demand is less elastic than supply** (|Ed| < Es) — consumers are less responsive to price changes, so they cannot easily reduce quantity demanded when price rises, forcing them to absorb more of the tax.
- **Step 5: Subsidy case.** A ₹5 subsidy shifts supply rightward. Consumer price **falls** by ΔPd = ₹3.08 (to ₹36.92). Producer price (net) **rises** by ΔPs = ₹1.92 (to ₹41.92). Quantity **increases** by ~3.84% (to ~103.84 lakh tonnes). Directions reverse; magnitudes stay the same (assuming constant elasticities).

**ANSWER:** (a) Consumer price ↑ ₹3.08/kg, producer price ↓ ₹1.92/kg. (b) New Q ≈ 96.16 lakh tonnes. (c) Consumers bear 61.6% of tax because |Ed| (0.5) < Es (0.8) — demand is more inelastic. (d) Subsidy: consumer price ↓ ₹3.08, producer price ↑ ₹1.92, quantity ↑ ~3.84%.

Read the following excerpt from the *Economic Survey 2023-24* and answer the questions that follow:  
> "The Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY) and the Modified National Agricultural Insurance Scheme have provided risk cover to farmers. However, the **effective supply response** to price signals in Indian agriculture remains constrained by **fragmented landholdings, inadequate irrigation, and credit bottlenecks**. In years of bumper harvest (e.g., wheat 2022-23), the **market price crashes below MSP**, leading to government procurement at MSP. In drought years (e.g., pulses 2015-16), **prices spike sharply** and imports are needed."  

Based on this excerpt and your knowledge of supply shifts:  
(a) Identify **two non-price determinants of supply** mentioned in the excerpt that cause **leftward shifts** in agricultural supply.  
(b) Explain why a **bumper harvest** (rightward supply shift) causes the market price to fall **below MSP** even though the government announces MSP *before* sowing. Use a diagram description (no drawing needed) showing the supply shift, the MSP line (price floor), and the resulting surplus.  
(c) The excerpt says "effective supply response to price signals remains constrained." What does this imply about the **price elasticity of supply (Es)** for Indian agriculture in the short run? How does a low Es affect the **magnitude of price spikes** during a drought (leftward supply shift)?  
(d) If the government wants to **stabilise prices** without maintaining large buffer stocks, suggest **one supply-side policy** (not demand-side) that would **increase Es** in the long run, and explain the mechanism.

**COMPETENCY:** Interpreting real-world policy text, linking supply determinants to elasticity, and applying supply-shift analysis to government intervention (MSP).

**STEPS:**
- **Step 1: Extract non-price determinants from text.** "Inadequate irrigation" (weather/rainfall dependency) and "credit bottlenecks" (access to inputs/technology) — both are non-price factors that shift supply leftward when adverse.
- **Step 2: Bumper harvest + MSP analysis.** MSP is a **price floor** (P_floor > equilibrium price without intervention). Bumper harvest = **large rightward supply shift**. At MSP, quantity supplied (Qs at MSP) far exceeds quantity demanded (Qd at MSP) → **large surplus**. Government must procure the surplus to maintain MSP. Diagram: D downward, S₁ → S₂ (rightward), horizontal line at MSP above new equilibrium. Surplus = Qs(MSP) – Qd(MSP).
- **Step 3: Low Es implication.** "Constrained supply response" means farmers **cannot easily increase output when prices rise** (e.g., during drought, they can't irrigate; during high prices, they can't expand area quickly). → **Short-run Es is low (inelastic, Es < 1)**. For a given leftward supply shift (drought), %ΔP = |%ΔS| / (|Ed| + Es). If Es is small, denominator ≈ |Ed| → %ΔP is **large**. Hence "prices spike sharply."

Mechanism: Irrigation reduces dependence on monsoon, allowing farmers to adjust cropping area and input use in response to price signals → supply becomes **more elastic (higher Es)**. With higher Es, future supply shifts (both good and bad monsoons) cause **smaller price fluctuations**, reducing the need for MSP procurement and imports.

**ANSWER:** (a) Inadequate irrigation (weather risk), credit bottlenecks (input access). (b) Bumper harvest shifts S rightward; at fixed MSP (price floor), Qs > Qd → surplus procured by govt. (c) Low short-run Es (inelastic); low Es amplifies price spikes for a given supply shock (%ΔP ∝ 1/Es). (d) Expand irrigation (PMKSY) → reduces weather dependence → farmers can respond to prices → Es rises → price volatility falls.
