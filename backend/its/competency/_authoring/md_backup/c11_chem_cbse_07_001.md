## DEFINITION
Equilibrium in physical processes is a dynamic state in a closed system where opposing physical changes (such as melting/freezing, evaporation/condensation, or sublimation/deposition) occur at equal rates, resulting in no net change in the macroscopic properties (temperature, pressure, concentration) of the system over time. The equilibrium is characterized by a constant value of an intensive property (e.g., vapour pressure for liquid-vapour equilibrium, melting point for solid-liquid equilibrium) at a given temperature.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to: (1) identify the type of physical equilibrium in a given scenario (solid-liquid, liquid-vapour, solid-vapour, gas-solution) and state the constant intensive property that defines it; (2) explain why physical equilibrium is dynamic at the molecular level but static at the macroscopic level; (3) predict the effect of changing temperature or pressure on the equilibrium position using Le Chatelier's principle for physical processes.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers only physical equilibria: solid-liquid (melting/freezing), liquid-vapour (evaporation/condensation), solid-vapour (sublimation/deposition), and gas-liquid (dissolution/effervescence) equilibria in closed systems. It does NOT cover chemical equilibrium (reversible chemical reactions), equilibrium constants (Kc, Kp), ionic equilibrium, or Le Chatelier's principle for chemical systems — these are covered in subsequent nodes (c11_chem_cbse_07_002 onwards).

## WALKTHROUGH
Observe a sealed bottle of carbonated drink (like Thums Up or Limca) at room temperature — you see no bubbles rising, yet when you open it, CO₂ fizzes out violently. This demonstrates gas-liquid equilibrium: inside the sealed bottle, CO₂ dissolves in the liquid at the same rate it escapes, maintaining constant pressure. Now place an ice cube in a insulated flask at 0°C — it neither melts completely nor grows; solid and liquid water coexist at constant temperature. These everyday Indian kitchen observations reveal that physical equilibrium requires a **closed system**, a **constant temperature**, and **opposing processes at equal rates**. Begin by identifying the two phases present and the intensive property that remains constant (vapour pressure, melting point, solubility). Connect this to Class 9's "Particulate Nature of Matter" (c8_chem_ch2_5) where you learned particles in liquids have enough energy to escape — now understand that at equilibrium, the *rate* of escape equals the *rate* of return.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
A student places 50 g of ice and 50 g of water in a perfectly insulated, sealed container at 0°C and 1 atm. After 2 hours, they measure the mass of ice and water. What will they observe, and why does this represent a physical equilibrium?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The masses of ice and water will remain 50 g each — no net melting or freezing occurs. This is solid-liquid equilibrium at the melting point (0°C at 1 atm) where the rate of melting equals the rate of freezing. A wrong answer (e.g., "all ice melts" or "all water freezes") reveals the student thinks equilibrium means "reaction stops" rather than "opposing processes continue at equal rates" — they miss the dynamic nature and the requirement of a closed system at constant T and P.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why does water in an open beaker at 25°C eventually evaporate completely, while water in a sealed bottle at 25°C reaches a constant vapour pressure? Explain using the concept of dynamic equilibrium and system closure.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
In the open beaker, water vapour escapes into the infinite surroundings, so the reverse process (condensation) cannot keep up — the system is not closed, so equilibrium cannot be established. In the sealed bottle, escaped molecules accumulate, increasing condensation rate until it equals evaporation rate. A wrong answer (e.g., "evaporation stops in sealed bottle") shows the student confuses "no net change" with "process stops" and fails to recognize that closure allows the reverse process to occur.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Solid iodine (I₂) is placed in a sealed evacuated flask at 25°C. Purple vapour appears and gradually darkens until its colour intensity becomes constant. Write the equilibrium equation, identify the constant intensive property, and calculate the vapour pressure of iodine at 25°C if the density of the vapour is 0.012 g/L (Molar mass I₂ = 254 g/mol, R = 0.0821 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹).

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
**Equation:** I₂(s) ⇌ I₂(g)  
**Constant property:** Vapour pressure of iodine at 25°C  
**Calculation:**  
PV = nRT → P = (density/M) × RT = (0.012 g/L ÷ 254 g/mol) × 0.0821 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹ × 298 K = 1.16 × 10⁻³ atm  
A wrong calculation (e.g., using M = 127 g/mol for I atom) reveals confusion between atomic and molecular molar mass. Forgetting to write the equilibrium equation with phases shows inability to represent physical equilibrium formally.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the solid-liquid equilibrium of water (ice ⇌ water) at 0°C and 1 atm with the liquid-vapour equilibrium of water (water ⇌ steam) at 100°C and 1 atm. Identify two similarities and two differences in terms of: (a) intensive property that remains constant, (b) effect of pressure on equilibrium temperature, (c) molar volume change, and (d) enthalpy change sign.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
**Similarities:** (1) Both are dynamic equilibria in closed systems at constant T and P; (2) Both have ΔG = 0 at equilibrium.  
**Differences:**  
(a) Constant property: Melting point (0°C) vs. Vapour pressure (1 atm / boiling point 100°C)  
(b) Pressure effect: Increasing pressure *lowers* melting point of ice (ΔV < 0) but *raises* boiling point (ΔV > 0)  
(c) Molar volume: Ice → water: ΔV = -1.6 cm³/mol (contraction); Water → steam: ΔV = +30,600 cm³/mol (large expansion)  
(d) Enthalpy: ΔH_fus = +6.01 kJ/mol (endothermic); ΔH_vap = +40.7 kJ/mol (endothermic) — both positive but magnitudes differ hugely.  
A wrong answer confusing "melting point" with "vapour pressure" as the constant property, or stating pressure raises melting point of ice, reveals fundamental misunderstanding of Clapeyron/Clausius-Clapeyron relationships.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student claims: "At liquid-vapour equilibrium, the rate of evaporation equals the rate of condensation, so the vapour pressure becomes zero because no net molecules enter the vapour phase." Evaluate this statement and correct the misconception.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is **false**. Equal rates mean *no net change* in vapour density/pressure, not zero vapour pressure. At equilibrium, vapour pressure reaches a **constant non-zero value** (saturated vapour pressure) characteristic of that temperature. Molecules continuously evaporate and condense — the vapour phase is not empty. A student making this claim confuses "net rate = 0" with "individual rates = 0" and fails to grasp that dynamic equilibrium maintains a steady-state concentration/pressure, not absence of the phase.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple experiment using household items (pressure cooker, water, thermometer, stopwatch) to demonstrate that the boiling point of water increases with pressure. Describe the procedure, what you would measure, and how you would relate it to liquid-vapour equilibrium and Le Chatelier's principle.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
**Procedure:** (1) Heat 200 mL water in an open pan — record temperature when vigorous boiling starts (T₁ ≈ 100°C at 1 atm). (2) Heat 200 mL water in a pressure cooker *without* the weight/whistle — record temperature when steady steam escapes the vent (T₂ > 100°C, pressure > 1 atm). (3) Repeat with weight on, noting higher temperature (T₃ > T₂).  
**Measurements:** Boiling temperature at different pressures.  
**Relation:** Liquid-vapour equilibrium H₂O(l) ⇌ H₂O(g) has ΔV > 0. Increasing pressure (by restricting vapour escape) shifts equilibrium toward liquid (Le Chatelier), requiring higher temperature to re-establish equilibrium (higher vapour pressure). A poor design (e.g., not measuring temperature at steady steam, or using open pan for all trials) shows inability to control variables and connect macroscopic observation to molecular equilibrium shift.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students believe that at physical equilibrium, **all molecular motion stops** — they write "evaporation stops" or "no molecules cross the interface" instead of "rate of evaporation = rate of condensation". For example, in I₂(s) ⇌ I₂(g), they think the purple vapour colour becomes constant because sublimation ceases, not because sublimation and deposition rates are equal and non-zero. This error stems from confusing "no net macroscopic change" with "no microscopic process". It leads to wrong predictions: they think vapour pressure is zero at equilibrium, or that adding more solid increases vapour pressure (it doesn't — vapour pressure depends only on temperature).

## ON_FAILURE
1. **Visual demonstration:** Show a sealed syringe with water at room temperature — pull plunger to create vacuum, watch water boil (evaporate rapidly), then push plunger — condensation occurs instantly. Ask: "Did evaporation stop when colour/volume stabilized?"  
2. **Molecular animation:** Use PhET "States of Matter" simulation — show molecules leaving and entering liquid surface at equilibrium. Count leaving vs entering per 10 seconds.  
3. **Quantitative check:** Measure vapour pressure of ethanol at 25°C using a manometer — it's ~5.9 kPa, not zero. Have student calculate rate of evaporation from vapour pressure and kinetic theory (optional advanced).  
4. **Key phrase to memorize:** "Equilibrium = equal rates, not zero rates."

## THIS_FORMULA
Clapeyron Equation: dP/dT = ΔH_trans / (T ΔV_trans)  
Clausius-Clapeyron Equation (for liquid-vapour/solid-vapour): d(ln P)/dT = ΔH_vap / (R T²)  
Integrated form: ln(P₂/P₁) = -(ΔH_vap/R) (1/T₂ - 1/T₁)  
Henry's Law (gas-liquid): P_gas = k_H × x_gas (or C = k_H × P)

## FORMULA_VARIABLES
P = equilibrium vapour pressure (Pa or atm); T = absolute temperature (K); ΔH_trans = molar enthalpy of phase transition (J/mol); ΔV_trans = molar volume change (m³/mol); R = universal gas constant (8.314 J/mol·K); k_H = Henry's law constant (varies with units); x_gas = mole fraction of gas in liquid; C = concentration of dissolved gas (mol/L).

## FORMULA_UNITS
P: Pa or atm; T: K; ΔH: J/mol; ΔV: m³/mol; R: 8.314 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹; k_H: depends on definition (e.g., Pa·m³/mol or mol/L·atm)

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A sealed 2.0 L flask contains liquid water and water vapour at equilibrium at 30°C. The vapour pressure of water at 30°C is 4.24 kPa. Calculate the mass of water vapour present in the flask. If the flask volume is doubled to 4.0 L at constant temperature, what will be the new vapour pressure and mass of vapour after equilibrium is re-established? (Molar mass H₂O = 18.0 g/mol, R = 8.314 J/mol·K)

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the equilibrium: H₂O(l) ⇌ H₂O(g) at 30°C in a closed system. The constant intensive property is vapour pressure (P = 4.24 kPa at 30°C).
- Step 2: Use ideal gas law to find moles of vapour in 2.0 L: n = PV/RT = (4240 Pa × 0.002 m³) / (8.314 × 303) = 0.00336 mol.
- Step 3: Calculate mass: m = n × M = 0.00336 mol × 18.0 g/mol = 0.0605 g.
- Step 4: When volume doubles to 4.0 L at same T, vapour pressure initially drops, so evaporation rate > condensation rate until pressure returns to 4.24 kPa (equilibrium vapour pressure depends only on T).
- Step 5: New moles at equilibrium: n' = P(2V)/RT = 2 × 0.00336 = 0.00672 mol. New mass = 0.00672 × 18.0 = 0.121 g. Vapour pressure remains 4.24 kPa.

REASONING: Vapour pressure at a given temperature is an intensive property — it does not depend on volume or amount of liquid (as long as some liquid remains). Doubling volume causes more liquid to evaporate to restore the same pressure. The system shifts to counteract the pressure decrease (Le Chatelier for physical equilibrium).

ANSWER: Initial vapour mass = 0.0605 g; After volume doubling: vapour pressure = 4.24 kPa (unchanged), vapour mass = 0.121 g.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: The normal boiling point of water is 100°C at 1.013 bar. The enthalpy of vaporization is 40.7 kJ/mol. Estimate the boiling point of water at the top of Mount Abu (pressure ≈ 0.90 bar) using the integrated Clausius-Clapeyron equation. Assume ΔH_vap is constant.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Write integrated Clausius-Clapeyron: ln(P₂/P₁) = -(ΔH_vap/R) (1/T₂ - 1/T₁).
- Step 2: Identify knowns: P₁ = 1.013 bar, T₁ = 373.15 K, P₂ = 0.90 bar, ΔH_vap = 40700 J/mol, R = 8.314 J/mol·K.
- Step 3: Substitute: ln(0.90/1.013) = -(40700/8.314) (1/T₂ - 1/373.15).
- Step 4: Calculate ln(0.888) = -0.1188. Right side: -4895.4 (1/T₂ - 0.002680).
- Step 5: Solve for 1/T₂: -0.1188 / -4895.4 = 1/T₂ - 0.002680 → 2.427×10⁻⁵ = 1/T₂ - 0.002680 → 1/T₂ = 0.002704 → T₂ = 369.8 K = 96.6°C.
- Step 6: Verify: Lower pressure → lower boiling point, consistent with Le Chatelier (vapour expansion favored at lower P).

REASONING: The Clausius-Clapeyron equation quantifies how equilibrium vapour pressure changes with temperature. At boiling, vapour pressure = external pressure. Lower external pressure means equilibrium is reached at lower temperature. The derivation assumes ideal gas and negligible liquid molar volume — valid for water at near-ambient pressures.

ANSWER: Boiling point at 0.90 bar ≈ 96.6°C.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Solid CO₂ (dry ice) sublimes at -78.5°C (194.7 K) at 1 atm. The enthalpy of sublimation is 25.2 kJ/mol. A 1.00 g piece of dry ice is placed in an evacuated 5.00 L sealed vessel at 25°C (298 K). Calculate the final pressure in the vessel after all solid CO₂ sublimes and equilibrium is reached. If instead the vessel initially contains 0.50 g dry ice, what is the equilibrium pressure? (Molar mass CO₂ = 44.0 g/mol)

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify equilibrium: CO₂(s) ⇌ CO₂(g). This is solid-vapour equilibrium. Vapour pressure depends only on temperature.
- Step 2: Use Clausius-Clapeyron to find vapour pressure at 298 K. Known: P₁ = 1 atm at T₁ = 194.7 K, ΔH_sub = 25200 J/mol.
- Step 3: ln(P₂/1) = -(25200/8.314) (1/298 - 1/194.7) = -3031 × (0.003356 - 0.005136) = -3031 × (-0.001780) = 5.395.
- Step 4: P₂ = e^5.395 = 220 atm. This is the equilibrium vapour pressure of CO₂ at 25°C.
- Step 5: For 1.00 g CO₂ (0.0227 mol) in 5.00 L at 298 K, ideal gas pressure if all gas: P = nRT/V = (0.0227 × 0.0821 × 298)/5 = 0.111 atm.
- Step 6: Since 0.111 atm < 220 atm, all solid sublimes — no solid remains at equilibrium. Final pressure = 0.111 atm.
- Step 7: For 0.50 g (0.0114 mol): P = (0.0114 × 0.0821 × 298)/5 = 0.0555 atm < 220 atm. Again, all sublimes. Pressure = 0.0555 atm.
- Step 8: Key insight: Equilibrium vapour pressure (220 atm) is the *maximum* pressure solid CO₂ can sustain at 25°C. If amount of CO₂ is insufficient to reach this pressure, all solid converts to gas — no equilibrium between phases is established.

REASONING: Physical equilibrium requires coexistence of phases. The equilibrium vapour pressure is a fixed value at a given T. If the total amount of substance cannot produce that pressure in the given volume, the phase with higher molar volume (gas) will completely consume the other phase. This is a critical distinction from chemical equilibrium where K is a ratio, not an absolute pressure.

ANSWER: For 1.00 g: final pressure = 0.111 atm (no solid remains). For 0.50 g: final pressure = 0.0555 atm (no solid remains). Equilibrium vapour pressure of CO₂ at 25°C is 220 atm — neither sample reaches it.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A student sets up three sealed syringes at 25°C: (A) 1 mL water + 9 mL air, (B) 1 mL ethanol + 9 mL air, (C) 1 mL mercury + 9 mL air. After equilibrium, they measure the total pressure in each. The vapour pressures at 25°C are: water = 3.17 kPa, ethanol = 7.88 kPa, mercury = 0.0002 kPa. Atmospheric pressure = 101.3 kPa. Rank the syringes by final total pressure (highest to lowest) and explain why the pressure in syringe (C) is essentially unchanged from 101.3 kPa while (A) and (B) show measurable increases.

COMPETENCY
Interpreting phase equilibrium data and applying Dalton's law of partial pressures to physical equilibrium systems.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Recognize each syringe establishes liquid-vapour equilibrium: liquid ⇌ vapour. The vapour exerts its saturated vapour pressure (P_vap) at 25°C.
- Step 2: Total pressure = P_air + P_vap (Dalton's law). Initial air pressure ≈ 101.3 kPa (assuming syringes sealed at 1 atm).
- Step 3: For water: P_total = 101.3 + 3.17 = 104.5 kPa. For ethanol: P_total = 101.3 + 7.88 = 109.2 kPa. For mercury: P_total = 101.3 + 0.0002 ≈ 101.3 kPa.
- Step 4: Rank: Ethanol (109.2) > Water (104.5) > Mercury (101.3).
- Step 5: Mercury's vapour pressure is negligible (0.0002 kPa) because metallic bonding gives extremely low volatility — almost no Hg atoms escape at 25°C. Water and ethanol have hydrogen bonding/intermolecular forces allowing significant evaporation.

ANSWER: Rank: Ethanol > Water > Mercury. Mercury's vapour pressure is ~50,000× smaller than water's due to strong metallic bonds, so its contribution to total pressure is undetectable.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: In a pressure cooker, water boils at 120°C because the sealed lid allows pressure to build up. The safety valve releases steam when pressure exceeds 1.5 atm. Using the Clausius-Clapeyron equation, calculate the approximate boiling point of water at 1.5 atm. Then explain why food cooks faster at this temperature despite the same heat input, connecting to the temperature dependence of reaction rates (Arrhenius equation) — a cross-concept link to chemical kinetics. (ΔH_vap = 40.7 kJ/mol, normal BP = 100°C at 1 atm)

COMPETENCY
Integrating physical equilibrium (Clausius-Clapeyron) with chemical kinetics (Arrhenius) to explain a real-world cooking phenomenon.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Use Clausius-Clapeyron: ln(P₂/P₁) = -(ΔH_vap/R)(1/T₂ - 1/T₁). P₁=1 atm, T₁=373 K, P₂=1.5 atm.
- Step 2: ln(1.5) = 0.4055 = -(40700/8.314)(1/T₂ - 1/373) = -4895(1/T₂ - 0.002681).
- Step 3: 1/T₂ - 0.002681 = -0.4055/4895 = -8.28×10⁻⁵ → 1/T₂ = 0.002598 → T₂ = 384.9 K = 111.8°C.
- Step 4: Note: Actual pressure cookers reach ~120°C at 1.5 atm gauge (2.5 atm absolute). Recalculate for 2.5 atm: ln(2.5)=0.9163 → 1/T₂=0.002681-0.9163/4895=0.002494 → T₂=401 K=128°C. The 120°C figure uses gauge pressure + approximations.
- Step 5: Cooking involves chemical reactions (protein denaturation, starch gelatinization). Rate constant k = A e^(-Ea/RT). At 120°C (393 K) vs 100°C (373 K), rate increases by factor exp[(Ea/R)(1/373 - 1/393)]. For typical Ea=50 kJ/mol: factor = exp[6014×(0.000136)] = exp[0.818] ≈ 2.27× faster.
- Step 6: Higher pressure → higher boiling point (physical equilibrium) → higher temperature → exponentially faster reaction rates (chemical kinetics). Pressure cooker exploits this coupling.

ANSWER: Boiling point at 1.5 atm absolute ≈ 112°C (at 2.5 atm absolute ≈ 128°C). Food cooks ~2-3× faster because reaction rates double per ~10°C rise (Arrhenius), and pressure cooker achieves 20-28°C higher temperature via elevated vapour pressure equilibrium.
