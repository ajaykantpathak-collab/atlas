## DEFINITION
Solubility is the maximum amount of solute (in grams or moles) that can dissolve in a specified amount of solvent (typically 100 g or 1 kg) at a given temperature and pressure to form a saturated solution under dynamic equilibrium between dissolution and crystallisation. For solids in liquids, solubility depends primarily on temperature and the chemical nature of solute-solvent (like dissolves like); for gases in liquids, it is governed by Henry's law (p = KH x) where solubility is directly proportional to the partial pressure of the gas above the solution.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can (1) predict whether a given solute-solvent pair will be miscible based on polarity and intermolecular forces, (2) apply Le Chatelier's principle to predict the effect of temperature on solubility of solids given the enthalpy of solution, (3) calculate gas solubility using Henry's law constant at different partial pressures, and (4) explain real-world phenomena (soft drink fizzing, scuba diving bends, high-altitude anoxia) using these principles.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers: (a) solubility of solids in liquids — nature of solute/solvent (polar/non-polar), temperature dependence via Le Chatelier's principle (endothermic vs exothermic dissolution), negligible pressure effect; (b) solubility of gases in liquids — Henry's law (p = KH x), pressure dependence, temperature dependence (exothermic dissolution), applications. It does NOT cover: solubility product (Ksp), common ion effect, colligative properties, or Raoult's law (these are in later nodes c12_chem_cbse_01_005 onwards and Class 11 c11_chem_cbse_07_014).

## WALKTHROUGH
Begin with a kitchen observation: sugar dissolves rapidly in hot tea but barely in ice-cold water, while a soda bottle fizzes violently when opened warm but stays calm when chilled. Ask students why temperature helps solids but hurts gases. Connect to Class 9 (c9_chem_ch1_3) where they learned "like dissolves like" — now deepen it: polar solutes (NaCl) need polar solvents (water) for ion-dipole interactions; non-polar solutes (naphthalene) need non-polar solvents (benzene) for London forces. Introduce dynamic equilibrium: dissolution ⇌ crystallisation. For solids, use Le Chatelier's principle — if dissolution is endothermic (most solids), heat is a reactant, so raising temperature shifts equilibrium right → higher solubility. For gases, dissolution is exothermic (gas molecules lose kinetic energy entering liquid), so raising temperature shifts equilibrium left → lower solubility. Pressure affects gases dramatically (Henry's law) because compressing gas increases concentration at interface; solids/liquids are incompressible. Work through Henry's law calculations (Example 1.4 from NCERT). End with applications: CO2 in soft drinks (high pressure sealing), scuba divers' bends (N2 bubbles on depressurisation), anoxia at high altitude (low pO2).

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
A student claims: "Since pressure increases gas solubility, it should also increase the solubility of salt in water because both involve dissolving." Is this statement correct? Explain why or why not in 2-3 sentences.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The statement is incorrect. Pressure has negligible effect on solubility of solids in liquids because solids and liquids are highly incompressible — their molar volumes change insignificantly under pressure. In contrast, gases are highly compressible; increasing pressure increases the concentration of gas molecules at the gas-liquid interface, driving more dissolution until a new equilibrium is established (Henry's law). The student confuses the response of compressible vs incompressible phases to pressure changes.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
The dissolution of ammonium nitrate (NH4NO3) in water is endothermic (ΔsolH > 0). A saturated solution of NH4NO3 at 30°C is heated to 50°C. Will the solution become unsaturated, remain saturated, or become supersaturated? Justify using Le Chatelier's principle.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The solution will become unsaturated. Since dissolution is endothermic, heat acts as a reactant: NH4NO3(s) + heat ⇌ NH4NO3(aq). Raising temperature adds heat, shifting equilibrium to the right (forward dissolution), increasing solubility. The previously saturated solution can now dissolve more solid, so it becomes unsaturated relative to the new higher solubility at 50°C. A common error is to think heating always makes solutions supersaturated; that only occurs if a hot saturated solution is cooled without crystallisation.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
At 298 K, Henry's law constant (KH) for O2 in water is 34.86 kbar. Calculate the mole fraction of O2 dissolved in water when the partial pressure of O2 above the solution is 0.21 bar (approximate atmospheric pO2). Then calculate the molar concentration of dissolved O2 assuming 1 L of water ≈ 55.5 mol.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Henry's law: p = KH × x → x = p/KH = 0.21 bar / 34,860 bar = 6.02 × 10⁻⁶.
Step 2: Mole fraction x = nO2 / (nO2 + nH2O) ≈ nO2 / nH2O (since nO2 ≪ nH2O).
Step 3: nO2 = x × nH2O = 6.02 × 10⁻⁶ × 55.5 mol = 3.34 × 10⁻⁴ mol.
Step 4: Molar concentration = nO2 / V = 3.34 × 10⁻⁴ mol / 1 L = 3.34 × 10⁻⁴ M.
Common error: forgetting to convert kbar to bar (34.86 kbar = 34,860 bar) or using molarity directly in Henry's law instead of mole fraction.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the temperature dependence of solubility for: (a) NaCl in water (ΔsolH ≈ +3.9 kJ/mol), (b) Ce2(SO4)3 in water (ΔsolH < 0, exothermic), (c) O2 in water (exothermic dissolution). Sketch qualitative solubility vs temperature curves for all three on the same axes. Label axes.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
(a) NaCl: endothermic dissolution → solubility increases with temperature (positive slope, gentle curve).
(b) Ce2(SO4)3: exothermic dissolution → solubility decreases with temperature (negative slope).
(c) O2: gas dissolution is exothermic → solubility decreases sharply with temperature (steeper negative slope than solids).
All curves start at some solubility at 0°C. The key distinction: for solids, the sign of ΔsolH determines the trend; for gases, dissolution is always exothermic (condensation-like), so solubility always decreases with temperature. Students often assume all solids behave like NaCl.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A sealed soda bottle contains CO2 at 4 atm partial pressure over the liquid at 298 K. Henry's law constant for CO2 is 1.67 × 10³ bar. The bottle is opened, and the pressure above the liquid drops to 0.0004 atm (atmospheric pCO2). Calculate the ratio of CO2 mole fraction before and after opening. Explain why the drink fizzes.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Before: x₁ = p₁/KH = (4 atm × 1.013 bar/atm) / 1670 bar = 4.052/1670 = 2.43 × 10⁻³.
After: x₂ = p₂/KH = (0.0004 atm × 1.013 bar/atm) / 1670 bar = 4.05 × 10⁻⁴/1670 = 2.43 × 10⁻⁷.
Ratio x₁/x₂ = 10,000. The dissolved CO2 is now 10,000× supersaturated relative to the new equilibrium. The system rapidly evolves CO2 gas (fizzing) to re-establish equilibrium at the lower pressure. Students often forget unit conversion (atm to bar) or misapply Henry's law as C = kp instead of p = KH x.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple experiment using household materials to demonstrate that gas solubility decreases with temperature. Specify: materials, procedure, observations, and the underlying principle. How would you modify it to show pressure effect?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Materials: Two identical transparent bottles of carbonated water (same brand, same initial pressure), refrigerator, bowl of hot water (~60°C).
Procedure: Chill one bottle in refrigerator for 30 min. Place the other in hot water bath for 5 min. Open both simultaneously and observe fizzing.
Observation: Warm bottle fizzes violently (large bubbles, rapid gas escape); cold bottle fizzes gently.
Principle: CO2 solubility decreases with temperature (exothermic dissolution). Warm liquid holds less CO2 at same pressure, so more gas escapes on opening.
Pressure modification: Use a soda maker or pressure pump to carbonate water at different pressures (e.g., 1 atm vs 3 atm CO2) at same temperature. Higher pressure bottle will have more dissolved CO2 and fizz more on opening — demonstrating Henry's law.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students write Henry's law as C = kp (concentration proportional to pressure) and use molarity directly with KH values tabulated for mole fraction (p = KH x). This causes unit errors: KH in kbar is for mole fraction, not molarity. The correct form is p = KH x where x = n_gas/(n_gas + n_solvent) ≈ n_gas/n_solvent for dilute solutions. Another error: assuming all solids become more soluble on heating — they cite NaCl but forget exceptions like Ce2(SO4)3, Ca(OH)2, Li2CO3 where dissolution is exothermic. Also, students think "pressure has no effect on solid solubility" means zero effect — it's negligible but not zero; at extreme pressures (kbar), solid solubility can change due to volume changes (Le Chatelier's principle with ΔV).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A 2.0 L bottle of soda water is sealed under 3.5 atm CO2 pressure at 298 K. Henry's law constant for CO2 at 298 K is 1.67 × 10³ bar. Calculate (a) the mole fraction of CO2 in the sealed bottle, (b) the mass of CO2 dissolved in the water (assume density of solution ≈ 1 g/mL, molar mass H2O = 18 g/mol), and (c) the volume of CO2 gas (at STP) that will escape when the bottle is opened to atmospheric pCO2 = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ atm.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Convert all pressures to consistent units (bar). p_CO2(sealed) = 3.5 atm × 1.013 bar/atm = 3.5455 bar. p_CO2(atm) = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ atm × 1.013 bar/atm = 4.052 × 10⁻⁴ bar.
- Step 2: Apply Henry's law p = KH × x to find mole fraction in sealed bottle. x_sealed = p_sealed / KH = 3.5455 bar / 1670 bar = 2.123 × 10⁻³.
- Step 3: Calculate moles of water in 2.0 L solution. Mass of solution ≈ 2000 g (density 1 g/mL). Since CO2 is dilute, mass of water ≈ 2000 g. Moles H2O = 2000 g / 18 g mol⁻¹ = 111.1 mol.
- Step 4: Find moles of CO2 dissolved (sealed). x = n_CO2 / (n_CO2 + n_H2O) ≈ n_CO2 / n_H2O → n_CO2 = x × n_H2O = 2.123 × 10⁻³ × 111.1 = 0.236 mol.
- Step 5: Mass of CO2 = n_CO2 × M_CO2 = 0.236 mol × 44 g mol⁻¹ = 10.38 g.
- Step 6: Find mole fraction at atmospheric pressure. x_atm = p_atm / KH = 4.052 × 10⁻⁴ bar / 1670 bar = 2.426 × 10⁻⁷.
- Step 7: Moles CO2 remaining at equilibrium after opening. n_CO2(atm) = x_atm × n_H2O = 2.426 × 10⁻⁷ × 111.1 = 2.70 × 10⁻⁵ mol.
- Step 8: Moles CO2 escaped = 0.236 - 2.70 × 10⁻⁵ ≈ 0.236 mol. Volume at STP (22.4 L/mol) = 0.236 × 22.4 = 5.29 L.

REASONING: Henry's law relates equilibrium partial pressure to mole fraction in liquid. The large pressure drop (3.5 atm → 0.0004 atm) causes massive supersaturation, driving CO2 out of solution. The approximation x ≈ n_gas/n_solvent is valid because n_gas ≪ n_solvent. STP volume uses 22.4 L/mol (old convention; 22.7 L/mol at 1 bar — specify which is used).

ANSWER: (a) x_CO2 = 2.12 × 10⁻³; (b) mass CO2 = 10.4 g; (c) volume CO2 escaped = 5.29 L at STP.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: The solubility of Ca(OH)2 in water decreases with temperature (exothermic dissolution: Ca(OH)2(s) ⇌ Ca²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) + heat). At 25°C, a saturated solution has [Ca²⁺] = 0.020 M. A student heats 500 mL of this saturated solution to 80°C, where solubility drops to [Ca²⁺] = 0.008 M. Calculate the mass of Ca(OH)2 that precipitates. Molar mass Ca(OH)2 = 74 g/mol.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify initial and final concentrations of Ca²⁺. Initial [Ca²⁺] = 0.020 M at 25°C. Final [Ca²⁺] = 0.008 M at 80°C.
- Step 2: Calculate initial moles of Ca²⁺ in 500 mL. n_initial = 0.020 mol/L × 0.500 L = 0.0100 mol.
- Step 3: Calculate final moles of Ca²⁺ in 500 mL (assume volume change negligible). n_final = 0.008 mol/L × 0.500 L = 0.0040 mol.
- Step 4: Moles of Ca²⁺ precipitated = n_initial - n_final = 0.0100 - 0.0040 = 0.0060 mol.
- Step 5: Since 1 mol Ca(OH)2 gives 1 mol Ca²⁺, moles Ca(OH)2 precipitated = 0.0060 mol.
- Step 6: Mass precipitated = 0.0060 mol × 74 g/mol = 0.444 g.
- Step 7: Verify with Le Chatelier's principle: heating adds heat to exothermic equilibrium → shifts left → precipitation occurs.

REASONING: For exothermic dissolution, solubility decreases with temperature. The calculation assumes volume of solution doesn't change significantly (reasonable for dilute solutions). The stoichiometry of Ca(OH)2 → Ca²⁺ + 2OH⁻ means [Ca²⁺] directly gives solubility in mol/L. This is a practical example of "retrograde solubility" seen in few solids.

ANSWER: 0.444 g of Ca(OH)2 precipitates on heating from 25°C to 80°C.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A scuba diver breathes compressed air (79% N2, 21% O2 by volume) at 4 atm total pressure at 25°C. Henry's law constants at 298 K: KH(N2) = 6.51 × 10⁴ bar, KH(O2) = 3.30 × 10⁴ bar. Calculate (a) the mole fractions of N2 and O2 dissolved in the diver's blood plasma (assume plasma behaves like water), and (b) the ratio of dissolved N2 to O2. Explain why N2 causes "bends" but O2 does not, despite O2 being more soluble.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Find partial pressures. p_N2 = 0.79 × 4 atm = 3.16 atm. p_O2 = 0.21 × 4 atm = 0.84 atm.
- Step 2: Convert to bar. p_N2 = 3.16 × 1.013 = 3.20 bar. p_O2 = 0.84 × 1.013 = 0.851 bar.
- Step 3: Apply Henry's law for each gas. x_N2 = p_N2 / KH(N2) = 3.20 bar / 65100 bar = 4.92 × 10⁻⁵. x_O2 = p_O2 / KH(O2) = 0.851 bar / 33000 bar = 2.58 × 10⁻⁵.
- Step 4: Ratio x_N2 / x_O2 = 4.92 × 10⁻⁵ / 2.58 × 10⁻⁵ = 1.91.
- Step 5: Interpret: Although O2 is more soluble per unit pressure (lower KH), the much higher partial pressure of N2 in air (79% vs 21%) results in nearly 2× more dissolved N2.
- Step 6: On ascent, pressure drops. N2 comes out of solution forming bubbles (bends) because: (i) large amount dissolved, (ii) N2 is metabolically inert — not consumed by tissues, so it accumulates. O2 is continuously metabolised, so its tissue concentration stays low; no supersaturation bubbles form.

REASONING: Henry's law applies to each gas independently (Dalton's law). The "bends" (decompression sickness) is caused by N2 bubbles because N2 is not metabolised and its high partial pressure in air leads to high dissolved load. O2 solubility is higher per bar, but its lower partial pressure and rapid metabolic consumption prevent bubble formation.

ANSWER: (a) x_N2 = 4.92 × 10⁻⁵, x_O2 = 2.58 × 10⁻⁵; (b) ratio N2:O2 = 1.91:1. N2 causes bends due to high dissolved amount + metabolic inertness.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A mountain climber at 6000 m altitude experiences anoxia (low blood O2). Atmospheric pressure at 6000 m is 0.47 atm. Air is 21% O2 by volume. Henry's law constant for O2 in blood plasma at 37°C is 4.0 × 10⁴ bar. (a) Calculate the mole fraction of O2 dissolved in plasma at sea level (1 atm) and at 6000 m. (b) By what factor does dissolved O2 decrease? (c) Explain why hyperventilation helps partially but cannot fully compensate. (d) If the climber breathes pure O2 at 6000 m (using an oxygen mask), what is the new mole fraction? Compare to sea-level air breathing.

COMPETENCY: Applying Henry's law to physiological gas exchange; quantitative reasoning with partial pressures; evaluating medical interventions.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate pO2 at sea level: 0.21 × 1 atm = 0.21 atm = 0.213 bar. At 6000 m: 0.21 × 0.47 atm = 0.0987 atm = 0.100 bar.
- Step 2: Apply Henry's law x = p/KH. KH = 4.0 × 10⁴ bar. x_sea = 0.213 / 40000 = 5.33 × 10⁻⁶. x_6000 = 0.100 / 40000 = 2.50 × 10⁻⁶.
- Step 3: Factor decrease = x_sea / x_6000 = 2.13. Dissolved O2 drops by ~53%.
- Step 4: Hyperventilation increases alveolar ventilation but cannot raise alveolar pO2 above inspired pO2 (0.100 bar). It reduces pCO2, slightly increasing pO2 via alveolar gas equation, but fundamental limit is inspired pO2.
- Step 5: Pure O2 at 6000 m: pO2 = 1 × 0.47 atm = 0.475 bar. x_pureO2 = 0.475 / 40000 = 1.19 × 10⁻⁵. This is 2.23× sea-level air breathing (5.33 × 10⁻⁶) — more than compensates.

ANSWER: (a) Sea level: 5.33 × 10⁻⁶; 6000 m: 2.50 × 10⁻⁶. (b) Factor = 2.13 decrease. (c) Hyperventilation limited by inspired pO2. (d) Pure O2 at 6000 m gives x = 1.19 × 10⁻⁵, which is 2.23× sea-level air value.

DIFFICULTY: medium

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A chemical plant uses a water scrubber to remove HCl gas from an exhaust stream. The gas enters at 1.2 atm total pressure with 5% HCl by volume at 298 K. Henry's law constant for HCl in water is 1.83 × 10⁻⁵ bar (note: very low KH = very high solubility). The scrubber operates at 298 K with a water flow rate of 500 L/h. The gas flow rate is 10,000 L/h at STP. Assuming equilibrium is reached and HCl reacts completely with water (HCl + H2O → H3O⁺ + Cl⁻), calculate: (a) the equilibrium mole fraction of HCl in the exiting water, (b) the molar concentration of HCl in the exiting water, (c) the mass of HCl removed per hour, and (d) whether the water flow rate is sufficient to absorb all HCl (assume max safe [HCl] = 10 M for equipment).

COMPETENCY: Integrating Henry's law with gas-liquid mass transfer, stoichiometry, and process design constraints; dimensional analysis with mixed units.

STEPS:
- Step 1: p_HCl = 0.05 × 1.2 atm = 0.06 atm = 0.0608 bar.
- Step 2: x_HCl = p_HCl / KH = 0.0608 bar / 1.83 × 10⁻⁵ bar = 3322. This is >1! Impossible.
- Step 3: Realise KH = 1.83 × 10⁻⁵ bar is for molecular HCl, but HCl dissociates completely in water. The effective solubility is governed by reaction equilibrium, not Henry's law for molecular HCl. Henry's law applies only to undissociated gas. For strong acids, solubility is essentially infinite (limited by water amount).
- Step 4: Calculate HCl input: 10,000 L/h at STP = 10000/22.4 = 446.4 mol/h HCl.
- Step 5: Water available: 500 L/h = 500 kg/h = 27,778 mol/h H2O.
- Step 6: If all HCl dissolves, [HCl] = 446.4 mol / 500 L = 0.893 M. Well below 10 M limit.
- Step 7: Water flow is sufficient. Mass HCl removed = 446.4 mol × 36.5 g/mol = 16,290 g/h = 16.3 kg/h.

REASONING: The trap is the Henry's law constant — for HCl, KH is tiny because molecular HCl is negligible; dissociation drives absorption. Students must recognise that Henry's law in its simple form (p = KH x) applies only to non-reacting gases. For reacting gases (HCl, NH3, CO2, SO2), effective solubility is much higher. The calculation then becomes a stoichiometric mass balance.

ANSWER: (a) Henry's law not directly applicable due to complete dissociation; (b) [HCl] = 0.893 M; (c) 16.3 kg/h; (d) Yes, water flow is sufficient (0.893 M ≪ 10 M).
