## DEFINITION
As per Accounting Standard-3 (AS-3), **Cash** comprises cash in hand and demand deposits with banks (i.e., balances that can be withdrawn at any time without notice). **Cash Equivalents** are short-term, highly liquid investments that are readily convertible into known amounts of cash and are subject to an insignificant risk of changes in value. To qualify as a cash equivalent, an investment must have a short maturity of **three months or less** from the date of acquisition. Investments in equity shares are excluded unless they are, in substance, cash equivalents (e.g., preference shares acquired shortly before their specific redemption date with insignificant risk of default).

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are the CFO of a startup. Your bank balance shows ₹50 lakh, but you also hold ₹20 lakh in a 6-month Fixed Deposit, ₹10 lakh in Treasury Bills maturing in 45 days, and ₹5 lakh in shares of a blue-chip company. A potential investor asks: "What is your *true* liquid cash position today?" You cannot simply add everything. The 6-month FD isn't instantly accessible without penalty; the shares fluctuate daily. AS-3 solves this by defining a strict boundary: only items that are **as good as cash** — available on demand or maturing within 3 months with near-zero value risk — go into "Cash and Cash Equivalents." This node teaches you to draw that line precisely. Start by memorising the two definitions verbatim. Then, for every instrument, ask: "Is it cash on hand/demand deposit?" (Cash) OR "Is it a highly liquid investment maturing ≤ 3 months with insignificant risk?" (Cash Equivalent). If no to both, it's excluded. The key insight: **Cash Equivalents are not just "short-term investments" — they are substitutes for cash held to meet short-term commitments, not for investment returns.**

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
State the two components of "Cash" as defined in AS-3.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Correct Answer: (1) Cash in hand (physical currency), (2) Demand deposits with banks (balances withdrawable on demand without notice).  
A wrong answer (e.g., including "time deposits" or "cheques in hand not yet deposited") reveals the student confuses **Cash** (immediately available) with **near-cash items** that require conversion time or bank processing. AS-3 is strict: only demand deposits qualify as Cash; fixed/time deposits are evaluated under Cash Equivalents only if ≤ 3 months maturity.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
A company holds the following on March 31, 2024: (a) ₹2,00,000 in a Savings Account, (b) ₹5,00,000 in a Fixed Deposit maturing on June 15, 2024 (opened March 1, 2024), (c) ₹3,00,000 in Equity Shares of Reliance Industries (highly liquid, traded daily), (d) ₹1,00,000 in 10% Preference Shares of X Ltd. acquired on March 20, 2024, redeemable on April 30, 2024. Classify each as Cash, Cash Equivalent, or Neither per AS-3. Justify each classification in one sentence.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Correct Answer:  
(a) **Cash** — Demand deposit withdrawable anytime.  
(b) **Cash Equivalent** — Maturity = 106 days (March 1 to June 15) ≈ 3.5 months > 3 months → **Neither** (fails 3-month rule).  
(c) **Neither** — Equity shares are explicitly excluded regardless of liquidity; value risk is significant.  
(d) **Cash Equivalent** — Preference shares acquired shortly before specific redemption date (April 30), maturity ~40 days (< 3 months), insignificant default risk → qualifies as per NCERT example.  
A wrong answer classifying (b) as Cash Equivalent shows the student ignores the **exact 3-month cutoff from acquisition date**. ## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
PQR Ltd. has a bank overdraft of ₹1,50,000 repayable on demand. The company also holds ₹4,00,000 in a Fixed Deposit maturing in 2 months. The accountant includes the overdraft as a component of "Cash and Cash Equivalents" in the Cash Flow Statement, netting it against the FD. Is this correct per AS-3? Explain why or why not.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Correct Answer: **Incorrect.** AS-3 defines Cash Equivalents as *assets* (investments). Bank overdraft is a **liability** (short-term borrowing). While some frameworks (like IAS-7) allow netting overdrafts if they are integral to cash management, **AS-3 (as per NCERT) does not explicitly permit this netting in the definition of Cash and Cash Equivalents**. The overdraft should be shown under **Financing Activities** (short-term borrowings) or as a separate line item in the balance sheet. The FD (maturity 2 months < 3 months) is a Cash Equivalent. Netting them misstates the true cash position and violates the gross presentation principle of AS-3.  
A wrong answer ("Yes, it's correct because overdraft is repayable on demand") reveals confusion between **cash management practice** and **accounting standard definition**. The student must distinguish: Cash Equivalents are *assets held*; overdraft is a *liability owed*.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare and contrast: Why does AS-3 allow **preference shares acquired shortly before redemption** as Cash Equivalents but **excludes equity shares** even if they are highly liquid and traded on a stock exchange? Your answer must reference the two criteria: "known amount of cash" and "insignificant risk of changes in value."

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Correct Answer:  
Preference shares near redemption have a **fixed redemption amount** (known cash) and **fixed date** — the only risk is issuer default, which is insignificant if the company is solvent. Thus, they meet both criteria.  
Equity shares have **no fixed maturity** and **no fixed redemption value** — their market price fluctuates daily (significant value risk). Even if highly liquid, the amount realizable is **not known** in advance. Hence, they fail both criteria.  
A weak answer ("Preference shares are safer") misses the precise AS-3 logic: it's about **certainty of amount and timing**, not general safety. The student must articulate that "known amount" = fixed redemption value, and "insignificant risk" = no market price volatility.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Critique this statement: *"Any investment with a maturity of less than 3 months automatically qualifies as a Cash Equivalent."* Provide a counter-example from the NCERT text and explain which AS-3 criterion it violates.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Correct Answer: The statement is **false**. Maturity ≤ 3 months is **necessary but not sufficient**. NCERT gives the counter-example: **Investments in shares are excluded unless they are in substance cash equivalents (like preference shares near redemption).** An equity share portfolio held for 2 months violates the **"insignificant risk of changes in value"** criterion — its market value can change significantly in 2 months. Similarly, a 2-month corporate bond of a distressed company violates the same criterion due to default risk.  
A wrong answer ("True, 3 months is the rule") shows the student has memorised the maturity rule but missed the **dual criteria** (maturity + risk). This is a classic board exam trap: questions often give a 2-month investment in volatile securities and ask if it's a Cash Equivalent.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Expected Answer (sample):  
**Scenario:** ABC Manufacturing invests ₹50 lakh in **Commercial Paper (CP) of a NBFC** maturing in 60 days. The CP is rated A1+ (high liquidity). However, the NBFC faces a sudden credit rating downgrade risk due to sectoral stress.  
**Classification:** **Not a Cash Equivalent.**  
**Failed Criterion:** "Insignificant risk of changes in value" — the downgrade risk means the realisable value may drop significantly before maturity.

A poor answer (e.g., "It's a Cash Equivalent because maturity < 3 months and rated high") fails to apply the **risk criterion** in a dynamic context. The student must understand that AS-3 requires *judgment* on risk at the reporting date, not just at acquisition.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
**Misconception:** "All short-term investments with maturity less than 3 months are Cash Equivalents."  
**Why it's wrong:** Students memorise the "3-month" rule but ignore the **second limb** of the definition: *"subject to an insignificant risk of changes in value."* They treat maturity as the sole filter.  
**Concrete Error:** In a board question, a company holds 2-month Government Treasury Bills (T-Bills) and 2-month Equity Mutual Fund units. The student classifies **both** as Cash Equivalents.  
**Correct Treatment:** T-Bills = Cash Equivalent (sovereign risk = insignificant, known redemption value). Equity Mutual Fund = **Not** Cash Equivalent (NAV fluctuates daily = significant value risk).  
**Why students make this error:** The NCERT text states the maturity rule first ("say, three months or less") and the risk criterion second. Students latch onto the quantitative cutoff (3 months) because it's easier to apply than the qualitative risk assessment. Competitive exams (CUET, JEE-adjacent finance questions) explicitly test this distinction.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: On 31st March 2024, the Balance Sheet of XYZ Ltd. shows the following under Current Assets: (i) Cash in hand ₹45,000, (ii) Current Account with HDFC Bank ₹3,20,000, (iii) Fixed Deposit with SBI (maturity 2 months, opened 1st Feb 2024) ₹5,00,000, (iv) Fixed Deposit with ICICI (maturity 6 months, opened 1st Jan 2024) ₹10,00,000, (v) Investment in 90-day Commercial Paper of AAA-rated NBFC ₹20,00,000, (vi) Equity Shares of TCS Ltd. (purchased 15th March 2024) ₹15,00,000. Determine the total "Cash and Cash Equivalents" as per AS-3 for the Cash Flow Statement.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify items meeting "Cash" definition: Cash in hand (₹45,000) + Demand deposit (Current Account ₹3,20,000) = ₹3,65,000.
- Step 2: Evaluate each investment for Cash Equivalent criteria: (a) SBI FD: maturity 2 months (< 3 months from 1 Feb to 31 Mar), insignificant risk → **Yes**. (b) ICICI FD: maturity 6 months (> 3 months) → **No** (classified as Other Bank Balance). (c) Commercial Paper: 90-day maturity (< 3 months), AAA-rated → insignificant risk → **Yes**. (d) Equity Shares: explicitly excluded per AS-3, regardless of maturity/liquidity → **No**.
- Step 3: Sum Cash Equivalents: SBI FD ₹5,00,000 + Commercial Paper ₹20,00,000 = ₹25,00,000.
- Step 4: Total Cash and Cash Equivalents = Cash (₹3,65,000) + Cash Equivalents (₹25,00,000) = ₹28,65,000.
- Step 5: Verify: ICICI FD (₹10,00,000) and TCS Shares (₹15,00,000) are excluded. They will appear separately in Balance Sheet (Other Bank Balances / Current Investments) and their cash flows will be shown in Investing Activities.
REASONING: AS-3 requires a two-step filter: first, separate Cash (currency + demand deposits); second, apply the dual test (≤3 months maturity + insignificant risk) to short-term investments. The 3-month clock starts at **acquisition date**, not balance sheet date. Equity shares are a per se exclusion.
ANSWER: ₹28,65,000

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: ABC Ltd. has a Bank Overdraft of ₹2,00,000 (repayable on demand) and a Fixed Deposit of ₹8,00,000 maturing in 45 days. The accountant wants to net the overdraft against the FD and show net Cash and Cash Equivalents of ₹6,00,000. The company also holds ₹50,000 cash in hand. As per AS-3, what is the correct Cash and Cash Equivalents figure? Explain the treatment of the overdraft.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Compute Cash: Cash in hand ₹50,000. Demand deposits? Overdraft is a **liability**, not a demand deposit asset. So Cash = ₹50,000.
- Step 2: Evaluate FD: Maturity 45 days (< 3 months), highly liquid, insignificant risk → **Cash Equivalent** = ₹8,00,000.
- Step 3: Total Cash and Cash Equivalents = ₹50,000 + ₹8,00,000 = **₹8,50,000**. Do **not** net the overdraft.
- Step 4: Overdraft treatment: It is a **Short-term Borrowing** (Financing Activity). In the Cash Flow Statement, it appears under "Cash Flows from Financing Activities" as "Proceeds from short-term borrowings" (if increased) or "Repayment of short-term borrowings" (if decreased). In the Balance Sheet, it is shown under Current Liabilities (Short-term Borrowings), not netted against Cash.
- Step 5: Reconcile: The opening/closing Cash and Cash Equivalents in the Cash Flow Statement will be ₹8,50,000 (or whatever the opening was). The overdraft movement is a separate financing cash flow.
REASONING: AS-3 defines Cash Equivalents as **assets**. Bank overdraft is a **liability**. Netting is not permitted under AS-3 (unlike IAS-7). The Cash Flow Statement shows **gross** cash inflows/outflows for operating, investing, financing. The overdraft is a financing cash inflow when taken, outflow when repaid.
ANSWER: Correct Cash and Cash Equivalents = ₹8,50,000. Overdraft is a financing liability, not netted.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: On 1st April 2023, PQR Ltd. had Cash and Cash Equivalents of ₹12,00,000. During 2023-24, the following occurred: (a) Purchased 91-day Treasury Bills for ₹25,00,000 on 1st Jan 2024. (b) Invested ₹10,00,000 in Equity Mutual Fund on 15th Feb 2024 (highly liquid, redeemable anytime). (c) Received ₹5,00,000 dividend on equity shares. (d) Repaid bank overdraft of ₹3,00,000. (e) Purchased machinery for ₹20,00,000 by cheque. (f) Issued equity shares for ₹30,00,000 cash. Determine the closing Cash and Cash Equivalents on 31st March 2024 and classify each item's cash flow activity.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Opening Cash and Cash Equivalents = ₹12,00,000.
- Step 2: Classify each item's cash flow & effect on Cash and Cash Equivalents:
  (a) T-Bills: 91 days from 1 Jan = maturity 1 April 2024. **Acquisition date 1 Jan, maturity ≤ 3 months (91 days ≈ 3 months), sovereign risk = insignificant** → **Cash Equivalent**. Cash outflow ₹25,00,000 but **no change in total Cash and Cash Equivalents** (one form of cash equivalent converted to another). *Activity: Investing (purchase of cash equivalent — often netted in preparation, but gross presentation shows outflow in Investing, inflow in Operating? No — purchase of cash equivalent is NOT a cash flow per AS-3 para 45: "Cash management includes the investment of excess cash in cash equivalents. Hence, purchase of marketable securities or short-term investment which constitutes cash equivalents is not considered while preparing cash flow statement.") → **No cash flow reported.** Wait, NCERT 6.4: "Cash management includes the investment of excess cash in cash equivalents. Hence, purchase of marketable securities or short-term investment which constitutes cash equivalents is not considered while preparing cash flow statement." So this transaction is **excluded entirely** from the Cash Flow Statement. It does not change Cash and Cash Equivalents total.
  (b) Equity Mutual Fund: Not a Cash Equivalent (equity-based, value risk). Cash outflow ₹10,00,000 → **Decrease in Cash and Cash Equivalents**. *Activity: Investing (purchase of current investment).*
  (c) Dividend received: Cash inflow ₹5,00,000 → **Increase in Cash and Cash Equivalents**. *Activity: Investing (non-finance co.) / Operating (finance co.). Assume non-finance → Investing.*
  (d) Repaid overdraft: Cash outflow ₹3,00,000 → **Decrease in Cash and Cash Equivalents**. *Activity: Financing (repayment of short-term borrowing).*
  (e) Machinery purchase: Cash outflow ₹20,00,000 → **Decrease in Cash and Cash Equivalents**. *Activity: Investing.*
  (f) Issue equity shares: Cash inflow ₹30,00,000 → **Increase in Cash and Cash Equivalents**. *Activity: Financing.*
- Step 3: Net change in Cash and Cash Equivalents = (b) -10,00,000 + (c) +5,00,000 + (d) -3,00,000 + (e) -20,00,000 + (f) +30,00,000 = **+2,00,000**. (Item (a) no effect).
- Step 4: Closing Cash and Cash Equivalents = Opening 12,00,000 + Net change 2,00,000 = **₹14,00,000**.
- Step 5: Verify composition: The T-Bills (₹25,00,000) are now part of Cash Equivalents. The Mutual Fund (₹10,00,000) is a Current Investment. The rest is cash/bank.
REASONING: This example tests the **critical AS-3 rule**: movements *between* Cash and Cash Equivalents (e.g., buying T-Bills with cash) are **non-cash transactions** for the Cash Flow Statement — they are excluded entirely. Only movements *across the boundary* (Cash/Cash Equivalent ↔ Non-Cash) are reported. The Mutual Fund crosses the boundary (Cash → Non-Cash Equivalent Investment), so it's an investing outflow. The T-Bill purchase does not cross the boundary.
ANSWER: Closing Cash and Cash Equivalents = ₹14,00,000. T-Bill purchase: No cash flow reported (excluded). Mutual Fund purchase: Investing outflow. Dividend: Investing inflow. Overdraft repayment: Financing outflow. Machinery: Investing outflow. Share issue: Financing inflow.

The Cash Flow Statement should show it as Cash and Cash Equivalents." Using AS-3 criteria, explain why the student is wrong. COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to accounting standards / Error identification and correction

- Step 2: Apply to the 4-month FD: Maturity = 4 months > 3 months → **Fails Criterion 1**.
- Step 3: Conclude: It is **not** a Cash Equivalent. It is a "Short-term deposit" / "Other Bank Balance".
