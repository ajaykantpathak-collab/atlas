DEFINITION: The equation of trajectory of a projectile is the mathematical relationship between the vertical displacement (y) and horizontal displacement (x) of an object projected with initial velocity \(v_0\) at an angle \(\theta_0\) to the horizontal, under constant gravitational acceleration \(g\) and negligible air resistance. It is given by \(y = x \tan\theta_0 - \frac{g x^2}{2 v_0^2 \cos^2\theta_0}\), which describes a parabolic path in the vertical plane.
WALKTHROUGH: Watch a cricket ball hit for a six — it follows a curved path. That curve is not circular; it is a parabola. We already know the horizontal motion is uniform (\(x = v_0 \cos\theta_0 \, t\)) and vertical motion is uniformly accelerated (\(y = v_0 \sin\theta_0 \, t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2\)). To find the *shape* of the path, we need a direct relation between \(y\) and \(x\) without time. Solve the first equation for \(t = \frac{x}{v_0 \cos\theta_0}\) and substitute into the second. The result is a quadratic in \(x\), confirming the parabolic trajectory. This equation is the "fingerprint" of the projectile — it tells us the height at any horizontal distance.
D6 Q: Design a simple experiment using a smartphone camera and a ball thrown in a darkened room with a strobe light (or video analysis) to verify that the trajectory is parabolic. What measurements would you take, and how would you plot the data to confirm the quadratic relationship?
D6 A: Record a video of a ball thrown at an angle (e.g., 30°) against a grid background. Use video analysis software (like Tracker) to mark the ball's position \((x, y)\) in each frame. Plot \(y\) vs \(x\). Fit a quadratic curve \(y = ax + bx^2\). The coefficient \(b\) should be negative. Calculate theoretical \(b_{\text{theory}} = -\frac{g}{2u^2\cos^2\theta}\) using measured \(u\) and \(\theta\). Compare \(b_{\text{exp}}\) and \(b_{\text{theory}}\). A good answer includes: controlling for camera angle (perpendicular to plane of motion), calibrating pixels to meters using the grid, and acknowledging air resistance causes slight deviation at high speeds. A poor answer suggests plotting \(y\) vs \(t\) (which is also quadratic but doesn't test the *trajectory* equation) or ignores calibration.

DEFINITION: The law of conservation of momentum states that for an isolated system (no net external force), the total vector momentum remains constant in time: \(\sum \vec{p}_{\text{initial}} = \sum \vec{p}_{\text{final}}\). Momentum \(\vec{p} = m\vec{v}\) is a vector quantity measured in kg·m/s. The law follows directly from Newton's second and third laws and holds for all collisions and explosions in isolated systems, regardless of whether kinetic energy is conserved.
WALKTHROUGH: Watch a cricket bowler deliver a ball: the ball gains forward momentum while the bowler experiences a tiny backward recoil. Now imagine the same interaction on a frictionless icy lake — the total momentum of bowler+ball system before release (zero) equals total momentum after. Begin by identifying the system boundaries: are external forces (friction, gravity component along motion) negligible during the brief interaction? If yes, apply \(\sum \vec{p}_i = \sum \vec{p}_f\) as a vector equation. Break into x and y components. For 1D problems, choose a sign convention. Always verify isolation assumption before writing the conservation equation.
D5 Q: A student solves a recoil problem: "A 100 kg gun fires a 10 g bullet at 800 m/s. Find recoil speed." They write: \(0 = 0.01 \times 800 + 100 \times v \Rightarrow v = -0.08\ \text{m/s}\). Is this correct? If not, identify the error and give the correct answer.
D5 A: The equation is correct but the mass conversion is wrong: 10 g = 0.01 kg ✓. \(0 = 8 + 100v \Rightarrow v = -0.08\ \text{m/s}\). Actually correct! Wait — check: \(0.01 \times 800 = 8\ \text{kg·m/s}\). \(100 \times (-0.08) = -8\ \text{kg·m/s}\). Sum = 0 ✓. The negative sign means opposite direction. The student's answer is correct. A wrong evaluation would be claiming the sign is wrong or mass conversion error. This tests careful unit checking and sign convention understanding.

DEFINITION: Kinetic energy is the energy possessed by a body due to its motion, defined as the work needed to accelerate a body of mass \( m \) from rest to velocity \( \vec{v} \). For a particle of mass \( m \) moving with speed \( v \), its kinetic energy \( K \) is given by \( K = \frac{1}{2} m v^2 \). It is a scalar quantity, always non-negative, with SI unit joule (J) and dimensions \([ML^2T^{-2}]\).
WALKTHROUGH: Observe a cricket ball and a tennis ball thrown at the same speed — the cricket ball hurts more because it has more mass. Now imagine the same cricket ball thrown at different speeds — the faster one does more damage. This shows kinetic energy depends on both mass and speed. In Class 9 you learned \( v^2 - u^2 = 2as \) and multiplied by \( m/2 \) to get \( \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{1}{2}mu^2 = mas = Fs = W \). The quantity \( \frac{1}{2}mv^2 \) is kinetic energy, and the change in it equals the net work done. Start by measuring mass (kg) and speed (m/s), then apply \( K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 \).
D6 Q: Design a simple experiment using a school laboratory setup (trolley, light gate, masses, runway) to verify that kinetic energy is proportional to the square of velocity. Outline the procedure, measurements, and how you would analyze the data.
D6 A: Procedure: Place trolley on friction-compensated runway. Attach a card of known length. Use a light gate to measure time \( t \) for card to pass → velocity \( v = \text{length}/t \). Vary starting height to change \( v \). Measure \( v \) and compute \( K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 \). Plot \( K \) vs \( v \) (curved) and \( K \) vs \( v^2 \) (straight line through origin). Slope = \( \frac{1}{2}m \). A poor answer (e.g., "measure speed and energy directly" or no graph analysis) reveals lack of experimental design skills and misunderstanding of how to test a quadratic relationship.

NODE c11_phy_cbse_07_001 verdict=PARTIAL cluster=[11, 'phy', 7] failing_abs=[6]
DEFINITION: Kepler's laws of planetary motion are three empirical principles describing the motion of planets around the Sun: (1) Law of Orbits – Every planet moves in an elliptical orbit with the Sun at one focus; (2) Law of Areas – The radius vector from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal time intervals, a consequence of angular momentum conservation under a central force; (3) Law of Periods – The square of the orbital period \(T\) is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis \(a\) of the ellipse, i.e., \(T^2 \propto a^3\). For circular orbits of radius \(r\) around a central mass \(M\), the precise relation is \(T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}r^3\), where \(G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \, \text{N·m}^2\text{/kg}^2\) is the universal gravitational constant.
WALKTHROUGH: Begin by asking students to observe the night sky over several weeks (or use a free planetarium app like Stellarium) and note that planets like Mars or Jupiter appear to move against the background stars, sometimes even reversing direction (retrograde motion). This historical puzzle – which troubled astronomers for millennia – was resolved by Tycho Brahe's precise naked-eye data and Kepler's analysis. Connect to their prior knowledge of uniform circular motion (Class 9, node c9_physics_ch2_10) and central forces (Class 9, node c9_physics_ch3_4): the Sun's pull is always directed toward the Sun, making it a central force. Then introduce the ellipse using the string-and-pins construction (NCERT Fig. 7.1b) to define semi-major axis \(a\), perihelion, and aphelion. Derive the second law from angular momentum conservation (\(L = mvr_\perp = \text{constant}\)) for a central force, showing that \(\Delta A/\Delta t = L/(2m)\). Finally, present the third law as a proportionality \(T^2 \propto a^3\) and give the exact formula for circular orbits, emphasizing that the constant \(\frac{4\pi^2}{GM}\) depends only on the central mass \(M\).
D6 Q: Design a simple tabletop experiment using a ball on a string (or a puck on an air table) to demonstrate the principle behind Kepler's second law (equal areas in equal times). Describe the setup, what you would measure, and how you would verify the law.
D6 A: Expected answer criteria: 
- Setup: A small mass (ball/puck) attached to a string passing through a hole in a frictionless horizontal surface (air table or smooth glass). The string is pulled from below to provide a central force. Alternatively, a ball on a string whirled horizontally with the hand providing the central force (less ideal because hand moves). 
- Measurement: Mark positions of the mass at equal time intervals (e.g., using a video camera and frame-by-frame analysis). Draw radius vectors from the center to each position. 
- Verification: Compute the area of each triangle formed by two successive radius vectors (area = \(\frac{1}{2} r_1 r_2 \sin \Delta\theta\)). Check if these areas are constant within experimental error. 
- Key point: The force must be purely central (string tension directed exactly toward the center). Any friction or tangential force will cause angular momentum to change, violating the law.

What a poor answer reveals: If the student suggests a vertical circle (like a conical pendulum), gravity adds a non-central component. If they propose measuring speed at different radii without measuring areas, they are testing \(v \propto 1/r\) (which is a consequence) but not the law itself. If they ignore friction, they won't understand why real data deviates.

NODE c11_phy_cbse_11_010 verdict=PARTIAL cluster=[11, 'phy', 11] failing_abs=[6]
DEFINITION: A thermodynamic process is reversible if it can be reversed such that both the system and the surroundings return to their original states with no net change anywhere else in the universe; it is irreversible if it cannot be reversed without leaving a net change in the universe. Reversibility requires the process to be quasi-static (infinitesimally slow, maintaining equilibrium at every stage) and free from dissipative effects like friction, viscosity, or unrestrained expansion.
WALKTHROUGH: Observe a gas leaking from a punctured LPG cylinder in a kitchen – the gas spreads irreversibly throughout the room and never spontaneously returns to the cylinder. Ask: "What would need to happen for this to reverse?" Guide students to see that reversal would require external work (a compressor) and would heat the surroundings, leaving a net change. Contrast with an idealised frictionless piston compressing gas infinitely slowly: at each step, an infinitesimal pressure difference drives the change, and the process can be retraced exactly. Connect to prior nodes: thermal equilibrium (c11_phy_cbse_11_002) defines the quasi-static condition; First Law (c11_phy_cbse_11_003) shows energy conservation; Second Law (c11_phy_cbse_11_009) forbids complete heat-to-work conversion, making reversibility the theoretical limit for maximum efficiency.
D6 Q: Design a thought experiment to test whether the condensation of steam on a cold mirror is reversible or irreversible. Specify the system, surroundings, and the criterion you would use to decide.
D6 A: System: steam + mirror. Surroundings: room air at T_room. Process: steam at T₁ > T_room condenses on mirror at T₂ < T₁. Criterion: check if (a) the process is quasi-static (infinitesimal T difference at interface) and (b) no dissipation occurs. In reality, condensation occurs with finite ΔT across the steam-mirror interface and involves latent heat release into a temperature gradient – both violate quasi-static condition. To reverse, you would need to supply heat from a reservoir at exactly T₂ to evaporate the water, but the mirror is at T₂ < T₁, so heat would flow from hot to cold, leaving a net change. A poor answer that says "it's reversible because you can boil the water again" misses that boiling requires a hotter source, changing the surroundings.

NODE c11_phy_cbse_12_007 verdict=PARTIAL cluster=[11, 'phy', 12] failing_abs=[6]
DEFINITION: Mean free path is the average distance traveled by a gas molecule between two successive collisions with other molecules. It is a statistical quantity representing the mean of the free paths of all molecules in a gas at equilibrium. The SI unit is metre (m). For an ideal gas of rigid spherical molecules of diameter \(d\) and number density \(n\), the mean free path \(\lambda\) is given by \(\lambda = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2} \pi d^2 n}\).
WALKTHROUGH: Observe that when a gas cylinder leaks in a kitchen, the smell takes minutes to reach the other corner even though molecules move at ~500 m/s. This is because molecules collide frequently, deflecting their paths. Imagine a single molecule moving through a crowd of stationary molecules: it sweeps a cylindrical volume \(\pi d^2 \langle v \rangle \Delta t\) in time \(\Delta t\). Any molecule whose centre lies in this cylinder will collide. The number of collisions is \(n \pi d^2 \langle v \rangle \Delta t\), giving collision frequency \(n \pi d^2 \langle v \rangle\). The average distance between collisions is \(\langle v \rangle\) divided by this frequency, yielding \(\lambda = 1/(\pi d^2 n)\). A rigorous treatment replaces \(\langle v \rangle\) with average relative speed \(\langle v_r \rangle = \sqrt{2} \langle v \rangle\), giving the exact formula \(\lambda = 1/(\sqrt{2} \pi d^2 n)\).
D6 Q: Design a thought experiment to estimate the diameter of an air molecule using only macroscopic measurements of mean free path (e.g., by measuring viscosity or diffusion). Outline the steps and the formula you would use.
D6 A: Measure the viscosity \(\eta\) of air at known \(T\) and \(P\). From kinetic theory, \(\eta = \frac{1}{3} n m \langle v \rangle \lambda\). Measure \(\eta\) experimentally. Calculate \(n = P/(k_B T)\), \(m = M/N_A\), \(\langle v \rangle = \sqrt{8 k_B T/(\pi m)}\). Solve for \(\lambda = 3\eta/(n m \langle v \rangle)\). Then use \(\lambda = 1/(\sqrt{2} \pi d^2 n)\) to find \(d\). A poor answer would omit the connection between viscosity and mean free path, or fail to identify all measurable quantities needed.

DEFINITION: Maxwell's Speed Distribution Law describes the statistical distribution of molecular speeds in an ideal gas at thermal equilibrium, giving the fraction of molecules having speeds between v and v+dv as f(v)dv = 4π (m/2πk_BT)^{3/2} v^2 e^{-mv^2/2k_BT} dv, where m is the molecular mass (kg), k_B is the Boltzmann constant (1.38×10^{-23} J/K), and T is the absolute temperature (K). The distribution is asymmetric, peaking at the most probable speed v_p = √(2k_BT/m), with mean speed v_avg = √(8k_BT/πm) and root-mean-square speed v_rms = √(3k_BT/m), satisfying v_p < v_avg < v_rms.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine releasing a drop of perfume in one corner of a classroom – the scent doesn't reach everyone at once because air molecules move at vastly different speeds, not a single uniform speed. In Class 9 you learned that gas pressure arises from molecular collisions, but we treated all molecules as having the same rms speed. Now we ask: what is the actual spread of speeds? Start by plotting a histogram of molecular speeds from a simulation – you'll see a skewed bell curve. The peak gives the most probable speed v_p, the area under the curve is total molecules, and the average speed v_avg is the first moment. Connect this to the kinetic interpretation of temperature (c11_phy_cbse_12_003): since average kinetic energy is fixed by T, lighter molecules must have a broader, right-shifted distribution. The key formula f(v) = 4π (m/2πk_BT)^{3/2} v^2 e^{-mv^2/2k_BT} emerges from maximizing entropy subject to energy constraints.
D6 Q: Design a thought experiment using a velocity selector (rotating slotted disks) to experimentally verify the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution for argon gas at 400 K. Specify the key measurements and how you would extract f(v).
D6 A: Use two coaxial disks with narrow radial slots offset by angle θ, separated by distance L. Only molecules with speed v = ωL/θ pass through both slots. Vary ω and measure the transmitted flux I(ω) with a detector. The flux is proportional to v^3 f(v) (accounting for flux ∝ v and the v^2 in f(v)). Plot I(ω) vs v; normalize area to 1. Compare with theoretical f(v). A poor answer omits the v^3 factor or assumes the selector transmits all speeds equally.

DEFINITION: Periodic motion is a motion that repeats itself identically after a fixed interval of time called the period (T). Oscillatory motion is a special type of periodic motion where a particle moves to and fro about a stable equilibrium position under the action of a restoring force. The frequency (ν) is the reciprocal of the period (ν = 1/T) measured in hertz (Hz). Displacement in oscillations refers to the change with time of any physical quantity (position, angle, voltage, pressure) from its equilibrium value and can be represented by a sinusoidal function of time.
WALKTHROUGH: Observe a ceiling fan rotating at constant speed — its motion repeats but the blade does not move to and fro about a fixed point; it is periodic but not oscillatory. Now observe a swing in a playground: the child moves back and forth about the lowest point (equilibrium). Use a stopwatch to measure the time for 10 complete to-and-fro motions of the swing; divide by 10 to get the period T. Recall from Class 7 (c7_physics_ch04_002) that the pendulum's period depends on length. The number of oscillations per second is the frequency ν = 1/T. Generalize: the voltage across a capacitor in an AC circuit also oscillates — its displacement is voltage, not position.
D4 Q: Figure shows four x-t graphs. Graph A: straight line with positive slope. Graph B: parabola opening upward. Graph C: repeating sinusoidal curve. Graph D: repeating sawtooth pattern (sharp peaks). Which graphs represent periodic motion? For the periodic ones, which represent oscillatory motion? Explain your reasoning.
D4 A: Graphs C and D are periodic (repeat after a fixed interval). Graph C is oscillatory (sinusoidal, symmetric about an equilibrium position, implying a restoring force). Graph D is periodic but not oscillatory (the motion is not symmetric to-and-fro about a mean position; it resembles a particle moving with constant velocity and instantly reversing, which requires infinite force). A wrong answer shows inability to distinguish oscillatory from non-oscillatory periodic motion based on symmetry and physical plausibility.

NODE c11_phy_cbse_13_008 verdict=PARTIAL cluster=[11, 'phy', 13] failing_abs=[5]
DEFINITION: Forced oscillations occur when an external periodic force drives a damped oscillator at a frequency different from its natural frequency; resonance is the phenomenon where the driving frequency matches the natural frequency of the system, causing the amplitude to reach a maximum limited only by damping. The steady-state amplitude is given by A = (F₀/m) / √[(ω₀² - ω²)² + (2βω)²] where F₀ is the driving force amplitude (N), m is mass (kg), ω₀ is natural angular frequency (rad/s), ω is driving angular frequency (rad/s), and β = b/2m is the damping constant (s⁻¹) with b being the damping coefficient (N·s/m).
WALKTHROUGH: Watch a swing in a playground: if you push it at random times, it barely moves, but if you push exactly when it starts moving forward each time, the swing goes higher and higher — this is resonance. In the lab, attach a mass-spring system to a vibration generator and vary the driving frequency while measuring amplitude; you will see a sharp peak at the natural frequency. Connect this to the damped SHM you studied: the external force adds energy each cycle, while damping removes it. The balance between energy input and dissipation determines the steady-state amplitude. The key formula emerges from solving mẍ + bẋ + kx = F₀ cos(ωt) for the particular solution.
D5 Q: A bridge has a natural frequency of 0.5 Hz. Soldiers marching in step at 120 steps/minute cross it. Will resonance occur? If the commander orders "break step," explain why this prevents resonance using the concept of driving frequency spectrum.
D5 A: Marching frequency = 120/60 = 2 Hz. Natural frequency = 0.5 Hz. No resonance because 2 Hz ≠ 0.5 Hz. However, the periodic marching force contains Fourier harmonics: the 4th harmonic (4 × 0.5 Hz = 2 Hz) matches the marching fundamental, but the bridge's natural frequency 0.5 Hz could be excited by the 1st harmonic of a 0.5 Hz component — but marching at 2 Hz has no 0.5 Hz component. "Break step" destroys the periodicity, spreading the driving energy over a continuous spectrum instead of discrete harmonics, so no single frequency component matches the natural frequency strongly. A wrong answer claiming resonance occurs shows confusion between frequency and its harmonics; claiming break step works only by reducing force magnitude misses the spectral argument.
