NODE c12_phy_cbse_02_007 verdict=PARTIAL cluster=[12, 'phy', 2] failing_abs=[6]
DEFINITION: In electrostatic equilibrium, a conductor is a material containing mobile charge carriers (free electrons in metals) that redistribute themselves such that the electric field inside the conductor is zero, the conductor becomes an equipotential volume, and any net charge resides entirely on its surface with surface charge density σ (C/m²). An insulator (dielectric) lacks mobile charge carriers; charges remain bound to atoms/molecules and cannot move freely, so the electric field inside can be non-zero and charge distribution is fixed.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine touching a metal car door after sliding across the seat on a dry day — you feel a spark. That spark is excess charge on your body (a conductor) discharging through the air. Now consider a hollow metal box (Faraday cage) used to protect sensitive electronics in a lightning storm: the lightning's enormous electric field exists outside, but inside the cavity the field is zero. Why? Because free electrons in the metal instantly rearrange to cancel any internal field. Start by visualizing a neutral metal sphere placed in a uniform external field E₀. Free electrons drift opposite to E₀, creating induced surface charges that produce an opposing field E_ind inside. In equilibrium, E₀ + E_ind = 0 everywhere inside. Next, use a Gaussian pillbox straddling the surface to prove E = σ/ε₀ normal to the surface. Finally, argue that since E=0 inside, potential V is constant (∇V = -E = 0), making the entire conductor an equipotential volume. This logic extends to cavities: any cavity inside a conductor, even with external fields, has E=0 if no charges are inside the cavity.
D6 Q: Design a simple experiment using household items to demonstrate electrostatic shielding (Faraday cage effect). List materials, procedure, and the expected observation that proves the interior field is zero.
D6 A: Materials: Metal mesh strainer or aluminum foil wrapped around a cardboard box (cage), a small battery-operated AM radio (or a phone receiving a call), a source of strong electromagnetic noise (e.g., electric drill, hair dryer, or lightning — but safely: a piezoelectric gas lighter spark near the cage).
Procedure: (1) Turn on the radio and tune to a station with static. (2) Bring the noise source close — static increases. (3) Place the radio inside the metal cage (ensure no gaps). (4) Bring the noise source close again.
Observation: Static/noise disappears or drastically reduces when the radio is inside the closed metal cage, even though the noise source is outside.
Reason: The cage's free charges rearrange to cancel external fields inside the cavity. A poor answer (e.g., "put phone in microwave") uses a device not designed for low-frequency EM shielding or misses the key requirement of a conductive enclosure with no large apertures.

NODE c12_phy_cbse_08_004 verdict=PARTIAL cluster=[12, 'phy', 8] failing_abs=[5]
DEFINITION: An electromagnetic wave is a self-propagating transverse wave consisting of mutually perpendicular, sinusoidally oscillating electric field vector E and magnetic field vector B, both perpendicular to the direction of propagation k. In vacuum, it travels at the universal constant speed \(c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0} = 3.00 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}\), with the field amplitudes related by \(E_0 = c B_0\). The wave carries energy, momentum, and angular momentum, and exerts radiation pressure on surfaces.
WALKTHROUGH: Observe a microwave oven: the rotating turntable ensures even heating because the microwaves form standing waves with hot and cold spots — this reveals that EM waves carry energy in a spatially varying pattern. Begin by recalling from Class 11 (c11_phy_cbse_14_001) that transverse waves have oscillations perpendicular to propagation. Use Maxwell’s equations (c12_phy_cbse_08_002) to deduce that a changing E produces B and vice versa, forcing E, B, k to be mutually perpendicular. Derive \(c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}\) from the wave equation. Introduce the sinusoidal form \(E_x = E_0 \sin(kz - \omega t)\), \(B_y = B_0 \sin(kz - \omega t)\). Show that energy density \(u_E = u_B\) leads to intensity \(I = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 c E_0^2\). Finally, connect momentum \(p = U/c\) to radiation pressure \(P = I/c\) (absorption) and \(2I/c\) (reflection) — a measurable force used in solar sails.
D5 Q: A student argues: “Since light carries momentum, a flashlight should recoil like a gun when turned on. Why don’t we observe this?” Evaluate this statement quantitatively. Estimate the recoil force of a 5 W flashlight and compare it to the weight of a mosquito (\(mg \approx 2 \times 10^{-5} \text{ N}\)).
D5 A: The student is correct in principle: light carries momentum \(p = U/c\), so power \(P\) exerts force \(F = P/c\) (for absorption). For a 5 W flashlight, \(F = 5 / (3 \times 10^8) = 1.67 \times 10^{-8} \text{ N}\). This is \(\sim 1000\times\) smaller than a mosquito’s weight (\(2 \times 10^{-5} \text{ N}\)), hence imperceptible. The statement is physically sound but the effect is negligible for everyday power levels. A wrong evaluation might say “light has no mass so no momentum” (confusing rest mass with relativistic momentum) or overestimate force by using \(F = P/v\) with wrong \(v\).

NODE c12_phy_cbse_09_003 verdict=PARTIAL cluster=[12, 'phy', 9] failing_abs=[4]
DEFINITION: Refraction is the bending of a light ray when it passes obliquely from one transparent medium into another due to a change in its speed. Snell's law states that for a given pair of media and a given wavelength of light, the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence (i) to the sine of the angle of refraction (r) is a constant, called the relative refractive index (n₂₁): n₂₁ = sin i / sin r. The angles are measured from the normal at the point of incidence. The absolute refractive index of a medium (n) is the ratio of the speed of light in vacuum (c) to its speed in that medium (v): n = c/v.
WALKTHROUGH: Observe a pencil partially immersed in a glass of water — it appears bent at the water surface. This everyday observation is refraction. Begin by placing a rectangular glass slab on a white sheet, trace its outline, and direct a pin at an angle to one face. View from the other side and place two more pins so all four appear collinear. Remove the slab, join the pin holes, and measure the angles with the normal. You will find sin i / sin r is constant for different angles. This constant is the relative refractive index of glass with respect to air. Connect this to Class 10 knowledge: the speed of light decreases in glass, causing the ray to bend towards the normal. The key formula n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r emerges from the definition n = c/v and the wavefront continuity condition at the boundary.
D4 Q: A student performs the glass slab experiment and obtains the following data for sin i and sin r:

| sin i | 0.20 | 0.40 | 0.60 | 0.80 |
| sin r | 0.13 | 0.26 | 0.39 | 0.52 |

Plot sin i vs sin r mentally. What does the slope represent? If the student accidentally swapped the axes (plotting sin r on y-axis and sin i on x-axis), what would the slope represent then?
D4 A: The data shows sin i / sin r = 0.20/0.13 ≈ 1.54 (constant). Plotting sin i (y) vs sin r (x) gives a straight line through origin with slope = n₂₁ = n_glass/n_air ≈ 1.54. If axes are swapped (sin r vs sin i), the slope becomes 1/n₂₁ = n₁₂ ≈ 0.65. A student who says the slope is always n₂₁ regardless of axes does not understand that slope = Δy/Δx. This tests whether they can interpret graphical representation of Snell's law and the reciprocal relationship between relative refractive indices.

NODE c12_phy_cbse_10_004 verdict=PARTIAL cluster=[12, 'phy', 10] failing_abs=[1]
DEFINITION: Coherent sources are two or more wave sources that maintain a constant phase difference and have the same frequency, enabling stable interference patterns where positions of maxima and minima remain fixed in time. Incoherent sources have a rapidly varying phase difference, causing the interference pattern to fluctuate and time-average to uniform intensity.
WALKTHROUGH: Observe two LED bulbs in your room — they illuminate the wall uniformly without dark/bright bands. Now recall the ripple tank from Class 11 where two vibrating needles created stable nodal/antinodal lines. The difference is coherence: the needles were mechanically linked (same driver), while LEDs are independent. In Young's 1801 experiment, he used a single pinhole to illuminate two closely spaced pinholes, "locking" their phases. We begin by writing displacements y₁ = a cos ωt and y₂ = a cos(ωt + φ), then superpose them using trigonometric identities to find resultant amplitude and intensity. The key step is recognizing φ = (2π/λ) × path difference.
D1 Q: Two identical tuning forks of frequency 512 Hz are struck simultaneously. Fork A is at x = 0 m, Fork B is at x = 1.5 m. A microphone at x = 3.0 m detects sound. Speed of sound = 340 m/s. Are the two sources coherent? What is the phase difference at the microphone?
D1 A: Yes, the sources are coherent because they have the same frequency (512 Hz) and maintain a constant phase relationship (both struck simultaneously). Wavelength λ = v/f = 340/512 = 0.664 m. Path difference Δx = (3.0 - 1.5) - (3.0 - 0) = -1.5 m. Phase difference φ = (2π/λ) × |Δx| = (2π/0.664) × 1.5 = 14.2 rad ≡ 1.6 rad (mod 2π). WRONG ANSWER REVEALS: If student says "not coherent because they are separate forks", they confuse independence with incoherence — coherence requires constant phase difference, not common origin. If they forget 2π factor or use degrees/radians inconsistently, they lack unit discipline.

NODE c12_phy_cbse_10_008 verdict=PARTIAL cluster=[12, 'phy', 10] failing_abs=[6]
DEFINITION: Polarization is the phenomenon of restricting the oscillations of the electric field vector of a transverse light wave to a single plane perpendicular to the direction of propagation. Malus's law states that when a plane-polarized light beam of intensity \(I_0\) passes through an analyzer (a second polaroid) whose pass-axis makes an angle \(\theta\) with the polarization direction of the incident light, the transmitted intensity \(I\) is given by \(I = I_0 \cos^2\theta\). The SI unit of intensity is watt per square metre (W/m²); angle \(\theta\) is measured in radians or degrees.
WALKTHROUGH: Observe a pair of polarized sunglasses: when you rotate one lens in front of the other, the view darkens and brightens periodically. This is Malus's law in action. Begin by recalling that light is a transverse electromagnetic wave with the electric field oscillating perpendicular to the propagation direction. Unpolarized light (e.g., from a bulb) has its electric field vector randomly oriented in the transverse plane. A polaroid sheet absorbs the component parallel to its molecular chains and transmits only the perpendicular component, producing linearly polarized light. The transmitted intensity is half the incident intensity because the average of \(\cos^2\theta\) over \(0\) to \(2\pi\) is \(1/2\). When this polarized light passes through a second polaroid (analyzer), only the component of the electric field along the analyzer's pass-axis is transmitted, leading to \(I = I_0 \cos^2\theta\). Demonstrate with two polaroid sheets from a physics lab kit: hold them crossed (90°) to block nearly all light, then insert a third polaroid at 45° between them to show light reappears — a classic JEE/NEET demonstration.
D6 Q: Design a simple experiment using two polaroid sheets, a white LED torch, and a smartphone light sensor app to verify Malus's law. Describe the procedure, the data to be recorded, and how you would analyze the data to confirm the \(\cos^2\theta\) dependence. What precautions would you take to ensure the incident light is unpolarized?
D6 A: Procedure: (1) Place the LED torch facing the smartphone's light sensor (or use the phone's front camera with a light meter app). (2) Hold one polaroid (P1) fixed in front of the torch. (3) Hold the second polaroid (P2) between P1 and the sensor, and rotate P2 in steps of 10° from 0° to 180°. (4) Record the sensor reading (lux) at each angle. (5) Subtract the background reading (with torch off). (6) Plot normalized intensity \(I/I_{\text{max}}\) vs \(\theta\) and compare with \(\cos^2\theta\). To ensure incident light is unpolarized: use a standard white LED torch (not a laser) and verify that rotating a single polaroid in front of the torch (without P1) gives constant intensity. If intensity varies, the torch itself emits partially polarized light; use a diffuser (tracing paper) in front of the torch. A poor answer would omit background subtraction, angle calibration, or the check for unpolarized incident light, indicating the student does not understand experimental controls.

NODE c12_phy_cbse_12_001 verdict=PARTIAL cluster=[12, 'phy', 12] failing_abs=[1]
DEFINITION: Rutherford's nuclear model of the atom states that the entire positive charge and nearly all the mass of an atom are concentrated in a tiny, dense nucleus (radius ~10⁻¹⁵ m), while electrons revolve around this nucleus at distances ~10⁻¹⁰ m, making the atom mostly empty space. The alpha-particle scattering experiment (Geiger-Marsden, 1911) provided the empirical evidence: a narrow beam of 5.5 MeV α-particles (He²⁺ nuclei) directed at a thin gold foil showed most particles passing through undeviated, but a few (1 in 8000) scattered at angles >90°, which could only be explained by a concentrated central charge.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine holding a thin gold leaf used in Indian traditional sweets (varak) — it is so thin that light passes through, yet it is made of gold atoms. Now picture firing tiny, fast, positively charged helium nuclei (α-particles) at this foil. Most zip through as if the foil were empty space, but a few bounce straight back. This shocking result — like firing a cannonball at tissue paper and having it rebound — forces us to conclude that the atom's mass and positive charge are squeezed into a minuscule nucleus. We begin by measuring the α-particle's kinetic energy (from its radioactive source) and the scattering angles (using a ZnS screen and microscope). The key physical principle is conservation of energy: the initial kinetic energy converts entirely to electrostatic potential energy at the distance of closest approach. From this, we derive the formula that gives us the nuclear size upper bound.
D1 Q: State the two key experimental observations from the Geiger-Marsden alpha-scattering experiment that contradicted Thomson's plum pudding model.
D1 A: Correct answer: (1) Most α-particles passed through the gold foil with little or no deflection (indicating atoms are mostly empty space). (2) A very small fraction (~1 in 8000) were scattered at large angles >90°, some even backwards (indicating a concentrated, massive, positively charged nucleus). 
A wrong answer (e.g., "α-particles were absorbed" or "all particles deflected slightly") reveals the student confuses Rutherford's experiment with Thomson's model predictions or misunderstands the rarity of large-angle scattering. The plum pudding model predicted only small deflections because positive charge was diffuse; the observation of rare but violent large-angle scattering is the smoking gun for a tiny nucleus.

NODE c12_phy_cbse_13_001 verdict=PARTIAL cluster=[12, 'phy', 13] failing_abs=[4]
DEFINITION: The atomic number (Z) of an element is the number of protons in its nucleus, which determines the element's chemical identity and its position in the periodic table. The mass number (A) is the total number of nucleons (protons + neutrons) in the nucleus. A nuclide is represented as \(^A_Z X\), where X is the chemical symbol. The neutron number N = A - Z. The atomic mass unit (u) is defined as 1/12th the mass of a carbon-12 atom: 1 u = 1.660539 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.
WALKTHROUGH: Look at a periodic table on your classroom wall — every box shows an integer (atomic number) and a decimal (average atomic mass). The integer is the proton count, which fixes the element's identity. The decimal is a weighted average of isotopic masses. Now imagine a helium balloon: the helium-4 nucleus has 2 protons and 2 neutrons (A=4). If you could magically add a neutron, you'd get helium-3? No, that would be helium-3 (2 protons, 1 neutron). The key is that changing neutron number creates isotopes of the same element. Start by writing the nuclide symbol for carbon-12: \(^{12}_6 C\). Identify Z=6, A=12, N=6. Then do the same for carbon-13 and carbon-14 to see the pattern. This leads to the general relations A = Z + N and the definitions of isotopes/isobars/isotones.
D4 Q: Compare the following pairs and classify each as isotopes, isobars, or isotones: (a) \(^{14}_6 C\) and \(^{14}_7 N\); (b) \(^{12}_6 C\) and \(^{13}_6 C\); (c) \(^{40}_{18} Ar\) and \(^{40}_{20} Ca\); (d) \(^{14}_6 C\) and \(^{15}_7 N\). Explain your reasoning for each.
D4 A: (a) Isobars (same A=14, different Z). (b) Isotopes (same Z=6, different A). (c) Isobars (same A=40, different Z). (d) Isotones (same N=8: for C-14, N=8; for N-15, N=8). A wrong classification (e.g., calling (a) isotopes) reveals the student is matching only one parameter (A or Z) without checking both. The key is: isotopes = same Z; isobars = same A; isotones = same N.
