DEFINITION: Public Issue, Private Placement, and Rights Issue are three distinct methods through which a company can issue shares to raise capital. A Public Issue involves offering shares to the general public through a prospectus. A Private Placement involves offering shares to a select group of investors (maximum 200 in a financial year) without a public prospectus. A Rights Issue involves offering additional shares to existing shareholders in proportion to their current holdings, giving them a pre‑emptive right to subscribe.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine a startup, "GreenTech Ltd.", needs ₹50 crore to expand its solar panel manufacturing. The board debates three routes: (a) go to the public via an IPO, (b) approach a few venture capital funds privately, or (c) offer new shares to current shareholders first. Which route suits which situation? We begin by defining each method, then compare their investor base, disclosure norms, and speed. Next, we see that irrespective of the method, the accounting entries for share capital (share application, allotment, calls) remain similar — only the source of applications differs. The key insight: the *method of issue* affects *who subscribes* and *regulatory compliance*, but the *double‑entry bookkeeping* for share capital follows the same pattern.
D1 Q: A company wants to raise capital quickly without extensive regulatory filings and is willing to limit its investor base to fewer than 200 sophisticated investors. Which method of share issue should it choose?
D1 A: Correct answer: Private Placement. 
A wrong answer (e.g., Public Issue) reveals the student confuses the speed and regulatory burden: a public issue requires a prospectus, SEBI approval, and a minimum subscription of 90%, making it slower and more cumbersome. Private placement is designed for exactly this scenario — fewer investors, less disclosure, faster execution.

DEFINITION: Minimum Subscription is the SEBI-mandated threshold (90% of issued capital) that a company must receive in cash before making any allotment of shares; Over-subscription occurs when applications for shares exceed the number offered; Pro-rata Allotment is the proportional distribution of shares among applicants when over-subscription happens, where each applicant receives shares in proportion to their application.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine a popular IPO like Zomato or Paytm where millions of investors apply but the company only offered 10 crore shares. The company receives applications for 100 crore shares — that's over-subscription! How does the company fairly decide who gets shares? They can't give everyone what they asked for. SEBI says they must use pro-rata allotment — if you applied for 100 shares and the issue is oversubscribed 10 times, you get 10 shares. But what about the money you paid for 100 shares? The extra money gets adjusted against your future allotment and call payments. Let's learn the exact calculation steps and journal entries so you can handle any board or CUET question on this.
D5 Q: A company issued 2,00,000 shares of ₹10 each at par. Applications received for 3,00,000 shares. The board decides to: reject applications for 50,000 shares; make pro-rata allotment to remaining applicants. Application money: ₹3 per share. Allotment: ₹4 per share. Call: ₹3 per share. Evaluate if this decision complies with SEBI minimum subscription. Calculate the pro-rata ratio for the accepted applicants and the amount of application money refunded vs adjusted.
D5 A: Total applications = 3,00,000 shares. Rejected = 50,000 shares. Remaining applicants = 2,50,000 shares applied for 2,00,000 shares issued. Minimum subscription check: 2,00,000 shares issued × ₹10 = ₹20,00,000. 90% = ₹18,00,000. Application money received from all 3,00,000 applicants = 3,00,000 × ₹3 = ₹9,00,000. But rejected applicants' money (50,000 × ₹3 = ₹1,50,000) is refunded. Net from accepted = ₹7,50,000. This is LESS than ₹18,00,000 minimum subscription — DECISION VIOLATES SEBI RULE. Pro-rata ratio for accepted = 2,00,000 : 2,50,000 = 4:5. Refunded = ₹1,50,000. Adjusted = (2,50,000 × ₹3) - (2,00,000 × ₹3) = ₹7,50,000 - ₹6,00,000 = ₹1,50,000. A student who misses the minimum subscription check fails the evaluation competency.

DEFINITION: Trend analysis is a technique of financial statement analysis that expresses each item of a financial statement for a series of years as a percentage of the same item in a chosen base year, thereby revealing the direction and magnitude of changes over time.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine you are a credit analyst at a bank reviewing a loan application from a manufacturing company. The borrower provides five years of revenue data: ₹50 lakh, ₹55 lakh, ₹48 lakh, ₹62 lakh, ₹70 lakh. At first glance, the numbers fluctuate — how do you quickly assess whether the business is growing, stagnating, or volatile? Trend analysis solves this by converting each year's figure into a percentage of a single base year (say, Year 1 = 100%). Suddenly the pattern becomes visible: 100, 110, 96, 124, 140. You can now see a dip in Year 3 but strong recovery thereafter. To begin, you must choose a base year that is 'normal' — free from one-time events like fire losses or asset sales. Then for every subsequent year, divide the current year's figure by the base year figure and multiply by 100. The key insight is that trend percentages remove the distraction of absolute rupee amounts and expose the underlying growth or decline pattern, enabling meaningful inter-period comparison even when the scale of operations changes.
D5 Q: A student computes trend percentages for a company's Net Profit using 2020 as base year. In 2020, the company sold a factory building and recorded a large one-time gain, making Net Profit abnormally high. The student concludes: 'The company's profitability has been declining since 2020 because all later trend percentages are below 100.' Evaluate this conclusion.
D5 A: The conclusion is flawed. The base year 2020 is not 'normal' — it includes a non-recurring gain. Trend percentages below 100 in subsequent years may simply reflect a return to normal profitability, not a decline. The student should have selected a base year without exceptional items. A wrong answer that agrees with the student shows inability to critique base year selection and confusion between one-time and recurring items.

DEFINITION: Internal analysis is the examination of financial statements by persons who have access to the detailed accounting records of the enterprise (e.g., management, employees, internal auditors), while external analysis is conducted by parties who rely solely on published financial statements (e.g., investors, creditors, government agencies, credit rating agencies). The distinction lies in the depth of information access, purpose of analysis, and the specific tools and techniques each user group employs.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine you are the CFO of a large Indian manufacturing company preparing the annual report. The board of directors (internal users) asks for a detailed breakdown of why raw material costs increased by 18% — they want to see purchase orders, vendor contracts, and production waste reports. Simultaneously, a potential investor (external user) reads the same annual report and only sees "Cost of Materials Consumed" as a single line item in the Statement of Profit and Loss. How would each analyse the company's performance differently? Start by listing all parties interested in a company's financial health. Group them by whether they work inside the organisation or outside it. Then ask: what data can each group actually see? The key insight is that internal users analyse to *control and improve* operations using granular data, while external users analyse to *decide on resource allocation* (invest, lend, regulate) using summarised, published data.
D5 Q: Evaluate this statement: "Since external users only have access to published financial statements, their analysis is always less reliable than internal analysis." Defend or refute with two specific arguments.
D5 A: Correct answer: Refute. Argument 1: External analysis uses *audited* financial statements verified by independent Chartered Accountants, which adds credibility; internal analysis may use unaudited management accounts subject to bias or window-dressing. Argument 2: External users (especially institutional investors, rating agencies) apply *standardised, comparable frameworks* (Ind AS/IFRS, industry benchmarks) enabling cross-firm comparison; internal analysis is often firm-specific and non-comparable. A wrong answer that agrees with the statement reveals the student equates "more data access" with "more reliable conclusion," ignoring the *quality assurance* (audit) and *comparability* (standards) that strengthen external analysis.

DEFINITION: As per Accounting Standard-3 (AS-3), Cash comprises cash in hand and demand deposits with banks (i.e., balances that can be withdrawn at any time without notice). Cash Equivalents are short-term, highly liquid investments that are readily convertible into known amounts of cash and are subject to an insignificant risk of changes in value. To qualify as a cash equivalent, an investment must have a short maturity of three months or less from the date of acquisition. Investments in equity shares are excluded unless they are, in substance, cash equivalents (e.g., preference shares acquired shortly before their specific redemption date with insignificant risk of default).
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine you are the CFO of a startup. Your bank balance shows ₹50 lakh, but you also hold ₹20 lakh in a 6-month Fixed Deposit, ₹10 lakh in Treasury Bills maturing in 45 days, and ₹5 lakh in shares of a blue-chip company. A potential investor asks: "What is your *true* liquid cash position today?" You cannot simply add everything. The 6-month FD isn't instantly accessible without penalty; the shares fluctuate daily. AS-3 solves this by defining a strict boundary: only items that are as good as cash — available on demand or maturing within 3 months with near-zero value risk — go into "Cash and Cash Equivalents." This node teaches you to draw that line precisely. Start by memorising the two definitions verbatim. Then, for every instrument, ask: "Is it cash on hand/demand deposit?" (Cash) OR "Is it a highly liquid investment maturing ≤ 3 months with insignificant risk?" (Cash Equivalent). If no to both, it's excluded. The key insight: Cash Equivalents are not just "short-term investments" — they are substitutes for cash held to meet short-term commitments, not for investment returns.
D5 Q: Critique this statement: *"Any investment with a maturity of less than 3 months automatically qualifies as a Cash Equivalent."* Provide a counter-example from the NCERT text and explain which AS-3 criterion it violates.
D5 A: Correct Answer: The statement is false. Maturity ≤ 3 months is necessary but not sufficient. NCERT gives the counter-example: Investments in shares are excluded unless they are in substance cash equivalents (like preference shares near redemption). An equity share portfolio held for 2 months violates the "insignificant risk of changes in value" criterion — its market value can change significantly in 2 months. Similarly, a 2-month corporate bond of a distressed company violates the same criterion due to default risk. 
A wrong answer ("True, 3 months is the rule") shows the student has memorised the maturity rule but missed the dual criteria (maturity + risk). This is a classic board exam trap: questions often give a 2-month investment in volatile securities and ask if it's a Cash Equivalent.
