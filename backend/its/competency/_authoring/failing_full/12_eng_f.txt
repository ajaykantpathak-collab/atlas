DEFINITION: Irony is a literary device where the intended meaning of words or events is opposite to their literal or surface meaning, creating a contrast between expectation and reality. Symbolism is the use of objects, characters, settings, or actions to represent abstract ideas or qualities beyond their literal sense. In "Going Places," irony exposes the gap between Sophie's fantasies and her socio-economic reality, while symbols like the arcade, the canal path, and Danny Casey embody her yearning for escape, the secrecy of her inner world, and the unattainable ideal of hero-worship.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine you are scrolling through social media and see a friend post a photo of a luxury vacation with the caption "Living my best life #blessed," but you know they are drowning in debt and took a loan for the trip. That gap between the curated image and the hidden reality is irony — and the vacation photo itself becomes a symbol of the life they *wish* they had. In "Going Places," Sophie does exactly this: she constructs a fantasy life around Danny Casey and a boutique, while her actual world is a cramped, steamy kitchen and a predetermined future in a biscuit factory. To analyze this, first locate the moments where what Sophie says clashes with what the reader knows is true (irony). Then, track recurring objects or places that carry emotional weight beyond their physical description (symbolism). Ask: "What does this object *stand for* in Sophie's mind versus what it *is* in reality?" The key insight is that Barton uses irony not to mock Sophie but to evoke empathy: the tragedy is that her symbols (Casey, the canal meeting) are the only agency she has in a world that has already scripted her future.
D1 Q: Define verbal irony and give one example from "Going Places" where Sophie uses verbal irony (or where her words are ironic in hindsight).
D1 A: Correct Answer: Verbal irony occurs when a speaker says something but means the opposite, or when the literal meaning contradicts the intended meaning. In the story, when Sophie tells Jansie, "I'll find it [money for a boutique]... I'll be a manager... I'll be like Mary Quant," her words are verbally ironic because the reader knows (and Jansie knows) that Sophie has no capital, no connections, and is earmarked for the biscuit factory — her confident assertions are the opposite of her achievable reality. 
What a wrong answer reveals: If a student cites Sophie's father saying "This another of your wild stories?" as verbal irony, they confuse a character's *dismissal* with the device itself. Verbal irony must reside in the *speaker's* intent or the *text's* structural contradiction, not in another character's reaction. If they cannot distinguish verbal irony from mere lying or exaggeration, they miss the literary function: irony here is structural, not intentional deceit.
D2 Q: Explain the difference between situational irony and dramatic irony. Then identify which type operates when Sophie waits by the canal for Danny Casey, and why.
D2 A: Correct Answer: Situational irony arises when the outcome of a situation is starkly different from what was expected (e.g., a fire station burning down). Dramatic irony occurs when the audience/reader knows something that a character does not (e.g., we know Sophie's meeting is imagined, but Sophie herself believes it will happen). At the canal, dramatic irony operates: the reader has already seen Sophie invent the encounter in the arcade and heard Geoff say "As if he'd ever show up," so we know Danny Casey will not come. Sophie, however, waits with genuine anticipation ("wishing Danny would come"). The irony deepens because *we* also know that even if Casey *did* appear, he would not match her fantasy — but the primary layer is the reader's superior knowledge. 
What a wrong answer reveals: If a student labels this situational irony ("she expected him, he didn't come"), they are describing a *disappointment*, not the structural irony of the narrative. The situational irony would be if Casey *did* come but turned out to be rude — the reversal of expectation. Here, the expectation (Casey comes) is never a realistic possibility; the irony is that the *reader* sees the futility while Sophie lives it. Confusing the two shows the student cannot distinguish character-level surprise from reader-level awareness.
D3 Q: Apply the concept of symbolism: The "solitary elm" by the canal appears twice — once as a childhood play spot, once as the waiting place for Danny Casey. What does this elm symbolize, and how does its meaning shift between the two appearances?
D3 A: Correct Answer: The solitary elm symbolizes isolation and the threshold between fantasy and reality. In the first mention ("a place she had often played in when she was a child"), it represents innocent, solitary childhood — a safe, private space. In the second appearance ("a wooden bench beneath a solitary elm where lovers sometimes came... the perfect place... for a meeting of this kind"), it transforms into a staging ground for her romantic fantasy: the elm now witnesses not child's play but a hoped-for tryst with a celebrity. The shift from "played in" to "waiting for Danny Casey" marks the elm as a symbol of Sophie's refusal to grow up — she repurposes a childhood refuge into an adult rendezvous point, but the elm remains *solitary*, mirroring her emotional isolation. The symbol deepens because the elm does not change; only Sophie's projection onto it changes. 
What a wrong answer reveals: If a student says "it symbolizes nature" or "it's just a setting detail," they treat the object literally and miss the *pattern of recurrence* that signals symbolism. If they say "it symbolizes her love for Danny Casey," they confuse the *object of her fantasy* with the *symbol of her isolation*. The elm is not about Casey; it's about Sophie's solitary waiting.
D4 Q: Analyze how the boutique functions as a symbol that evolves across the story. Trace its mentions from Sophie's opening declaration to her father's reaction ("This another of your wild stories?") to the final canal scene.
D4 A: Correct Answer: The boutique symbolizes Sophie's desire for autonomy and creative identity in a world that denies her both. 
- Opening: "I'm going to have a boutique... I'll be like Mary Quant" — the boutique is a *vehicle for self-invention*, a space where she controls aesthetics, labor, and success. 
- Middle: Father's grimace ("Where'd you hear that?... This another of your wild stories?") turns the boutique into a *site of class conflict* — her father sees it as delusion; the reader sees it as a legitimate aspiration crushed by economic reality. 
- End: At the canal, the boutique vanishes from her thoughts; she is wholly consumed by Danny Casey. The boutique has been displaced by a more potent symbol (Casey) because the boutique requires *money and work* — things she cannot access — while Casey requires only *imagination*. The boutique thus symbolizes the *failed pragmatic dream*; Casey symbolizes the *escapist fantasy* that replaces it. 
What a wrong answer reveals: Listing the three mentions without explaining the *shift* (pragmatic dream → class conflict → displacement) shows the student sees symbols as static labels rather than dynamic thematic engines. If they claim the boutique "symbolizes her greed," they impose a moral judgment the text does not support — Barton portrays her aspiration as poignant, not vulgar.
D5 Q: Evaluate the following claim: "The irony in 'Going Places' is cruel because it mocks a working-class girl for dreaming beyond her station." Defend or refute using textual evidence.
D5 A: Correct Answer: Refute. The irony is *tragic*, not cruel. Barton aligns the reader's sympathy with Sophie, not with the forces that constrain her. Evidence: 
1. Narrative perspective: The story is filtered through Sophie's consciousness ("She saw herself riding there behind Geoff... the world rose to greet them"). We experience her fantasy as vividly as she does. 
2. Jansie as foil: Jansie's sensible resignation ("They don't pay well for shop work... you really should be sensible") is portrayed as *melancholy*, not wisdom. The text says Jansie "became melancholy. She wished Sophie wouldn't say these things" — implying Sophie's dreams are a *light* in the gloom. 
3. The ending: The final paragraph returns to the arcade fantasy ("He turns, reddening slightly... the soft melodious voice... the prodigy"). The story *ends* in her imagination, not in the factory. If the irony were mocking, the last word would be the factory floor; instead, it is the "shimmer of green eyes." 
4. Authorial tone: The description of her home ("steamy... cluttered... heavy-breathing man... dirty washing") evokes *suffocation*, not ridicule. The irony serves to heighten the pathos of a imagination too large for its container. 
What a wrong answer reveals: Agreeing with the claim suggests the student reads irony as *authorial judgment* rather than *structural contrast*. They may also confuse the *father's* disdain (which *is* class-contemptuous) with the *author's* stance. This is a critical board-exam error: conflating a character's voice with the narrative voice.
