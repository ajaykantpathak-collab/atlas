DEFINITION: Individual differences refer to the distinctiveness and variability among people in their psychological characteristics (such as intelligence, personality, aptitude, interests, values) and behavior patterns, arising from the complex interplay of hereditary (nature) and environmental (nurture) factors, and the interaction between personal traits and situational demands.
WALKTHROUGH: Start by asking students to observe their classmates: "Why do some students finish a math test quickly while others take longer? Why is one friend outgoing at parties but quiet in class?" This real-world observation introduces individual differences. Then guide them to understand that psychologists study these differences through two lenses: traits (internal dispositions) and situations (external pressures). Use the example of a normally aggressive person acting submissive in front of a boss to illustrate situationism. Finally, connect to the nature-nurture interplay: heredity sets a range, environment shapes where in that range a person falls. End with the key insight: individual differences are not just about traits or situations alone, but their dynamic interaction.
D3 Q: A student, Rohan, is usually very talkative and outgoing with friends but becomes quiet and reserved during a formal school assembly. Using the trait-situation debate, explain Rohan's behavior.
D3 A: Rohan's behavior illustrates situationism: the formal assembly situation (presence of teachers, large audience, norms of silence) overrides his trait of talkativeness. The trait perspective would predict he remains talkative across situations. The correct analysis acknowledges that both traits and situations matter; the situation triggers a different behavioral response. A wrong answer might attribute it solely to his trait changing or ignore the situational influence. This reveals inability to apply the interactionist perspective.

DEFINITION: Behaviour therapy is a therapeutic approach based on learning theory principles that identifies malfunctioning behaviours, their antecedent triggers, and consequent maintaining factors, then applies antecedent operations (modifying triggers) and consequent operations (modifying reinforcements/punishments) to replace faulty behaviours with adaptive ones.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine a Class 12 student, Rohan, who procrastinates on studying by scrolling social media until 2 AM, then feels anxious and sleeps poorly. Where would a behaviour therapist start? Not with "why" but with "what, when, and what happens after." First, we define the target behaviour (procrastination/social media use). Next, we identify antecedents: fatigue after school, phone on desk, notification sounds. Then consequences: immediate dopamine relief from boredom, avoidance of difficult subjects. The therapist then designs antecedent operations (phone in another room during study hours, scheduled breaks) and consequent operations (reward for 45-min focused study, removal of phone privilege if exceeded). This ABC analysis is the engine of behaviour therapy — every technique flows from it. Let's break down each component systematically.
D6 Q: Create a novel 5-step behavioural analysis and treatment plan for a college student who compulsively checks phone notifications during lectures, missing key content. Use the exact sequence: target behaviour → antecedents → maintaining consequences → antecedent operation → consequent operation.
D6 A: 1. Target behaviour: unlocking phone and viewing notifications during lecture (count per 50-min class). 2. Antecedents: phone visible on desk, vibration/sound alerts, boredom during dense slides, social anxiety (fear of missing out). 3. Maintaining consequences: immediate relief from FOMO (negative reinforcement), dopamine from social validation (positive reinforcement), avoidance of difficult lecture material (negative reinforcement). 4. Antecedent operation: phone on silent + inside bag under seat (removes visual/tactile cues); pre-lecture 2-min "notification check" scheduled (satiates FOMO). 5. Consequent operation: self-monitoring log — each lecture with ≤1 check earns a point; 10 points = preferred coffee (positive reinforcement); if >3 checks, lose 2 points (response cost). A poor answer lists vague antecedents ("stress"), omits maintaining consequences, or proposes only punishment (e.g., "give phone to friend") without reinforcement for the alternative.

DEFINITION: Cognitive therapy is a structured, short-term psychotherapy that identifies and modifies distorted thinking patterns (cognitions) causing emotional distress and maladaptive behaviours; Ellis' Rational Emotive Therapy (RET) uses the ABC model (Antecedent-Belief-Consequence) to dispute irrational "must/should" beliefs, while Beck's Cognitive Therapy targets negative automatic thoughts and core schemas through collaborative cognitive restructuring.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine your friend says, "I failed one test — I'm a total failure and will never succeed in life." You notice they jump from one event to a global self-verdict. This is exactly what cognitive therapy addresses: the *interpretation* of events, not the events themselves. Start by learning the ABC framework — write down a recent upsetting situation (A), your immediate thought (B), and your feeling/action (C). Then ask: "Is this thought a 'must/should' demand? What evidence supports/contradicts it?" For Beck's approach, identify the "negative automatic thought" (e.g., "I'm stupid"), name the distortion (e.g., labelling), and generate a balanced alternative ("I did poorly on this test; I can study differently next time"). The key insight: emotions follow *beliefs*, not events directly — changing the belief changes the emotion.
D3 Q: Case: Rahul, 17, is not selected for the school football team. He thinks: "The coach hates me. I'll never make any team. I'm useless at sports." 
Using Beck's model, identify: (a) the negative automatic thought (NAT), (b) the cognitive distortion (name it), and (c) the likely core schema activated.
D3 A: (a) Negative Automatic Thought (NAT): "The coach hates me. I'll never make any team. I'm useless at sports." (The *entire cluster* is the NAT — persistent, involuntary, and accepted as true.) 
(b) Cognitive Distortion: Overgeneralisation (drawing a sweeping negative conclusion from a single event) + Labelling ("I'm useless") + Fortune-telling ("I'll never make any team"). *Any one correctly named with explanation earns credit.* 
(c) Core Schema: "I am incompetent / inadequate / unlovable" — a deep, enduring belief formed from early experiences (e.g., repeated criticism from parents) that lies dormant until a critical incident (non-selection) activates it. 

A wrong answer confusing NAT with core schema (e.g., writing "Core schema: The coach hates me") shows the student doesn't grasp the *hierarchy*: Core schema (deep, global) → Critical incident → NATs (situation-specific, multiple) → Distortions (thinking errors in NATs).

DEFINITION: Cognitive Behaviour Therapy (CBT) is a structured, time-limited, evidence-based psychotherapy that integrates cognitive therapy (identifying and restructuring maladaptive thoughts and core schemas) with behavioural techniques (modifying overt behaviours through conditioning principles), grounded in a bio-psychosocial framework addressing biological arousal, psychological cognitions, and social-environmental contingencies.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine a Class 12 student, Rohan, who freezes during board exam mock tests — heart racing, thoughts like "I'll fail, my future is ruined," leading to avoidance of practice papers. How would a CBT therapist help? Start by recognising this as a bio-psychosocial problem: biological (arousal), psychological (catastrophic thoughts), social (exam pressure). First, map the ABC: Activating event (mock test) → Beliefs ("I must score 95% or I'm a failure") → Consequences (panic, avoidance). Then introduce behavioural techniques: graded exposure to timed practice, relaxation training for arousal. Next, cognitive restructuring: identify distortions (all-or-nothing, catastrophising), challenge evidence, develop balanced thoughts. Finally, assign homework — thought records, behavioural experiments. The key insight: CBT doesn't just "talk about feelings" — it actively changes the maintaining cycles across all three domains simultaneously.
D6 Q: Create a mini CBT case formulation for a 17-year-old student, Priya, who experiences panic attacks before chemistry practical exams. Include: (a) bio-psychosocial predisposing/precipitating/perpetuating factors, (b) a vicious cycle diagram description (thoughts → feelings → behaviours → physical), (c) two specific CBT interventions with rationale.
D6 A: (a) Predisposing: Biological — family history of anxiety, high trait neuroticism; Psychological — perfectionistic core schema ("I must be perfect or I'm inadequate"); Social — high parental expectations, competitive school environment. Precipitating: Failed a chemistry practical in Class 11, public criticism by teacher. Perpetuating: Biological — hyperventilation during panic maintains physical symptoms; Psychological — catastrophic misinterpretation of bodily sensations ("heart racing = heart attack"); Behavioural — avoidance of chemistry lab, safety behaviours (carrying water, sitting near exit); Social — parental reassurance reinforces illness role. (b) Vicious cycle: Activating event (entering lab) → Thoughts ("I'll faint, everyone will laugh") → Feelings (terror, shame) → Physical (tachycardia, dyspnea, dizziness) → Behaviours (escape, avoidance) → reinforces Thoughts ("See, I couldn't handle it"). (c) Interventions: 1) Interoceptive exposure + cognitive restructuring: deliberately induce feared sensations (spinning, hyperventilation) in session while testing catastrophic beliefs ("Does dizziness mean heart attack? No, it's hyperventilation") — breaks misinterpretation cycle. 2) Behavioural experiment: graded exposure to lab (visit empty lab → watch peer → assist → perform simple task) with dropout of safety behaviours — tests belief "I cannot cope" and builds self-efficacy. A poor answer listing generic "relaxation and positive thinking" misses case-specific formulation, vicious cycle mechanics, and evidence-based intervention selection.
