DEFINITION: Participant observation is a qualitative research method where the sociologist immerses themselves in a community for an extended period (typically a year or more), participating in daily life while systematically observing and recording social interactions, cultural practices, and meanings from the insider's perspective to produce a holistic account of the 'whole way of life'.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine you want to understand why students in your school's canteen form the same friendship groups every day — not just who sits with whom, but what their gestures, inside jokes, and seating choices *mean* to them. A survey would give you categories; an interview would give you rehearsed answers. But if you spent three months eating lunch with them daily, learning their slang, noticing who gets teased and who mediates conflicts, you'd grasp the *lived logic* of their world. That is participant observation: trading breadth for depth, distance for immersion. The key insight is that the method's greatest strength — the researcher becoming an 'insider' — is also the source of its deepest limitation: the inability to fully separate the researcher's voice from the community's voice.

DEFINITION: Social facts are patterns of behaviour, beliefs, and institutions that exist externally to individuals, exert coercive constraint on individual actions, and emerge from collective association rather than individual psychology; they are the proper subject matter of sociology because they can be studied empirically through aggregated behavioural patterns such as suicide rates, marriage rates, or crime statistics.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine a Class 11 student reads a news report about a farmer's suicide in Vidarbha and thinks, "This is a personal tragedy caused by debt and depression — how can sociology explain an individual's private despair?" This is exactly the starting point Durkheim challenges. Begin by asking: "If suicide is the most personal act imaginable, how can it reveal society's structure?" Guide students to see that Durkheim did not study *why* a particular person died; he studied *why the rate* of suicide varies systematically across groups (Protestants vs Catholics, married vs unmarried, war vs peace). The key insight: when individual cases are aggregated, the 'noise' of personal circumstance cancels out, revealing the 'signal' of social forces — integration and regulation — that constrain even our most private choices.

DEFINITION: Bureaucracy, in Weber's formulation, is the institutional embodiment of rational-legal authority where legitimate domination rests on a belief in the legality of enacted rules and the right of those elevated to authority under such rules to issue commands; it operates through a hierarchical organisation of offices with fixed jurisdictions, governed by written rules, staffed by appointed officials selected on technical qualifications, and separates the office from the person holding it.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine you visit a government office to get a caste certificate: you take a token, wait for your number, submit documents to a clerk who checks them against a checklist, the clerk forwards the file to a supervisor who signs it, and you collect the signed certificate days later.
