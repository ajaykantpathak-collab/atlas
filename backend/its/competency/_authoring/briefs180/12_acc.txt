DEFINITION: Public Issue, Private Placement, and Rights Issue are three distinct methods through which a company can issue shares to raise capital. A Public Issue involves offering shares to the general public through a prospectus. A Private Placement involves offering shares to a select group of investors (maximum 200 in a financial year) without a public prospectus. A Rights Issue involves offering additional shares to existing shareholders in proportion to their current holdings, giving them a pre‑emptive right to subscribe.

Next, we see that irrespective of the method, the accounting entries for share capital (share application, allotment, calls) remain similar — only the source of applications differs. The key insight: the *method of issue* affects *who subscribes* and *regulatory compliance*, but the *double‑entry bookkeeping* for share capital follows the same pattern.

DEFINITION: Trend analysis is a technique of financial statement analysis that expresses each item of a financial statement for a series of years as a percentage of the same item in a chosen base year, thereby revealing the direction and magnitude of changes over time.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine you are a credit analyst at a bank reviewing a loan application from a manufacturing company. The borrower provides five years of revenue data: ₹50 lakh, ₹55 lakh, ₹48 lakh, ₹62 lakh, ₹70 lakh. At first glance, the numbers fluctuate — how do you quickly assess whether the business is growing, stagnating, or volatile? Trend analysis solves this by converting each year's figure into a percentage of a single base year (say, Year 1 = 100%). Suddenly the pattern becomes visible: 100, 110, 96, 124, 140. You can now see a dip in Year 3 but strong recovery thereafter. To begin, you must choose a base year that is 'normal' — free from one-time events like fire losses or asset sales. Then for every subsequent year, divide the current year's figure by the base year figure and multiply by 100. The key insight is that trend percentages remove the distraction of absolute rupee amounts and expose the underlying growth or decline pattern, enabling meaningful inter-period comparison even when the scale of operations changes.

DEFINITION: As per Accounting Standard-3 (AS-3), Cash comprises cash in hand and demand deposits with banks (i.e., balances that can be withdrawn at any time without notice). Cash Equivalents are short-term, highly liquid investments that are readily convertible into known amounts of cash and are subject to an insignificant risk of changes in value. To qualify as a cash equivalent, an investment must have a short maturity of three months or less from the date of acquisition. Investments in equity shares are excluded unless they are, in substance, cash equivalents (e.g., preference shares acquired shortly before their specific redemption date with insignificant risk of default).
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine you are the CFO of a startup. A potential investor asks: "What is your *true* liquid cash position today?" You cannot simply add everything. The 6-month FD isn't instantly accessible without penalty; the shares fluctuate daily. AS-3 solves this by defining a strict boundary: only items that are as good as cash — available on demand or maturing within 3 months with near-zero value risk — go into "Cash and Cash Equivalents." This node teaches you to draw that line precisely. Start by memorising the two definitions verbatim. Then, for every instrument, ask: "Is it cash on hand/demand deposit?" (Cash) OR "Is it a highly liquid investment maturing ≤ 3 months with insignificant risk?" (Cash Equivalent). If no to both, it's excluded. The key insight: Cash Equivalents are not just "short-term investments" — they are substitutes for cash held to meet short-term commitments, not for investment returns.
