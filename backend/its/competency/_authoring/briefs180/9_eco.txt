DEFINITION: Substitute goods are products that satisfy a similar want, so a rise in the price of one increases demand for the other; complementary goods are products used together, so a rise in the price of one decreases demand for the other. The relationship is captured by the cross-demand function: Dx = f(Py), where Py is the price of the related good.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine you run a small tea stall near a Delhi Metro station. Your regular customers buy tea and biscuits together every morning. One day, the price of biscuits jumps from ₹10 to ₹15 per pack because of a wheat shortage. Observe what happens: fewer customers buy biscuits, and because they prefer tea with biscuits, they also buy less tea. This shows tea and biscuits are complements — a price rise in one reduces demand for the other. Now suppose the price of coffee (a substitute for tea) rises. Customers switch to your tea, increasing its demand even though your tea price hasn't changed. This is a rightward shift of the tea demand curve. The key principle: a change in the price of a related good shifts the entire demand curve for the original good, unlike a change in its own price which causes movement along the curve.

D2 A: Cars and petrol are complements. A rise in petrol price increases the cost of using a car, so demand for cars decreases — the demand curve for cars shifts leftward.
