DEFINITION: Contamination in animal cell culture refers to the unwanted presence of microorganisms (bacteria, fungi, yeast, mycoplasma, viruses) or foreign cells that compromise culture integrity, alter cellular physiology, and invalidate experimental results. It arises from aseptic technique breaches, contaminated reagents (especially serum), or environmental sources, and exploits the slow doubling time (18–24 h) of animal cells versus rapid microbial proliferation (20–30 min for bacteria).
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine you are culturing CHO cells for recombinant EPO production. On Day 3, the medium turns yellow‑orange and tiny moving dots appear under the inverted microscope. Step 1: Recognise that a pH drop (phenol red shift) plus motile particles signal bacterial contamination — bacteria divide every 20–30 min, outpacing your 24‑h doubling CHO cells. Step 2: Check the laminar‑flow hood log: was the HEPA filter certified? Was the sash height correct? Step 3: Review reagent sterility — was the FBS heat‑inactivated and filtered? Step 4: Decide whether to discard (bacterial/fungal) or attempt cure (mycoplasma with specific antibiotics). Key insight: Contamination is not random; it follows predictable routes (operator, reagents, equipment) and each contaminant class leaves a distinct signature that guides the response.

DEFINITION: Agrobacterium-mediated transformation is a natural gene transfer mechanism where *Agrobacterium tumefaciens*, a soil bacterium, transfers a defined segment of its Ti (tumour-inducing) plasmid called T-DNA into the genome of wounded dicotyledonous plants, causing crown gall disease; this natural genetic engineering ability is exploited in biotechnology by replacing the oncogenic T-DNA genes with desired transgenes to create transgenic plants.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine you find a tumour-like growth on a rose stem — that is crown gall disease, nature's own genetic engineering. *Agrobacterium* senses wounded plant cells releasing phenolic compounds like acetosyringone, which activates its vir genes to excise T-DNA from the Ti plasmid as a single-stranded T-strand, escort it through a type IV secretion system into the plant cytoplasm, and guide it to the nucleus where it randomly integrates into a chromosome. We hijack this by deleting the oncogenes (auxin/cytokinin synthesis) from T-DNA and inserting our gene of interest plus a selectable marker, using either a binary vector (two plasmids) or co-integrate (single plasmid) system. The key insight: the only cis-acting elements required for transfer are the 25-bp left and right border repeats — everything else (vir genes, origin of replication) acts in trans and can be supplied separately.
D1 Q: What are the minimal cis-acting elements required on the Ti plasmid for T-DNA transfer into a plant cell, and why are the vir genes not considered cis-acting?
D1 A: The minimal cis-acting elements are the 25-bp left border (LB) and right border (RB) direct repeats flanking the T-DNA. The vir genes are not cis-acting because they encode diffusible proteins (VirA, VirG, VirD1/D2, VirB operon, etc.) that act in trans — they can be supplied from a separate plasmid (as in binary vectors) and do not need to be physically linked to the T-DNA.
