DEFINITION: Dynamic programming for sequence alignment is an algorithmic technique that guarantees finding the optimal pairwise alignment (global or local) by breaking the problem into smaller subproblems, storing their solutions in a scoring matrix, and using a recurrence relation that considers match/mismatch scores and gap penalties to build the optimal alignment score step by step.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine you have two short DNA sequences from different strains of *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* isolated in India, and you want to find the exact regions of similarity to track a mutation. You could try aligning them by eye, but with longer sequences that becomes impossible. Dynamic programming solves this by building a grid where each cell represents the best alignment score up to that position. Start by defining a scoring scheme (e.g., +1 for match, -1 for mismatch, -2 for gap). Draw a matrix with one sequence on rows and the other on columns. Initialize the first row and column according to global (cumulative gaps) or local (zeros) rules. Fill each cell by taking the maximum of three possibilities: diagonal (match/mismatch), up (gap in sequence 2), left (gap in sequence 1). Finally, trace back from the highest score (local) or bottom-right (global) to reconstruct the alignment. The key insight is that the optimal alignment of two sequences depends only on the optimal alignments of their prefixes, which is why storing subproblem solutions works.

D5 A: The claim is generally true for linear gap penalties: a more negative gap penalty discourages gaps, so the optimal alignment will tend to have fewer gaps. However, it is not *always* true in the sense that the number of gaps may stay the same if the alignment is dominated by strong matches, or if the change does not cross a threshold that alters the optimal path. D6 A: A good answer: Use a substitution matrix like BLOSUM62 (positive scores for conservative substitutions, negative for non-conservative), set match scores high for identical residues, and use a moderate gap penalty (e.g., -5) to allow gaps within domains but not excessive gaps.
