DEFINITION: Natural numbers (denoted by ℕ) are the set of counting numbers {1, 2, 3, ...} used for ordering and quantifying discrete objects. Whole numbers (denoted by W) extend natural numbers by including zero: W = {0, 1, 2, 3, ...}. Zero is the unique additive identity in W, meaning for any whole number a, a + 0 = a = 0 + a. The discovery of zero as a number (not merely a placeholder) enabled the place-value system and the algebraic structure required for integers, rationals, and real numbers.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine a Class 9 teacher asks: "If you have ₹50 and spend exactly ₹50, how much money do you have left?" You answer "zero rupees." But is "zero" a number you can add, subtract, or multiply like 1, 2, 3? Ancient Indians asked this too. Start by writing the set ℕ = {1, 2, 3, ...} and W = {0, 1, 2, 3, ...}. Notice that 0 ∉ ℕ but 0 ∈ W. Connect to Class 6 (c6_math_ch01_002) where you learned whole numbers include zero. Now, test if zero behaves like other numbers: take any whole number a, compute a + 0 and a × 0. Observe that a + 0 = a (additive identity) but a × 0 = 0 (annihilation property). This distinction is crucial — zero is not "nothing" in multiplication; it actively changes the product. The key insight: zero's dual role as additive identity and multiplicative annihilator makes the whole number system algebraically complete for addition and multiplication, setting the stage for integers where every number gets an additive inverse.

DEFINITION: Representing square roots on the number line is a geometric construction method that uses the Pythagorean theorem to locate the exact position of irrational numbers of the form √n (where n is a positive integer) on the real number line. Starting from a known unit length, we construct a series of right triangles where the hypotenuse of each triangle becomes the leg of the next, allowing us to mark √2, √3, √5, etc., at precise distances from zero.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine you have a 1-metre ruler and need to mark exactly √2 metres without a calculator. You know √2 ≈ 1.414, but how do you locate it precisely? Start by drawing a number line with 0 and 1 marked. At point 1, construct a perpendicular line of length 1 unit using your compass. Join the endpoint to 0 — this hypotenuse is exactly √2 by Pythagoras. Now swing an arc from 0 with radius equal to this hypotenuse to cut the number line: that intersection is √2. To get √3, repeat: at √2, erect a perpendicular of 1 unit, join to 0, and swing the arc. Each step builds on the previous hypotenuse, creating a "square root spiral" that places every √n exactly.

DEFINITION: The perimeter of a plane figure is the total length of its boundary, calculated as the sum of the lengths of all its sides. For a rectangle with length $l$ and breadth $b$, perimeter $P = 2(l + b)$; for a square with side $a$, $P = 4a$; for a triangle with sides $a$, $b$, $c$, $P = a + b + c$.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine you are helping your family fence a rectangular garden that is 12 metres long and 8 metres wide. The fencing wire costs ₹45 per metre. How much wire do you need and what will it cost? Start by drawing the rectangle and labelling the sides. Recall from Class 6 that perimeter is the distance around the shape. For a rectangle, opposite sides are equal, so you add length + breadth + length + breadth, which simplifies to $2(l + b)$. Substitute the values: $2(12 + 8) = 40$ metres. Then multiply by cost per metre: $40 \times 45 = ₹1800$. The key insight is that perimeter is a linear measure (metres) while cost involves multiplication of two quantities (metres × rupees/metre), connecting to the unitary method from Class 7.

DEFINITION: A composite plane figure is a shape formed by combining two or more basic plane figures (rectangles, triangles, circles, sectors, segments) such that they share boundaries but do not overlap. The total area is the algebraic sum of the areas of the constituent parts: areas of added parts are summed, and areas of removed parts (holes, cut-outs) are subtracted.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine a school is designing a new badge: a rectangular strip 10 cm by 3 cm with a semicircular top of diameter 3 cm, and a triangular cut-out at the bottom for a pin. "How much metal is needed?" This real-world fabrication question forces decomposition. First, sketch the figure and label all given dimensions. Second, identify each distinct region: rectangle, semicircle (addition), triangle (subtraction). Third, recall the area formula for each from previous nodes (c9_math_ch6_4, c9_math_ch6_5, c9_math_ch6_9). Fourth, compute each area separately, watching for radius vs. diameter. Finally, combine: Total Area = Area_rectangle + Area_semicircle - Area_triangle. The key insight is that area is additive for non-overlapping regions, and subtraction handles removed material.

DEFINITION: Heron's Formula calculates the area of a triangle when the lengths of all three sides (a, b, c) are known, without requiring the height. The formula is Area = √[s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)], where s = (a+b+c)/2 is the semi-perimeter (half the perimeter) of the triangle.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine a farmer in Punjab has a triangular field with sides measuring 50 m, 60 m, and 70 m. He needs to buy fertilizer but doesn't know the field's area because he cannot measure the height easily. We already know Area = ½ × base × height, but that requires the height which is hard to measure in a large field. Heron, an ancient Greek mathematician, discovered we can find the area using only the three side lengths. First, we find the semi-perimeter s by adding all three sides and dividing by 2. Then we substitute s and the three sides into the formula √[s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)]. The key insight is that the semi-perimeter acts as a "balancing point" that makes the formula work for any triangle shape.
