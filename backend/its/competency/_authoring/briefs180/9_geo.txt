DEFINITION: Coastal landforms are natural features shaped by the dynamic interaction of sea waves, currents, tides, and wind along the shoreline, classified as erosional (cliffs, wave-cut platforms, sea caves, arches, stacks) or depositional (beaches, spits, bars, tombolos, barrier islands, lagoons). Human-landform relationships refer to how coastal communities adapt to, modify, and depend on these features for settlements, ports, fisheries, tourism, and protection against hazards like erosion and storm surges.
WALKTHROUGH: Stand on Marina Beach in Chennai at low tide and observe the wide sandy beach, then walk to the rocky headlands near Mahabalipuram where waves crash against cliffs - you are seeing depositional and erosional landforms side by side. Begin by identifying whether the coast is rocky or sandy, which tells you if erosion or deposition dominates. Connect this to Class 8's "Erosion and Deposition by Sea Waves and Wind" (c9_geo_ch1_9) where you learned waves have erosional power; now we classify the specific landforms they create in sequence. The key principle: coastal landforms exist in a dynamic equilibrium where wave energy, sediment supply, and sea level determine whether a coast builds up or wears away.

D2 Q: Explain why a spit forms at a river mouth but a tombolo forms between an island and the mainland, even though both are depositional landforms made of sand and shingle.
D2 A: A spit forms when longshore drift deposits sediment across a river mouth or bay where the coastline changes direction sharply, while a tombolo forms when wave refraction around an offshore island reduces wave energy in the lee, causing sediment to accumulate and connect the island to the mainland. A wrong answer (e.g., "both form the same way") reveals the student cannot distinguish how coastal geometry and wave refraction control depositional patterns.

DEFINITION: Fold mountains are elevated landforms created when two continental tectonic plates converge, causing the sedimentary rock layers accumulated at the plate margins to crumple, buckle, and fold upwards into a series of parallel ridges (anticlines) and valleys (synclines) over millions of years.

Demonstrate with a simple tablecloth or layered cloth model: push two ends together to show folding. Guide them to see that the folds create anticlines (peaks) and synclines (valleys). Conclude with the key principle: Fold mountains are the surface expression of horizontal compressional forces acting on thick sedimentary sequences at convergent continental margins.
