DEFINITION: Functionalism is a school of psychology founded by William James that studies the adaptive functions of consciousness and behaviour—how mental processes help organisms survive and adjust to their environment—viewing consciousness as a continuous, flowing "stream" rather than a static collection of elements.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine you're watching a cricket match on TV while simultaneously thinking about your upcoming exam, hearing your mother call from the kitchen, and feeling the texture of the sofa—all at once. A structuralist would ask you to break this into isolated sensations: "red color," "loud sound," "rough texture." But William James would say that's impossible—your consciousness doesn't come in frozen slices; it flows like a river. Start by asking: "Can you pause your thoughts and examine them like specimens in a jar?" The answer is no—the act of observing changes the flow. This leads to James's radical insight: psychology must study the *function* of this flowing stream in helping us navigate life, not its static structure.

DEFINITION: Memory as a constructive process refers to the understanding that memory is not a passive, faithful recording of events that is later replayed intact, but an active, reconstructive process in which the brain uses schemas — mental frameworks organized by prior knowledge and expectations — to encode, store, and retrieve information. During retrieval, the mind does not simply "play back" a stored trace; it *reconstructs* the memory by combining fragmentary stored information with schema-based inferences, filling gaps with what "must have been there" based on what the schema predicts. This reconstructive nature makes memory inherently prone to distortion: we remember not what actually happened but what our schemas lead us to believe happened. Frederic Bartlett's "War of the Ghosts" study (1932) demonstrated this by showing that participants recalling a Native American story systematically distorted it to fit their own cultural schemas, and modern research on false memories and eyewitness testimony has confirmed that reconstruction errors are a fundamental — not exceptional — feature of human memory.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine you are telling a friend about a movie you saw last month. You don't replay the movie frame by frame in your head — you reconstruct the plot from fragments: "There was a scene where the hero... and then I think the villain... and the ending was something about..." You fill the gaps with what *makes sense* based on your knowledge of how movies typically work. That is reconstructive memory: you are not retrieving a recording but *rebuilding* the experience from fragments and schemas. Now consider Bartlett's famous experiment: he gave English participants a Native American folktale called "War of the Ghosts" and asked them to recall it repeatedly over days and weeks. The participants did not reproduce the story faithfully — they *transformed* it. They dropped details that didn't fit their cultural schemas (e.g., the supernatural elements), simplified complex features (leveling), exaggerated striking details (sharpening), and changed unfamiliar elements to fit familiar English cultural expectations (assimilation). The key insight: memory is not a camera that records and replays — it is a *builder* that constructs and reconstructs using schemas as blueprints. DEFINITION: Mnemonics are systematic memory enhancement strategies that impose meaningful organisation or vivid imagery onto to-be-remembered information, thereby creating effective retrieval cues and facilitating deep semantic encoding. They function by transforming arbitrary or low-meaningfulness material into structured, personally relevant representations that leverage the brain's natural preference for meaningful, organised, and imageable information.
