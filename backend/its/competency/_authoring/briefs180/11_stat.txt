DEFINITION: Measures of dispersion quantify the extent to which individual data points in a distribution deviate from a central value (mean, median, or mode), describing the spread, variability, or scatter of the dataset around its average.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine two Class 11 sections, Section A and Section B, both have an average (mean) score of 75 in Statistics. Section A has scores clustered tightly: 73, 74, 75, 76, 77. A teacher planning remedial classes needs to know the spread, not just the average. This is where measures of dispersion enter — they answer "how far are the values from the centre?" We begin by recognising that central tendency gives a single representative value, but dispersion reveals the reliability of that representative value. The key insight: two distributions with the same mean can have completely different structures, and dispersion measures capture this difference.

DEFINITION: Fisher's Ideal Index Number is a weighted price index calculated as the geometric mean of Laspeyres' and Paasche's price indices, expressed as \( P_{01} = \sqrt{P_L \times P_P} \times 100 \), where \( P_L = \frac{\sum p_1 q_0}{\sum p_0 q_0} \) uses base-year quantities as weights and \( P_P = \frac{\sum p_1 q_1}{\sum p_0 q_1} \) uses current-year quantities as weights. It is called "ideal" because it satisfies both the Time Reversal Test (\( P_{01} \times P_{10} = 1 \)) and the Factor Reversal Test (\( P_{01} \times Q_{01} = \frac{\sum p_1 q_1}{\sum p_0 q_0} \)), making it theoretically superior to Laspeyres and Paasche indices alone.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine you are a market analyst comparing rice prices between 2020 and 2024. You calculate Laspeyres index using 2020 quantities (giving 125) and Paasche index using 2024 quantities (giving 115). Which one is "correct"? Neither — Laspeyres overstates inflation by ignoring consumers switching to cheaper alternatives, while Paasche understates it by using current consumption patterns. Fisher's insight: take the geometric mean of both! Start by computing Laspeyres and Paasche separately, then multiply them and take the square root. This single number balances both perspectives and passes rigorous mathematical tests that neither index passes alone — that's why Fisher called it "ideal."

D1 A: Correct answer: Fisher's Ideal Price Index \( P_{01} = \sqrt{ \frac{\sum p_1 q_0}{\sum p_0 q_0} \times \frac{\sum p_1 q_1}{\sum p_0 q_1} } \times 100 \). Here \( p_0 \) = base year price, \( p_1 \) = current year price, \( q_0 \) = base year quantity, \( q_1 \) = current year quantity. The first fraction is Laspeyres index, the second is Paasche index.
