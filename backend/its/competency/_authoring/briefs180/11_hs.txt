DEFINITION: The nutrition-infection cycle is a bidirectional, self-reinforcing mechanism where poor nutritional status impairs immune function and increases susceptibility to infection, while infection increases nutrient losses, raises metabolic demands, and reduces intake, further worsening nutritional status. The four factors of nutritional well-being—Food and Nutrient Security, Care for the Vulnerable, Health for All, and Safe Environment—are the essential pillars identified by WHO that must be simultaneously addressed to break this cycle and achieve optimal nutritional status.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine a 3-year-old child in a rural Indian household who has had diarrhoea four times in the last six months. Each episode lasts 3-4 days. The mother says "he doesn't eat when sick" and "he seems to get thinner after every illness." Why does this child keep falling sick, and why does food alone not fix it? Begin by mapping the child's trajectory: infection → reduced intake + nutrient loss + increased needs → weight loss → weakened immunity → next infection. Then introduce the four-factor lens: Is food available? (Food Security). Is someone feeding him patiently during illness? (Care). Can he access ORS/antibiotics? (Health). Is the water clean? (Safe Environment). The key insight: the cycle is not a straight line—it is a spiral where each factor influences the others, and breaking it requires simultaneous action on all four pillars.

DEFINITION: Environmental hygiene refers to the comprehensive set of practices and conditions in the external surroundings—including safe water supply, sanitary disposal of human excreta and liquid/solid wastes, vector control, housing quality, and food sanitation—that prevent disease transmission and promote community health. Food hygiene specifically encompasses all measures from procurement to consumption (storage, preparation, cooking, serving) that prevent contamination by pathogenic micro-organisms, toxins, or physical hazards, ensuring food remains safe and wholesome.

You notice a toddler with recurrent diarrhoea. Start by mapping each observation to the environmental hygiene components from Figure 4 in your textbook: water storage → water supply safety; open ditch → liquid waste disposal and vector control; cutting board practice → cross-contamination in food hygiene. Then trace how each gap creates a transmission pathway for pathogens (faecal-oral, vector-borne, direct contact). Finally, prioritise interventions using the "safe environment" factor from the nutritional well-being framework—what can the family do immediately (cover water drums, wash board with soap, pour bleach in ditch) versus what needs community action (drainage system, regular water supply). The key insight: environmental hygiene breaks the extrinsic factor chain in Table 2, stopping pathogens before they reach the human host.
