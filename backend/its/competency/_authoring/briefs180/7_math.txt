Here, we extend that exact same left-to-right digit comparison past the decimal point into the tenths, hundredths, and thousandths places. The key insight is that placeholder zeros can be added at the end of a decimal (like making 5.4 into 5.40) to make both numbers have the same number of decimal places, making comparison effortless.

DEFINITION: Constructing triangles using a ruler and compass involves accurately drawing a triangle when specific dimensions (such as side lengths or angle measures) are provided, ensuring that the geometric properties of triangles are strictly satisfied. This process relies on basic geometric tools to measure line segments and draw arcs representing fixed distances from vertices.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine you are a builder designing a triangular roof truss for a community center in your village, and the blueprint specifies exact side lengths in metres to ensure the structure does not collapse. How do you translate these measurements from paper to actual building materials with absolute precision? We begin by drawing a clean baseline using a straightedge ruler to represent one of the known sides of the triangle. Next, we recall our prior knowledge of circles and arcs from geometry: every point on a compass arc is at an exact fixed distance from the center. By opening our compass to the length of the second side, we swing an arc from one endpoint of our baseline, and by opening it to the third side, we swing an intersecting arc from the other endpoint. The key insight is that the exact intersection point of these two arcs forms the third vertex of our triangle, guaranteeing that all side lengths are exact.
D1 Q: What tool is used alongside an ungraduated ruler to draw arcs of precise radii when constructing triangles?
D1 A: The correct answer is a compass. A student who answers "protractor" is confusing tools used for measuring angles with the tool used for measuring fixed distances and drawing circular arcs.
D2 Q: Explain why you cannot construct a triangle with side lengths 3 cm, 4 cm, and 8 cm using a ruler and compass.
D2 A: The correct explanation is that the sum of the two shorter sides (3 cm + 4 cm = 7 cm) is less than the third side (8 cm), which violates the Triangle Inequality Theorem, making arc intersection impossible. A student who says "the lines are too short" fails to understand the mathematical condition required for triangle closure.

D3 A: The correct answer is either 5 cm or 6 cm. Working steps: 1. Draw baseline AB = 7 cm using a ruler. 2. Open compass to 5 cm, place needle at A, and draw an arc above AB. 3. Open compass to 6 cm, place needle at B, and draw an arc to intersect the first arc at point C. 4. Connect A to C and B to C. A student who picks 7 cm has confused the base length with the radius used for drawing arcs.
D4 Q: If you accidentally use a graduated ruler instead of a compass to measure and place the third vertex by guessing the intersection point, what geometric error does this introduce?
D4 A: The correct answer is that guessing or using a ruler directly as a measuring caliper introduces inaccuracy because a ruler's edge cannot maintain a fixed radial distance from two separate points simultaneously, leading to a failure in satisfying the exact side-length constraints. A student who thinks it is acceptable believes construction is merely sketching rather than precise geometric mapping.

A student who defends the ruler method does not understand why compasses are essential for geometric loci.
