DEFINITION: The equation of trajectory of a projectile is the mathematical relationship between the vertical displacement (y) and horizontal displacement (x) of an object projected with initial velocity \(v_0\) at an angle \(\theta_0\) to the horizontal, under constant gravitational acceleration \(g\) and negligible air resistance. It is given by \(y = x \tan\theta_0 - \frac{g x^2}{2 v_0^2 \cos^2\theta_0}\), which describes a parabolic path in the vertical plane.
WALKTHROUGH: Watch a cricket ball hit for a six — it follows a curved path. That curve is not circular; it is a parabola. We already know the horizontal motion is uniform (\(x = v_0 \cos\theta_0 \, t\)) and vertical motion is uniformly accelerated (\(y = v_0 \sin\theta_0 \, t - \frac{1}{2}gt^2\)). To find the *shape* of the path, we need a direct relation between \(y\) and \(x\) without time. Solve the first equation for \(t = \frac{x}{v_0 \cos\theta_0}\) and substitute into the second. The result is a quadratic in \(x\), confirming the parabolic trajectory. This equation is the "fingerprint" of the projectile — it tells us the height at any horizontal distance.

DEFINITION: The law of conservation of momentum states that for an isolated system (no net external force), the total vector momentum remains constant in time: \(\sum \vec{p}_{\text{initial}} = \sum \vec{p}_{\text{final}}\). Momentum \(\vec{p} = m\vec{v}\) is a vector quantity measured in kg·m/s. The law follows directly from Newton's second and third laws and holds for all collisions and explosions in isolated systems, regardless of whether kinetic energy is conserved.
WALKTHROUGH: Watch a cricket bowler deliver a ball: the ball gains forward momentum while the bowler experiences a tiny backward recoil. Now imagine the same interaction on a frictionless icy lake — the total momentum of bowler+ball system before release (zero) equals total momentum after. Begin by identifying the system boundaries: are external forces (friction, gravity component along motion) negligible during the brief interaction? If yes, apply \(\sum \vec{p}_i = \sum \vec{p}_f\) as a vector equation. Break into x and y components. For 1D problems, choose a sign convention. Always verify isolation assumption before writing the conservation equation.

DEFINITION: Kinetic energy is the energy possessed by a body due to its motion, defined as the work needed to accelerate a body of mass \( m \) from rest to velocity \( \vec{v} \). For a particle of mass \( m \) moving with speed \( v \), its kinetic energy \( K \) is given by \( K = \frac{1}{2} m v^2 \). It is a scalar quantity, always non-negative, with SI unit joule (J) and dimensions \([ML^2T^{-2}]\).
WALKTHROUGH: Observe a cricket ball and a tennis ball thrown at the same speed — the cricket ball hurts more because it has more mass. The quantity \( \frac{1}{2}mv^2 \) is kinetic energy, and the change in it equals the net work done. Start by measuring mass (kg) and speed (m/s), then apply \( K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 \).

DEFINITION: Kepler's laws of planetary motion are three empirical principles describing the motion of planets around the Sun: (1) Law of Orbits – Every planet moves in an elliptical orbit with the Sun at one focus; (2) Law of Areas – The radius vector from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal time intervals, a consequence of angular momentum conservation under a central force; (3) Law of Periods – The square of the orbital period \(T\) is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis \(a\) of the ellipse, i.e., \(T^2 \propto a^3\). For circular orbits of radius \(r\) around a central mass \(M\), the precise relation is \(T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM}r^3\), where \(G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \, \text{N·m}^2\text{/kg}^2\) is the universal gravitational constant.
WALKTHROUGH: Begin by asking students to observe the night sky over several weeks (or use a free planetarium app like Stellarium) and note that planets like Mars or Jupiter appear to move against the background stars, sometimes even reversing direction (retrograde motion). This historical puzzle – which troubled astronomers for millennia – was resolved by Tycho Brahe's precise naked-eye data and Kepler's analysis. Connect to their prior knowledge of uniform circular motion (Class 9, node c9_physics_ch2_10) and central forces (Class 9, node c9_physics_ch3_4): the Sun's pull is always directed toward the Sun, making it a central force. Then introduce the ellipse using the string-and-pins construction (NCERT Fig. 7.1b) to define semi-major axis \(a\), perihelion, and aphelion. Derive the second law from angular momentum conservation (\(L = mvr_\perp = \text{constant}\)) for a central force, showing that \(\Delta A/\Delta t = L/(2m)\). Finally, present the third law as a proportionality \(T^2 \propto a^3\) and give the exact formula for circular orbits, emphasizing that the constant \(\frac{4\pi^2}{GM}\) depends only on the central mass \(M\).

DEFINITION: A thermodynamic process is reversible if it can be reversed such that both the system and the surroundings return to their original states with no net change anywhere else in the universe; it is irreversible if it cannot be reversed without leaving a net change in the universe. Reversibility requires the process to be quasi-static (infinitesimally slow, maintaining equilibrium at every stage) and free from dissipative effects like friction, viscosity, or unrestrained expansion.
WALKTHROUGH: Observe a gas leaking from a punctured LPG cylinder in a kitchen – the gas spreads irreversibly throughout the room and never spontaneously returns to the cylinder. Ask: "What would need to happen for this to reverse?" Guide students to see that reversal would require external work (a compressor) and would heat the surroundings, leaving a net change. Contrast with an idealised frictionless piston compressing gas infinitely slowly: at each step, an infinitesimal pressure difference drives the change, and the process can be retraced exactly. Connect to prior nodes: thermal equilibrium (c11_phy_cbse_11_002) defines the quasi-static condition; First Law (c11_phy_cbse_11_003) shows energy conservation; Second Law (c11_phy_cbse_11_009) forbids complete heat-to-work conversion, making reversibility the theoretical limit for maximum efficiency.

DEFINITION: Periodic motion is a motion that repeats itself identically after a fixed interval of time called the period (T). Oscillatory motion is a special type of periodic motion where a particle moves to and fro about a stable equilibrium position under the action of a restoring force. The frequency (ν) is the reciprocal of the period (ν = 1/T) measured in hertz (Hz). Displacement in oscillations refers to the change with time of any physical quantity (position, angle, voltage, pressure) from its equilibrium value and can be represented by a sinusoidal function of time.
WALKTHROUGH: Observe a ceiling fan rotating at constant speed — its motion repeats but the blade does not move to and fro about a fixed point; it is periodic but not oscillatory. Use a stopwatch to measure the time for 10 complete to-and-fro motions of the swing; divide by 10 to get the period T. Recall from Class 7 (c7_physics_ch04_002) that the pendulum's period depends on length. The number of oscillations per second is the frequency ν = 1/T. Generalize: the voltage across a capacitor in an AC circuit also oscillates — its displacement is voltage, not position.
