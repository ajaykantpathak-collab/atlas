DEFINITION: A solution is a homogeneous mixture of two or more chemically non-reacting substances whose composition can be varied within certain limits. Based on the physical states of solute and solvent, binary solutions are classified into nine types: gaseous solutions (gas in gas, liquid in gas, solid in gas), liquid solutions (gas in liquid, liquid in liquid, solid in liquid), and solid solutions (gas in solid, liquid in solid, solid in solid). The component present in larger amount is the solvent and determines the physical state of the solution; the other component(s) is the solute.
WALKTHROUGH: Look around your kitchen: air (gas in gas), soda water (gas in liquid), brass utensil (solid in solid), dental amalgam (liquid in solid). All are solutions but in different physical states. Recall from Class 9 (c9_chem_ch1_1) that mixtures can be homogeneous (solutions) or heterogeneous. Now we categorise homogeneous mixtures by the states of solute and solvent. Start by identifying the solvent — the component in excess that sets the solution's state. Then place the solute-solvent pair into the 3×3 grid (gas/liquid/solid × gas/liquid/solid). Notice that solid solutions like alloys are technologically crucial (steel, brass), while gas-in-liquid solutions like oxygen in water sustain aquatic life. This classification underpins all subsequent quantitative work.
D1 Q: Name the type of solution formed when oxygen gas dissolves in water. Identify the solute and solvent.
D1 A: Correct answer: Liquid solution — gas in liquid. Solute: oxygen (gas); Solvent: water (liquid). A wrong answer (e.g., "gas in gas" or swapping solute/solvent) reveals the student cannot map a real example to the 3×3 classification grid and confuses which component determines the physical state.
D2 Q: Why is a mixture of hydrogen gas in palladium metal classified as a solid solution and not a liquid solution? Explain using the definition of solvent.
D2 A: Correct answer: Palladium is the solvent (present in larger amount) and is a solid, so the solution's physical state is solid. Hydrogen is the solute (gas). A wrong answer (e.g., "because hydrogen is a gas") shows the student thinks the solute's state decides the solution type, violating the definition that solvent determines the physical state.
D3 Q: A dentist uses an amalgam of mercury (liquid) and silver (solid) for fillings. Classify this solution, identify solute and solvent, and state the physical state of the resulting solution.
D3 A: Correct answer: Solid solution — liquid in solid. Solute: mercury (liquid); Solvent: silver (solid); Physical state: solid. Steps: (1) Recognise amalgam is a solid solution. (2) Mercury is the minor component (solute), silver is the major component (solvent). (3) Solvent is solid → solution is solid. A wrong classification (e.g., "liquid solution") indicates the student cannot apply the solvent-dominance rule to a familiar real-world example.
D4 Q: Compare the three gaseous solutions in Table 1.1 (gas in gas, liquid in gas, solid in gas). What common feature allows all three to be homogeneous mixtures despite different solute states?
D4 A: Correct answer: In all three, the solvent is a gas. Gas molecules have large inter-particle spaces and weak intermolecular forces (recall Class 11 kinetic theory, c11_chem_cbse_05_009), allowing solute particles (gas, liquid, or solid) to disperse uniformly at molecular level. A wrong answer focusing only on "all are gases" misses the key role of the gaseous solvent's properties in achieving homogeneity.
D5 Q: A student claims: "Brass (copper + zinc) is a liquid solution because both metals melt at high temperatures." Evaluate this statement and correct the error.
D5 A: Correct evaluation: False. Brass is a solid solution (solid in solid) at room temperature. The physical state of a solution is determined by the state of the solvent at the given temperature, not by melting points. Copper is the solvent (major component), zinc is the solute; both are solids at room temperature → solid solution. The error reveals confusion between the solution's state at a specific temperature and the components' melting behaviour.

DEFINITION: Solubility is the maximum amount of solute (in grams or moles) that can dissolve in a specified amount of solvent (typically 100 g or 1 kg) at a given temperature and pressure to form a saturated solution under dynamic equilibrium between dissolution and crystallisation. For solids in liquids, solubility depends primarily on temperature and the chemical nature of solute-solvent (like dissolves like); for gases in liquids, it is governed by Henry's law (p = KH x) where solubility is directly proportional to the partial pressure of the gas above the solution.
WALKTHROUGH: Begin with a kitchen observation: sugar dissolves rapidly in hot tea but barely in ice-cold water, while a soda bottle fizzes violently when opened warm but stays calm when chilled. Ask students why temperature helps solids but hurts gases. Connect to Class 9 (c9_chem_ch1_3) where they learned "like dissolves like" — now deepen it: polar solutes (NaCl) need polar solvents (water) for ion-dipole interactions; non-polar solutes (naphthalene) need non-polar solvents (benzene) for London forces. Introduce dynamic equilibrium: dissolution ⇌ crystallisation. For solids, use Le Chatelier's principle — if dissolution is endothermic (most solids), heat is a reactant, so raising temperature shifts equilibrium right → higher solubility. For gases, dissolution is exothermic (gas molecules lose kinetic energy entering liquid), so raising temperature shifts equilibrium left → lower solubility. Pressure affects gases dramatically (Henry's law) because compressing gas increases concentration at interface; solids/liquids are incompressible. Work through Henry's law calculations (Example 1.4 from NCERT). End with applications: CO2 in soft drinks (high pressure sealing), scuba divers' bends (N2 bubbles on depressurisation), anoxia at high altitude (low pO2).

D3 Q: At 298 K, Henry's law constant (KH) for O2 in water is 34.86 kbar. Then calculate the molar concentration of dissolved O2 assuming 1 L of water ≈ 55.5 mol.
D3 A: Step 1: Henry's law: p = KH × x → x = p/KH = 0.21 bar / 34,860 bar = 6.02 × 10⁻⁶.

DEFINITION: Osmotic pressure (Π) is the minimum excess pressure that must be applied to a solution to prevent the net flow of solvent molecules through a semipermeable membrane from the pure solvent (or dilute solution) into the solution. It is a colligative property directly proportional to the molar concentration of solute particles for dilute solutions, expressed as Π = iCRT where i is the van't Hoff factor, C is molarity (mol L⁻¹), R is the gas constant, and T is absolute temperature.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine placing a raw mango in concentrated brine (salt water) — it shrivels as water leaves its cells. Now place wilted lettuce in fresh water — it becomes crisp as water enters. Both phenomena are osmosis: solvent (water) moves through semipermeable cell membranes from lower solute concentration to higher solute concentration. In Class 11 (c11_chem_cbse_05_006), you learned PV = nRT for gases. Here we make a powerful analogy: solute particles in dilute solution behave like gas molecules occupying the solution volume, exerting a "pressure" — osmotic pressure. Start by visualising the thistle funnel experiment (Fig 1.9 NCERT): semipermeable membrane tied at the mouth, filled with solution, inverted in pure solvent. The liquid column rises until the hydrostatic pressure (ρgh) balances the osmotic influx. That equilibrium height gives Π. Connect this to the ideal gas law: n₂/V = C (molarity), so ΠV = n₂RT → Π = CRT. This analogy is the key to solving every osmotic pressure problem in boards and JEE/NEET.
D1 Q: State the formula for osmotic pressure of a dilute solution and define each term with its SI unit. Why is molarity (not molality) used in this formula?
D1 A: Π = iCRT where Π = osmotic pressure (Pa), i = van't Hoff factor (dimensionless), C = molar concentration (mol m⁻³ or mol L⁻¹ with R adjusted), R = gas constant (8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ or 0.083 L bar mol⁻¹ K⁻¹), T = absolute temperature (K). Molarity is used because osmotic pressure depends on number of solute particles per unit volume of solution (like gas pressure depends on molecules per unit volume), and volume of solution appears naturally in the derivation

DEFINITION: IUPAC nomenclature of coordination compounds provides a systematic set of rules to name mononuclear coordination entities by identifying the central metal, its oxidation state (Roman numeral), the ligands (listed alphabetically with appropriate prefixes), and the counter ions, ensuring an unambiguous name-to-structure correspondence.
WALKTHROUGH: Observe a blister pack of the anticancer drug cisplatin — its generic name is *cis-diamminedichloridoplatinum(II)*, yet the trans isomer *transplatin* is inactive. This real-world consequence of precise naming motivates the need for rules. Begin by identifying the coordination sphere (inside square brackets) and counter ions (outside). List ligands alphabetically ignoring prefixes (di, bis), assign oxidation state to the metal using ligand charges, then assemble: cation name first (ligands + metal + oxidation state), then anion name. Connect to Werner's theory (c12_chem_cbse_05_001) where you learned to distinguish coordination sphere from counter ions. The key pattern: ligands alphabetically → metal with oxidation state → counter ion.
D1 Q: State the exact IUPAC rule for the order in which ligands are listed in the name of a coordination compound, and give the correct ligand name for NH₃ and H₂O.
D1 A: Ligands are listed in alphabetical order of their names (ignoring multiplicative prefixes like di-, tri-, bis-, tris-). The ligand name for NH₃ is ammine (not ammonia) and for H₂O is aqua (not water). A wrong answer (e.g., "alphabetical order of formulas" or "ammonia/water") reveals the student is confusing chemical formula ordering with name ordering, or using common names instead of IUPAC ligand names — both cause systematic naming errors in board exams.
