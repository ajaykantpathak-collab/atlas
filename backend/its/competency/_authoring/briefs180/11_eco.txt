DEFINITION: The Green Revolution's regional spread refers to the uneven geographical diffusion of High Yielding Variety (HYV) seed technology, irrigation, and chemical inputs across India, concentrated initially in Punjab, Haryana, Western Uttar Pradesh, and parts of Andhra Pradesh and Tamil Nadu during 1966-1985. Equity concerns arise from the resulting disparities in agricultural productivity, income, and access to subsidised inputs between large and small farmers, and between irrigated northwestern regions and rainfed eastern/southern regions, despite government interventions like subsidised credit and fertiliser.
WALKTHROUGH: Imagine a farmer in Punjab in 1970 with a tubewell, buying HYV wheat seeds and fertiliser on subsidised credit, harvesting 5 tonnes/hectare, while a farmer in Bihar with the same land size relies on monsoon rain, uses traditional seeds, and harvests 1 tonne/hectare. First, observe the input package: HYV seeds require assured water, fertiliser, and pesticide — a "package" that only irrigated regions could adopt. Next, connect to the prior node (c11_eco_cbse_02_005) where you learned HYV seeds increased wheat yields. Now ask: why did this package not reach eastern India? Analyse the role of infrastructure (irrigation, credit, roads) and land reforms (tenancy security) in enabling adoption. Finally, evaluate whether subsidies on fertiliser and credit actually reached small farmers or leaked to large farmers and the fertiliser industry, using the concept of marketed surplus from the previous node.
D1 Q: What were the two main phases of the Green Revolution in India, and which crops and regions characterised each phase?
D1 A: Phase 1 (mid-1960s to mid-1970s): Wheat in Punjab, Haryana, Western UP. Phase 2 (mid-1970s to mid-1980s): Rice in Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, and spread to more states. A wrong answer (e.g., saying rice was in Phase 1 or that all states benefited equally) reveals the student has not grasped the sequential, crop-specific, and region-specific nature of the diffusion.
