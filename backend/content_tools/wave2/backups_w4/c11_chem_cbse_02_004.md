## ELI5
A pencil's graphite carries its badge: 6 protons - that count is the atomic number Z, which uniquely identifies the element and equals the electrons in a neutral atom. Pile on neutrons and Z holds while the mass number A = Z + n climbs: carbon-12, carbon-13, carbon-14 written as superscript-subscript pairs on the board. Ions reshuffle the electron count, never the proton count; isotopes share Z with different A, isobars share A with different Z.

## DEFINITION
Atomic number (Z) is the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom, which uniquely identifies an element and equals the number of electrons in a neutral atom. Mass number (A) is the total number of protons and neutrons (nucleons) in the nucleus, represented as A = Z + n where n is the neutron number. The standard notation for an atom is \(^{A}_{Z}\text{X}\), where X is the element symbol.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can correctly calculate the number of protons, neutrons, and electrons for any given neutral atom or ion using its atomic number (Z) and mass number (A), write the proper \(^{A}_{Z}\text{X}\) notation including charge for ions, and distinguish between isotopes (same Z, different A) and isobars (same A, different Z) with justification.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the precise definitions of atomic number and mass number, the \(^{A}_{Z}\text{X}\) notation, calculation of subatomic particle counts for neutral atoms and ions, and the conceptual distinction between isotopes and isobars. It does NOT cover quantum numbers, orbital shapes, electronic configuration rules, or spectral series — those are addressed in subsequent nodes (c11_chem_cbse_02_013 onwards).

## WALKTHROUGH
Begin by showing a periodic table and asking: "Why is hydrogen placed at position 1 and helium at 2?" Guide students to realise the ordering reflects the number of protons in the nucleus — a concept established by Rutherford's nuclear model (c11_chem_cbse_02_003). Next, hold up a sample of carbon (like graphite from a pencil) and explain that while all carbon atoms have 6 protons, some have 6 neutrons and others 7 or 8 — introducing mass number variation. Connect this to the notation \(^{12}_{6}\text{C}\), \(^{13}_{6}\text{C}\), \(^{14}_{6}\text{C}\) written on the board. Finally, derive the relationship A = Z + n and practice calculating particles for ions like \(^{35}_{17}\text{Cl}^-\) and \(^{23}_{11}\text{Na}^+\), emphasising that electron count changes with charge but proton count never does.

Takeaway: mastery means reading any nucleus notation into a part count - protons from Z, neutrons from A - Z, electrons from Z minus the charge - and writing the A-over-Z symbol with the ion's charge attached. Exam moves: count-the-particles questions and isotope-versus-isobar items. Check yourself: the pencil's graphite is the anchor - the same 6 protons always, neutron counts free to vary. Students miss it because they let charge move protons, when the definition is absolute: the proton count is the element's identity and never changes, while electrons come and go with charge and neutrons with isotope.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Define atomic number (Z) and mass number (A) using the standard notation \(^{A}_{Z}\text{X}\). State the relationship between Z, A, and the number of neutrons (n).

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Atomic number (Z) = number of protons in the nucleus = number of electrons in a neutral atom. Mass number (A) = total number of nucleons = number of protons + number of neutrons. Relationship: A = Z + n, so n = A – Z. In notation \(^{A}_{Z}\text{X}\), A is the left superscript, Z is the left subscript. A wrong answer (e.g., swapping Z and A, or saying Z = neutrons) reveals the student confuses the two definitions or misremembers the notation layout — a critical error for writing nuclear symbols in board exams.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
A neutral atom has 17 protons and 18 neutrons. Write its complete symbol \(^{A}_{Z}\text{X}\) and name the element. Then write the symbol for the ion formed when this atom gains one electron.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Protons = 17 → Z = 17 (chlorine, Cl). Neutrons = 18 → A = 17 + 18 = 35. Neutral atom: \(^{35}_{17}\text{Cl}\). After gaining one electron, it has 18 electrons and 17 protons → charge = –1. Ion symbol: \(^{35}_{17}\text{Cl}^-\). A wrong answer (e.g., writing \(^{17}_{35}\text{Cl}\) or \(^{35}_{17}\text{Cl}^+\)) reveals the student either reverses the superscript/subscript positions or misunderstands that gaining electrons creates a negative charge — both are common board-exam mistakes.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Calculate the number of protons, neutrons, and electrons in \(^{80}_{35}\text{Br}^-\). Show all steps.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Identify Z = 35 (subscript) → protons = 35. Step 2: Identify A = 80 (superscript) → mass number = 80. Step 3: Neutrons = A – Z = 80 – 35 = 45. Step 4: Charge is –1 → electrons = protons + 1 = 35 + 1 = 36. Final answer: protons = 35, neutrons = 45, electrons = 36. A wrong answer (e.g., electrons = 35 or neutrons = 35) reveals the student either ignores the ion charge when counting electrons or confuses Z with neutron number — indicating they haven't internalised that Z always equals protons, never neutrons.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Two species are given: \(^{14}_{6}\text{C}\) and \(^{14}_{7}\text{N}\). Are they isotopes, isobars, or neither? Justify your answer using the definitions of Z and A.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
They are isobars. \(^{14}_{6}\text{C}\) has Z = 6, A = 14. \(^{14}_{7}\text{N}\) has Z = 7, A = 14. Same mass number (A = 14) but different atomic numbers (Z = 6 vs 7) → isobars by definition. Isotopes would require same Z, different A. A wrong answer (e.g., calling them isotopes) reveals the student memorised "same mass number = isotopes" without understanding that isotopes share Z (same element) while isobars share A (different elements) — a conceptual confusion that undermines nuclear chemistry.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student writes the symbol for a magnesium ion with 12 protons, 12 neutrons, and 10 electrons as \(^{24}_{12}\text{Mg}^{2-}\). Evaluate this symbol and correct it if necessary.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The symbol is incorrect. Protons = 12 → Z = 12 (Mg). Neutrons = 12 → A = 12 + 12 = 24. Electrons = 10, protons = 12 → net charge = +2 (cation), not –2. Correct symbol: \(^{24}_{12}\text{Mg}^{2+}\). The student's error shows they think "fewer electrons = negative charge" — a sign-reversal misconception. In board exams, such charge errors lose full marks even if A and Z are correct.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a set of three nuclear symbols \(^{A}_{Z}\text{X}\) that represent: (i) an isotope of carbon-12, (ii) an isobar of carbon-12, and (iii) a different element with the same neutron number as carbon-12. Explain your reasoning for each.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
(i) Isotope of \(^{12}_{6}\text{C}\): same Z = 6, different A. Example: \(^{13}_{6}\text{C}\) (7 neutrons) or \(^{14}_{6}\text{C}\) (8 neutrons). (ii) Isobar of \(^{12}_{6}\text{C}\): same A = 12, different Z. Example: \(^{12}_{5}\text{B}\) or \(^{12}_{7}\text{N}\). (iii) Same neutron number as \(^{12}_{6}\text{C}\) (which has n = 12 – 6 = 6): choose any element with n = 6. Example: \(^{13}_{7}\text{N}\) (Z=7, n=6) or \(^{14}_{8}\text{O}\) (Z=8, n=6). A poor answer (e.g., giving \(^{12}_{6}\text{C}\) itself for (i) or confusing the categories) reveals the student cannot independently manipulate Z, A, and n — they only recognise definitions passively.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students frequently write the nuclear symbol with the mass number (A) as subscript and atomic number (Z) as superscript (e.g., \(^{17}_{35}\text{Cl}\) instead of \(^{35}_{17}\text{Cl}\)). They also assume atomic number equals neutron number for neutral atoms, or that the charge on an ion is determined by the neutron count. In board exams, this leads to wrong particle counts and incorrect identification of isotopes/isobars.

## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Draw a large nucleus on the board with "p⁺" and "n⁰" labelled. Ask: "Which particle count never changes for a given element?" (Protons → Z). Step 2: Write \(^{A}_{Z}\text{X}\) vertically: "A on top (mass = heavy = top), Z at bottom (atomic = fundamental = bottom)". Step 3: Use a table: Species | Z | A | n = A–Z | e⁻ (neutral) | e⁻ (ion). Fill for \(^{23}_{11}\text{Na}\), \(^{23}_{11}\text{Na}^+\), \(^{35}_{17}\text{Cl}\), \(^{35}_{17}\text{Cl}^-\). Step 4: Have the student explain aloud why Na⁺ has 10 electrons but still Z=11. This visual + verbal routine fixes the notation and charge logic.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A sample of chlorine gas contains two isotopes: \(^{35}_{17}\text{Cl}\) (75.77% abundance) and \(^{37}_{17}\text{Cl}\) (24.23% abundance). Calculate the average atomic mass of chlorine. Verify that the atomic number remains 17 for both.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the data: Isotope 1: mass = 35 u, abundance = 75.77% = 0.7577. Isotope 2: mass = 37 u, abundance = 24.23% = 0.2423. Both have Z = 17.
- Step 2: Recall the formula for average atomic mass = Σ (isotopic mass × fractional abundance).
- Step 3: Compute contribution of each isotope: (35 × 0.7577) = 26.5195 u; (37 × 0.2423) = 8.9651 u.
- Step 4: Sum the contributions: 26.5195 + 8.9651 = 35.4846 u ≈ 35.5 u.
- Step 5: Verify atomic number: Both isotopes have 17 protons → Z = 17 for chlorine. Neutrons differ: 35–17=18 and 37–17=20.
- Step 6: Interpret: The non-integer average mass (35.5 u) reflects the natural mixture; Z=17 confirms same element.

REASONING: Average atomic mass is a weighted mean because isotopes exist in fixed natural ratios. The atomic number Z is invariant for a given element — it is the defining property. This calculation mirrors how the periodic table lists 35.5 for chlorine.

ANSWER: Average atomic mass = 35.5 u (approx). Atomic number Z = 17 for both isotopes.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: An ion has mass number 56, charge +3, and 30.4% more neutrons than electrons. Determine its complete symbol \(^{A}_{Z}\text{X}^{q+}\) and name the element.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Let number of electrons = e. Charge = +3 → protons = e + 3 = Z.
- Step 2: Neutrons = e + 30.4% of e = e + 0.304e = 1.304e.
- Step 3: Mass number A = protons + neutrons = (e + 3) + 1.304e = 2.304e + 3 = 56 (given).
- Step 4: Solve for e: 2.304e = 53 → e = 53 / 2.304 ≈ 23.00 → e = 23 (must be integer).
- Step 5: Protons = Z = e + 3 = 23 + 3 = 26. Element with Z=26 is Iron (Fe).
- Step 6: Neutrons = 1.304 × 23 = 30 (check: 26 + 30 = 56 = A ✓).
- Step 7: Write symbol: \(^{56}_{26}\text{Fe}^{3+}\).

REASONING: This problem combines the A = Z + n relationship with ion charge logic (protons – electrons = charge) and percentage reasoning. The integer constraint on particle counts validates the solution.

ANSWER: \(^{56}_{26}\text{Fe}^{3+}\) (Iron(III) ion)

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: In a mass spectrometry analysis of a pure element, three peaks are observed at mass numbers 24, 25, and 26 with relative abundances 78.99%, 10.00%, and 11.01% respectively. The atomic number is 12. (a) Write the nuclear symbols for the three isotopes. (b) Calculate the average atomic mass. (c) Explain why the chemical properties of all three isotopes are identical.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Given Z = 12 → element is Magnesium (Mg). A values: 24, 25, 26.
- Step 2: Write symbols: \(^{24}_{12}\text{Mg}\), \(^{25}_{12}\text{Mg}\), \(^{26}_{12}\text{Mg}\).
- Step 3: Neutron counts: n = A – Z → 12, 13, 14 respectively.
- Step 4: Average mass = (24 × 0.7899) + (25 × 0.1000) + (26 × 0.1101) = 18.9576 + 2.5000 + 2.8626 = 24.3202 u ≈ 24.3 u.
- Step 5: Chemical properties depend on electron configuration, which is determined by Z (protons = electrons in neutral atom). All three have Z=12 → same electron configuration → identical chemistry.
- Step 6: Physical properties (density, diffusion rate, mass spec behavior) differ due to different masses.

REASONING: This integrates isotope definition (same Z, different A), weighted average calculation, and the fundamental principle that chemical behaviour is governed by electrons (hence Z), not neutrons. It connects to the periodic table (Mg listed as 24.3 u) and explains why isotopes cannot be separated by chemical methods.

ANSWER: (a) \(^{24}_{12}\text{Mg}\), \(^{25}_{12}\text{Mg}\), \(^{26}_{12}\text{Mg}\). (b) 24.3 u. (c) Same Z = 12 → same electron configuration → identical chemical properties.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A forensic lab analyses a trace element found at a crime scene. The mass spectrometer shows a single peak at mass number 127 with charge +1. The element is known to have 74 neutrons in its most stable isotope. Identify the element, write its nuclear symbol for the detected ion, and explain why the mass spectrum shows only one peak despite the element having multiple isotopes in nature.

COMPETENCY: Interpreting mass spectrometry data using atomic number and mass number concepts

STEPS:
- Step 1: The detected ion has A = 127, charge = +1. Neutrons in stable isotope = 74.
- Step 2: For the stable isotope, n = 74. A = Z + n → Z = A – n. But we don't know A of stable isotope yet.
- Step 3: The detected ion is likely the stable isotope itself (single peak). So A = 127, n = 74 → Z = 127 – 74 = 53.
- Step 4: Element with Z = 53 is Iodine (I).
- Step 5: Neutral iodine has 53 electrons. Ion with +1 charge has 52 electrons. Symbol: \(^{127}_{53}\text{I}^+\).
- Step 6: Natural iodine is 100% \(^{127}_{53}\text{I}\) (monoisotopic). Other isotopes are radioactive with very short half-lives, so they don't appear in natural samples. Hence single peak.

ANSWER: Element: Iodine (I). Nuclear symbol of detected ion: \(^{127}_{53}\text{I}^+\). Single peak because iodine is effectively monoisotopic in nature (only \(^{127}\text{I}\) is stable and abundant).

DIFFICULTY: medium

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Two elements X and Y form ions X²⁺ and Y³⁻ respectively. The ions are isoelectronic with the noble gas argon (Z=18). The mass number of X is 40 and that of Y is 31. (a) Determine the atomic numbers of X and Y. (b) Write the nuclear symbols for the neutral atoms of X and Y. (c) Calculate the number of neutrons in each neutral atom. (d) Are the neutral atoms of X and Y isobars? Justify.

COMPETENCY: Multi-step analytical reasoning combining isoelectronic principle, ion charge, and isobar concept

STEPS:
- Step 1: Isoelectronic with Ar (Z=18) means both ions have 18 electrons.
- Step 2: X²⁺ has 18 electrons → neutral X has 18 + 2 = 20 electrons → Z_X = 20 (Calcium).
- Step 3: Y³⁻ has 18 electrons → neutral Y has 18 – 3 = 15 electrons → Z_Y = 15 (Phosphorus).
- Step 4: Neutral atoms: X = \(^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}\), Y = \(^{31}_{15}\text{P}\).
- Step 5: Neutrons in Ca: n = A – Z = 40 – 20 = 20. Neutrons in P: n = 31 – 15 = 16.
- Step 6: Isobars require same A. Ca has A=40, P has A=31 → different A → NOT isobars.

ANSWER: (a) Z_X = 20 (Ca), Z_Y = 15 (P). (b) \(^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}\), \(^{31}_{15}\text{P}\). (c) Ca: 20 neutrons, P: 16 neutrons. (d) No, they are not isobars because their mass numbers differ (40 ≠ 31).

DIFFICULTY: hard
