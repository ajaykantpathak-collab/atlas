## ELI5
Turn up the stove flame and the pressure cooker whistles sooner: rate answers to concentration, and the rate law is the exact bill. For a reaction, rate equals a constant times each reactant's concentration raised to its own power - powers that only experiments reveal, never the balanced equation's coefficients. Double a reactant and watch what happens: quadruple the rate and that reactant's order is two. The rate constant is the fixed price per degree of crowding, valid only at one temperature.

## DEFINITION
The rate law (or rate expression) for a chemical reaction expresses the reaction rate as a product of the rate constant \(k\) and the molar concentrations of reactants raised to powers (orders) that are determined experimentally: \(\text{Rate} = k[\text{A}]^x[\text{B}]^y\). The rate constant \(k\) is the proportionality factor that relates the rate to the concentration terms at a given temperature; its value is constant for a specific reaction at a fixed temperature but changes with temperature.

understand

## MASTERY_CRITERIA

It does NOT cover integrated rate equations, half-life calculations, temperature dependence (Arrhenius), or collision theory — those are in subsequent nodes (c12_chem_cbse_03_005–010).

## WALKTHROUGH
Observe how a pressure cooker whistles faster when you increase the flame — the rate of steam formation depends on temperature and the amount of water. In the lab, we measure how fast a reaction proceeds by tracking concentration changes. Start by recalling that the rate of reaction is defined as change in concentration per unit time (c12_chem_cbse_03_001). Then ask: "How does the rate change if I double the concentration of one reactant while keeping others constant?" This leads to the experimental method of initial rates. Show that for the reaction \(2\text{NO}(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \rightarrow 2\text{NO}_2(g)\), doubling \([\text{NO}]\) quadruples the rate, while doubling \([\text{O}_2]\) only doubles it — revealing the rate law \(\text{Rate} = k[\text{NO}]^2[\text{O}_2]\). Emphasize that the powers (2 and 1) came from experiment, not from the balanced equation. Finally, introduce \(k\) as the constant that makes the equation dimensionally consistent and temperature-dependent.

Takeaway first: the rate law is read off the laboratory, not the balanced equation - orders are measured exponents, and the constant their priced multiplier. Exam moves: initial-rate tables solved for orders, rate constants with units, and predictions of new rates when concentrations multiply. Method: rerun the cooker - flame raised, whistle quickened, then the lab's controlled doubling where one reactant's quadrupling outs the square. Check yourself: the flagged trap is copying stoichiometric coefficients into exponents; the nitrogen-monoxide example exists precisely to break that habit. Drill: solve one initial-rate table and state the units of its constant.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
For the reaction \(2\text{N}_2\text{O}_5(g) \rightarrow 4\text{NO}_2(g) + \text{O}_2(g)\), the rate law is found to be \(\text{Rate} = k[\text{N}_2\text{O}_5]\). What is the order of reaction with respect to \(\text{N}_2\text{O}_5\) and the overall order?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The order with respect to \(\text{N}_2\text{O}_5\) is 1 (first order) and the overall order is 1. A wrong answer (e.g., saying order is 2 because the stoichiometric coefficient is 2) reveals the misconception that rate law exponents come from the balanced equation rather than experiment.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
In the reaction \(\text{CHCl}_3 + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{CCl}_4 + \text{HCl}\), the experimental rate law is \(\text{Rate} = k[\text{CHCl}_3][\text{Cl}_2]^{1/2}\). Explain why the exponent for \(\text{Cl}_2\) is \(1/2\) and not 1 (its stoichiometric coefficient).

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The exponent \(1/2\) indicates a complex reaction mechanism where the rate-determining step involves the dissociation of \(\text{Cl}_2\) into chlorine atoms, so the rate depends on the square root of \(\text{Cl}_2\) concentration. A student who says "it should be 1 because the coefficient is 1" does not understand that rate law is experimentally determined and reflects mechanism, not stoichiometry.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
The following initial-rate data were obtained for the reaction \(2\text{A} + \text{B} \rightarrow \text{Products}\) at 300 K:

| Experiment | \([\text{A}]_0\) (mol L⁻¹) | \([\text{B}]_0\) (mol L⁻¹) | Initial Rate (mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹) |
|------------|---------------------------|---------------------------|----------------------------|
| 1          | 0.10                      | 0.10                      | \(2.0 \times 10^{-4}\)     |
| 2          | 0.20                      | 0.10                      | \(8.0 \times 10^{-4}\)     |
| 3          | 0.10                      | 0.20                      | \(4.0 \times 10^{-4}\)     |

Determine the rate law and calculate the rate constant \(k\) with proper units.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Comparing Experiments 1 and 2: \([\text{A}]\) doubles, \([\text{B}]\) constant, rate increases by factor 4 → order w.r.t. A = 2.  
Comparing Experiments 1 and 3: \([\text{B}]\) doubles, \([\text{A}]\) constant, rate doubles → order w.r.t. B = 1.  
Rate law: \(\text{Rate} = k[\text{A}]^2[\text{B}]\).  
Using Experiment 1: \(2.0 \times 10^{-4} = k(0.10)^2(0.10) = k \times 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\) → \(k = 0.20\ \text{L}^2\ \text{mol}^{-2}\ \text{s}^{-1}\).  
A student who gets orders wrong likely misreads the rate ratios; one who forgets units of \(k\) does not grasp dimensional consistency.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
A reaction \(\text{A} + 2\text{B} \rightarrow \text{C}\) has the rate law \(\text{Rate} = k[\text{A}][\text{B}]^2\). If the volume of the reaction vessel is suddenly halved (doubling all concentrations), by what factor does the initial rate change? Explain.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Halving volume doubles \([\text{A}]\) and \([\text{B}]\). New rate = \(k(2[\text{A}])(2[\text{B}])^2 = k \cdot 2[\text{A}] \cdot 4[\text{B}]^2 = 8 \times \text{original rate}\). The rate increases by a factor of 8. A student who says factor 2 or 4 is not applying the orders correctly to the concentration changes.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
The rate constant for a reaction is reported as \(k = 3.5 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}^{-1}\ \text{L}\ \text{s}^{-1}\). What is the overall order of the reaction? Could this reaction be elementary? Justify.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Units of \(k\) for overall order \(n\): \(\text{mol}^{1-n}\ \text{L}^{n-1}\ \text{s}^{-1}\). Given units \(\text{mol}^{-1}\ \text{L}\ \text{s}^{-1}\) match \(n=2\) (second order). The reaction could be elementary if it is bimolecular (e.g., \(\text{A} + \text{B} \rightarrow \text{products}\)) because molecularity = order for elementary steps. A student who says order is 1 or 3 cannot connect units to order; one who says "yes it must be elementary" confuses possibility with certainty — complex reactions can also have integer orders.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple experiment using the initial-rates method to determine the rate law for the reaction between sodium thiosulfate (\(\text{Na}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_3\)) and hydrochloric acid (\(\text{HCl}\)) which produces a sulfur precipitate. Outline the steps, what you would measure, and how you would analyze the data.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Steps: (1) Prepare several reaction mixtures with varying initial \([\text{Na}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_3]\) while keeping \([\text{HCl}]\) constant, and vice versa. (2) Start each reaction by mixing, and simultaneously start a timer. (3) Measure the time \(t\) for the precipitate to obscure a marked cross beneath the flask (a proxy for a fixed amount of product formed). (4) Initial rate \(\propto 1/t\). (5) Plot \(\log(\text{rate})\) vs \(\log[\text{reactant}]\); slope gives order. A poor answer omits controlling one reactant's concentration or uses only one trial per condition.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often write the rate law directly from the balanced equation, e.g., for \(2\text{N}_2\text{O}_5 \rightarrow 4\text{NO}_2 + \text{O}_2\) they write \(\text{Rate} = k[\text{N}_2\text{O}_5]^2\). This is wrong because the reaction is actually first order in \(\text{N}_2\text{O}_5\) (experimentally determined). The balanced equation only gives stoichiometry; the rate law reveals mechanism and must be found by experiment. Another error: thinking the rate constant \(k\) has the same units for all reactions — they forget that units of \(k\) depend on overall order.

## THIS_FORMULA
Rate = k [A]^x [B]^y  (Differential rate law);  -d[R]/dt = k [A]^x [B]^y

## FORMULA_VARIABLES
Rate = reaction rate (mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹); k = rate constant (units depend on overall order); [A], [B] = molar concentrations of reactants (mol L⁻¹); x, y = orders with respect to A and B (determined experimentally); overall order = x + y.

## FORMULA_UNITS
Rate: mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ (or atm s⁻¹ for gases). k for order n: mol^(1-n) L^(n-1) s⁻¹. Examples: n=0 → mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹; n=1 → s⁻¹; n=2 → L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: The reaction \(2\text{NO}(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \rightarrow 2\text{NO}_2(g)\) was studied at 298 K. The following initial-rate data were collected:

| Exp | [NO]₀ (mol L⁻¹) | [O₂]₀ (mol L⁻¹) | Initial Rate (mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹) |
|-----|----------------|----------------|----------------------------|
| 1   | 0.30           | 0.30           | 0.096                      |
| 2   | 0.60           | 0.30           | 0.384                      |
| 3   | 0.30           | 0.60           | 0.192                      |

Determine the rate law, the overall order, and the rate constant \(k\) with units.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Compare Experiments 1 and 2 where [O₂] is constant. [NO] doubles (0.30 → 0.60), rate increases from 0.096 to 0.384 → factor of 4. Since \(2^x = 4\), \(x = 2\). Order w.r.t. NO = 2.
- Step 2: Compare Experiments 1 and 3 where [NO] is constant. [O₂] doubles (0.30 → 0.60), rate increases from 0.096 to 0.192 → factor of 2. Since \(2^y = 2\), \(y = 1\). Order w.r.t. O₂ = 1.
- Step 3: Write the rate law: Rate = k [NO]² [O₂]. Overall order = 2 + 1 = 3 (third order).
- Step 4: Calculate k using Experiment 1: 0.096 = k (0.30)² (0.30) = k × 0.027 → k = 0.096 / 0.027 = 3.56 L² mol⁻² s⁻¹.
- Step 5: Verify with Experiment 2: Rate = 3.56 × (0.60)² × 0.30 = 3.56 × 0.108 = 0.384 ✓. Units: k = Rate / ([NO]²[O₂]) = (mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹) / (mol² L⁻² × mol L⁻¹) = L² mol⁻² s⁻¹.

REASONING: The method of initial rates isolates the effect of each reactant by holding others constant. The ratio of rates gives the order directly because Rate ∝ [reactant]^order. The rate constant k is then obtained by substitution and carries units that make the equation dimensionally consistent.

ANSWER: Rate law = k [NO]² [O₂]; overall order = 3; k = 3.56 L² mol⁻² s⁻¹.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: For the hydrolysis of ethyl acetate in aqueous solution: \(\text{CH}_3\text{COOC}_2\text{H}_5 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\text{H}^+} \text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}\), water is present in large excess (≈ 55.5 M). The experimentally observed rate law is Rate = k' [CH₃COOC₂H₅]. Explain why this reaction appears first order overall even though two reactants are involved, and calculate the true second-order rate constant k if k' = 1.2 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Recognize that water is the solvent and its concentration remains essentially constant during the reaction because it is in huge excess (55.5 M vs. ~0.01 M ester).
- Step 2: The true rate law is Rate = k [CH₃COOC₂H₅][H₂O]. Since [H₂O] ≈ constant, we can define a pseudo-first-order rate constant k' = k [H₂O].
- Step 3: The observed rate law Rate = k' [CH₃COOC₂H₅] is therefore pseudo-first-order. The overall order appears to be 1, but the true molecularity is 2 (bimolecular).
- Step 4: Calculate true k: k = k' / [H₂O] = (1.2 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹) / (55.5 mol L⁻¹) = 2.16 × 10⁻⁵ L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹.
- Step 5: Check units: k' has units s⁻¹ (first order). Dividing by concentration (mol L⁻¹) gives L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹, correct for a second-order rate constant.

REASONING: When one reactant is in large excess, its concentration change is negligible, so it gets absorbed into the rate constant. This simplifies kinetics to first order, but the true order is the sum of the exponents in the full rate law. This concept is called pseudo-first-order kinetics.

ANSWER: Pseudo-first-order because [H₂O] is constant; true k = 2.16 × 10⁻⁵ L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: The decomposition of \(\text{N}_2\text{O}_5\) in \(\text{CCl}_4\) solution at 318 K follows the reaction \(2\text{N}_2\text{O}_5 \rightarrow 4\text{NO}_2 + \text{O}_2\). The initial concentration of \(\text{N}_2\text{O}_5\) is 2.33 mol L⁻¹ and after 184 minutes it becomes 2.08 mol L⁻¹. Using the differential rate law \(\text{Rate} = -\frac{1}{2}\frac{d[\text{N}_2\text{O}_5]}{dt} = k[\text{N}_2\text{O}_5]\), calculate the average rate of reaction in mol L⁻¹ min⁻¹ and the rate constant k in min⁻¹. (Note: This is a first-order reaction.)

STEPS:
- Step 1: Write the relationship between reaction rate and rate of disappearance of \(\text{N}_2\text{O}_5\): Rate = \(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\Delta[\text{N}_2\text{O}_5]}{\Delta t}\) (stoichiometric coefficient 2).
- Step 2: Compute average rate of disappearance of \(\text{N}_2\text{O}_5\): \(\frac{\Delta[\text{N}_2\text{O}_5]}{\Delta t} = \frac{2.08 - 2.33}{184} = \frac{-0.25}{184} = -1.3587 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\ \text{min}^{-1}\).
- Step 3: Average reaction rate = \(-\frac{1}{2} \times (-1.3587 \times 10^{-3}) = 6.79 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\ \text{min}^{-1}\).
- Step 4: For a first-order reaction, the differential rate law is \(-\frac{d[\text{N}_2\text{O}_5]}{dt} = 2k[\text{N}_2\text{O}_5]\) (since Rate = k[N₂O₅] and Rate = \(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d[\text{N}_2\text{O}_5]}{dt}\)). So \(-\frac{d[\text{N}_2\text{O}_5]}{dt} = 2k[\text{N}_2\text{O}_5]\). But wait — the problem states the differential rate law as Rate = k[N₂O₅] with Rate defined as \(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d[\text{N}_2\text{O}_5]}{dt}\). So we use Rate = k[N₂O₅] directly.
- Step 5: Approximate the instantaneous rate at the midpoint concentration: [N₂O₅]_avg ≈ (2.33 + 2.08)/2 = 2.205 mol L⁻¹. Then k ≈ Rate / [N₂O₅]_avg = (6.79 × 10⁻⁴) / 2.205 = 3.08 × 10⁻⁴ min⁻¹. (Note: For exact k we would use integrated rate law, but this average-rate approximation is reasonable for small time intervals.)
- Step 6: Alternatively, use the integrated first-order equation (next node) for exact k: \(k = \frac{2.303}{t} \log \frac{[\text{N}_2\text{O}_5]_0}{[\text{N}_2\text{O}_5]_t} = \frac{2.303}{184} \log \frac{2.33}{2.08} = 0.0125 \times 0.049 = 6.1 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{min}^{-1}\). The difference shows why integrated forms are needed for exact k.

REASONING: The stoichiometric factor 2 in the rate definition is crucial. The average rate over a finite interval approximates the instantaneous rate at the average concentration. The exact rate constant requires integration because concentration changes continuously. This example bridges differential rate law (this node) and integrated rate law (next node).

ANSWER: Average rate = 6.79 × 10⁻⁴ mol L⁻¹ min⁻¹; approximate k ≈ 3.1 × 10⁻⁴ min⁻¹ (exact k = 6.1 × 10⁻⁴ min⁻¹ via integrated law).

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A student writes the rate law for the reaction \(2\text{NO}_2(g) + \text{F}_2(g) \rightarrow 2\text{NO}_2\text{F}(g)\) as \(\text{Rate} = k[\text{NO}_2]^2[\text{F}_2]\) because "the coefficients are 2 and 1". The teacher says this is incorrect unless the reaction is elementary. Design a set of two experiments (specify initial concentrations and what to measure) that would allow the student to test whether the rate law is indeed \(k[\text{NO}_2]^2[\text{F}_2]\) or something else. Explain how to analyze the results.

COMPETENCY: Designing experiments to determine rate laws (experimental design and data interpretation)

STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand that rate law must be determined experimentally; stoichiometry only gives a guess for elementary reactions.
- Step 2: Design Experiment Set 1: Vary [NO₂] while keeping [F₂] constant. Example: Exp A: [NO₂]=0.1 M, [F₂]=0.1 M; Exp B: [NO₂]=0.2 M, [F₂]=0.1 M. Measure initial rates.
- Step 3: Design Experiment Set 2: Vary [F₂] while keeping [NO₂] constant. Example: Exp C: [NO₂]=0.1 M, [F₂]=0.1 M; Exp D: [NO₂]=0.1 M, [F₂]=0.2 M. Measure initial rates.
- Step 4: Analyze: If rate quadruples when [NO₂] doubles (Set 1), order in NO₂ = 2. If rate doubles when [F₂] doubles (Set 2), order in F₂ = 1. Then rate law matches stoichiometry. If not, the reaction is complex.
- Step 5: Conclude that only experimental data can confirm the rate law.

ANSWER: Two sets of initial-rate experiments varying one reactant at a time. If orders match coefficients (2 and 1), the reaction could be elementary; if not, it is complex. The student's assumption is valid only for elementary reactions.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: The rate constant for a reaction is \(k = 4.5 \times 10^{-2}\ \text{L}\ \text{mol}^{-1}\ \text{s}^{-1}\) at 300 K. (a) What is the overall order of the reaction? (b) If the reaction is \(\text{A} + \text{B} \rightarrow \text{Products}\) and is first order in A, what is the order in B? (c) Calculate the initial rate when [A]₀ = 0.15 M and [B]₀ = 0.20 M. (d) If the volume of the reaction mixture is doubled (diluting both concentrations by half), what is the new initial rate? Express as a fraction of the original rate.

COMPETENCY: Quantitative application of rate law and rate constant (algebraic manipulation and proportional reasoning)

STEPS:
- Step 1: Units of k are L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹ → this matches second-order overall (n=2: units = mol^(1-2) L^(2-1) s⁻¹ = L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹). So overall order = 2.
- Step 2: Given order in A = 1, and overall order = 2 → order in B = 2 - 1 = 1.
- Step 3: Rate law: Rate = k [A][B]. Initial rate = (4.5 × 10⁻²) × 0.15 × 0.20 = 1.35 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹.
- Step 4: Diluting by factor 2: new [A] = 0.075 M, new [B] = 0.10 M. New rate = k (0.075)(0.10) = k × 0.0075. Original rate = k × 0.030. Ratio = 0.0075 / 0.030 = 1/4. New rate is 1/4 of original.

ANSWER: (a) Overall order = 2. (b) Order in B = 1. (c) Initial rate = 1.35 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹. (d) New rate = 1/4 of original rate.

DIFFICULTY: hard
