## ELI5
Two clubs with different membership rules: one fixes the exchange rate forever between two currencies until officials break the promise; the other lets the two currencies find their own price daily. Fixed regimes pin the rate with the central bank buying or selling unlimited foreign currency at the announced level; flexible regimes let demand and supply clear the market untouched. The difference is who absorbs a trade shock: under the peg the reserves bleed, under the float the price breathes.

## DEFINITION
**Flexible (floating) exchange rate:** A regime where the foreign exchange rate is determined solely by market forces of demand and supply for foreign currency, without central bank intervention to target a specific level. **Fixed (pegged) exchange rate:** A regime where the government/central bank announces and maintains the exchange rate at a specific level (or narrow band) by committing to buy/sell unlimited foreign currency at that price using official reserves. **Key distinction:** Under fixed rates, the central bank *must* intervene (changing reserves); under flexible rates, the exchange rate *itself* adjusts to clear the market (reserves unchanged).

## WALKTHROUGH
Start with a real Indian scenario: "In 1990, India had a fixed exchange rate (₹17.50/$). Our imports far exceeded exports, and foreign reserves fell to just $1.2 billion — barely 3 weeks of imports. The government *had* to devalue sharply in July 1991 (to ~₹25/$) as part of IMF-backed reforms." Ask: *Why did the fixed rate collapse? Why didn't the exchange rate adjust gradually earlier?* Connect to prior node `c12_eco_cbse_12_002` (determination of exchange rate): under flexible rates, the rupee would have depreciated *gradually* as the trade deficit widened, making exports cheaper and imports costlier *automatically*, reducing the deficit without a crisis. Under fixed rates, the RBI *had* to sell dollars from reserves to maintain ₹17.50/$, until reserves ran out. The key principle: **Flexible rates = price adjusts (exchange rate); Fixed rates = quantity adjusts (reserves).** This is the core analytical lens for this node.

Takeaway first: the adjustment channel is the whole distinction - price adjusts under flexible rates, quantity (reserves) adjusts under fixed - and every exam question in this node is that sentence wearing a costume. Exam moves: comparison questions on adjustment after a BoP shock, vulnerability questions on speculative attacks against thin reserves, and evaluation questions trading monetary autonomy against exchange-rate certainty. Method: replay the early-nineties peg - reserves draining to weeks of imports, the forced sharp devaluation - and ask what a float would have done gradually instead. Check yourself: devaluation is ordered, depreciation is suffered; the fixed regime's crisis arrives precisely because the price was forbidden to move early. Drill: state the adjustment channel for one BoP deficit under each regime in one line each.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Define "speculative attack" in the context of a fixed exchange rate regime. What specific condition makes a fixed exchange rate vulnerable to such an attack?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
A speculative attack occurs when market participants (investors, banks, public) anticipate that the central bank's foreign exchange reserves are insufficient to maintain the fixed peg, leading them to aggressively sell the domestic currency for foreign currency *before* a devaluation, forcing the central bank to deplete reserves rapidly and ultimately abandon the peg. **Vulnerability condition:** The fixed exchange rate is perceived as *overvalued* (domestic currency too strong relative to fundamentals) AND the central bank's reserves are low relative to the potential capital outflow. **Wrong answer reveals:** If a student says "speculative attack happens when exchange rate is flexible," they confuse the regime. If they say "it happens when reserves are high," they misunderstand the trigger — it's *inadequate* reserves relative to the overvaluation.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why a country with a flexible exchange rate does not need to hold large foreign exchange reserves to maintain external balance, whereas a country with a fixed exchange rate does. Use the concept of "automatic adjustment mechanism."

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Under flexible rates, a BoP deficit (excess demand for forex) causes the domestic currency to **depreciate automatically**. This makes exports cheaper and imports dearer, reducing the deficit *without* central bank intervention. Reserves remain unchanged. Under fixed rates, the exchange rate *cannot* move. The central bank *must* sell foreign reserves to meet the excess demand for forex. If the deficit persists, reserves deplete continuously. **Wrong answer reveals:** If a student says "flexible rates need reserves for intervention," they miss the defining feature — no intervention needed. If they say "fixed rates adjust via interest rates," they confuse the mechanism — under fixed rates with open capital accounts, monetary policy is *constrained* (Mundell-Fleming trilemma), not the adjustment tool.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
India currently follows a "managed floating" exchange rate regime. Suppose the RBI observes a sharp, sudden depreciation of the rupee due to foreign portfolio investors (FPIs) pulling out capital. The RBI sells $5 billion from reserves to slow the depreciation. Calculate the *immediate* impact on: (a) RBI's foreign exchange reserves, (b) Money supply (assuming no sterilization), (c) The exchange rate. Explain why this is "managed" floating, not "pure" floating.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
(a) Reserves **decrease by $5 billion** (RBI sells forex). (b) Money supply **decreases** — RBI receives rupees from banks in exchange for dollars, draining rupee liquidity (contractionary). (c) Exchange rate **depreciates less than it would have without intervention** (supply of $ increases in forex market). **Why managed floating:** In pure floating, RBI would *not* intervene; the rate would find its new equilibrium freely. Here, RBI *actively* moderates volatility ("leans against the wind") using reserves. **Wrong answer reveals:** If a student says "money supply increases," they confuse the direction — selling forex *absorbs* domestic currency. If they say "this is fixed exchange rate," they miss that intervention is *discretionary and temporary*, not a commitment to a fixed level.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the degree of **monetary policy autonomy** (ability to set domestic interest rates independently) under fixed vs. flexible exchange rates, assuming free capital mobility. Why does the "Impossible Trinity" (Mundell-Fleming trilemma) imply this difference?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
**Flexible rates:** Full monetary policy autonomy. Central bank can set interest rates for domestic goals (inflation, growth) without worrying about defending an exchange rate peg. Capital flows adjust the exchange rate. **Fixed rates:** **Zero monetary policy autonomy** (with free capital mobility). To maintain the peg, domestic interest rates *must* align with the anchor currency's rates. If domestic rates > anchor rates, massive capital inflows force revaluation pressure; if domestic rates < anchor rates, capital outflows force devaluation pressure. **Trilemma:** A country can only have 2 of 3: (1) Fixed exchange rate, (2) Free capital mobility, (3) Independent monetary policy. Flexible rates sacrifice (1) to keep (2) and (3). Fixed rates sacrifice (3) to keep (1) and (2). **Wrong answer reveals:** If a student says "fixed rates allow independent monetary policy if capital controls exist," they ignore the question's assumption of *free capital mobility*. If they say "flexible rates constrain monetary policy because of exchange rate volatility," they confuse *volatility* with *loss of autonomy* — the central bank *can* still set rates, though it may *choose* to consider exchange rate effects.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate the statement: "A flexible exchange rate system is always superior to a fixed exchange rate system for a developing economy like India because it provides automatic BoP adjustment and monetary policy independence." Provide two arguments *for* and two arguments *against* this view, specific to India's structural characteristics.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
**For:** (1) **Shock absorption:** India faces volatile oil prices (major import) and monsoon-dependent agriculture. Flexible rates allow the rupee to depreciate when oil prices spike, reducing the need for sharp interest rate hikes or reserve depletion. (2) **Monetary policy focus:** RBI can target domestic inflation (flexible inflation targeting framework since 2016) without subordinating rates to exchange rate defense. **Against:** (1) **Pass-through inflation:** India has high import dependence (oil, electronics, fertilizers). Sharp depreciation → immediate cost-push inflation, hurting the poor disproportionately. Fixed/managed rates provide anchor for inflation expectations. (2) **Shallow forex markets & dollarization:** Indian forex market is less liquid; excessive volatility invites speculation. Many Indian firms have unhedged foreign currency debt — sudden depreciation triggers balance sheet crises (corporate defaults). **Wrong answer reveals:** If a student gives only textbook merits/demerits without *India-specific* context (oil imports, inflation targeting, corporate FX debt), they are memorizing, not analyzing. If they say "fixed rates prevent inflation," they ignore that overvalued fixed rates *cause* black markets and resource misallocation.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Construct a hypothetical scenario for a small open economy (like Sri Lanka in 2022) where a **fixed exchange rate regime collapses** due to a combination of: (a) persistent current account deficit, (b) loss of market confidence, (c) inadequate reserves. Describe the sequence of events from the initial shock to the forced devaluation/floating, using the terms: "overvaluation," "speculative attack," "reserve depletion," "IMF program."

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
**Scenario:** Lanka pegs 1 USD = 200 LKR. **Step 1 (Overvaluation):** Tourism collapse (COVID) + tax cuts → large current account deficit. Real exchange rate appreciates (high inflation vs. trading partners), but nominal peg holds → LKR becomes *overvalued*. Exports uncompetitive, imports cheap. **Step 2 (Reserve depletion):** Central bank sells reserves to finance deficit & defend peg. Reserves fall from $8B to $2B (below 1 month imports). **Step 3 (Loss of confidence):** Markets see reserves < short-term external debt. Expect devaluation. **Step 4 (Speculative attack):** Exporters delay dollar conversions; importers prepay; FPIs flee. Daily forex demand >> supply. Central bank burns $1B/week. **Step 5 (Forced float):** Reserves near zero. Central bank abandons peg → LKR floats to 360/USD (devaluation). **Step 6 (IMF program):** Seeks bailout; conditions include floating rate, fiscal consolidation, monetary tightening. **Wrong answer reveals:** If a student omits "overvaluation" as the *root cause* (not just low reserves), they miss the structural imbalance. If they say "devaluation solves everything," they ignore the contractionary effects (debt burden in FX, inflation) and need for complementary policies.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
**Misconception:** "Devaluation (fixed rate) and Depreciation (flexible rate) are the same thing — just the currency becoming weaker." **Why this is wrong:** They describe *different causal mechanisms* and *policy choices*. **Devaluation** is a *deliberate, discrete policy decision* by the government to *raise* the fixed exchange rate (e.g., ₹17.50/$ → ₹25/$). It happens *once* (usually) and signals a regime shift. **Depreciation** is a *continuous, market-driven outcome* where the exchange rate falls due to demand-supply forces (e.g., ₹70/$ → ₹72/$ → ₹75/$ over weeks). **Concrete example:** India 1991 = **Devaluation** (two-step, announced by RBI). India 2013 (Taper Tantrum) = **Depreciation** (₹54 → ₹68 in months, no announcement). **Cost of confusion:** In board exams, writing "devaluation" for a flexible rate scenario loses marks. In competitive exams, misidentifying the regime leads to wrong policy prescriptions (e.g., suggesting "RBI should devalue" when the rate is already flexible).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Suppose India's MPC (c) = 0.75 and MPM (m) = 0.15. The government increases autonomous spending (G) by ₹100 crore. Calculate the change in equilibrium income (ΔY) under: (a) Flexible exchange rate regime (using open economy multiplier), (b) Hypothetical closed economy (for comparison). Explain the economic reason for the difference.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the formula for open economy multiplier: k_open = 1 / (1 - c + m)
- Step 2: Plug in values: c = 0.75, m = 0.15 → 1 - 0.75 + 0.15 = 0.40
- Step 3: Calculate k_open = 1 / 0.40 = 2.5
- Step 4: Calculate ΔY_open = k_open × ΔG = 2.5 × ₹100 crore = ₹250 crore
- Step 5: Calculate closed economy multiplier: k_closed = 1 / (1 - c) = 1 / 0.25 = 4
- Step 6: Calculate ΔY_closed = 4 × ₹100 crore = ₹400 crore
- Step 7: Interpret: The open economy multiplier (2.5) is smaller than closed (4) because m = 0.15 means 15% of every new rupee of income leaks out as imports, not spent on domestic goods. This leakage reduces the induced consumption rounds.
REASONING: The open economy multiplier is smaller because imports act as an additional leakage from the circular flow of domestic income, alongside savings and taxes. Each round of induced consumption generates less demand for *domestic* output because part of it goes to foreign goods. This is the "automatic stabilizer" feature of flexible rates — fiscal policy is less potent, but the exchange rate adjusts to offset external imbalances.
ANSWER: (a) ΔY = ₹250 crore; (b) ΔY = ₹400 crore. The open economy multiplier is 37.5% smaller due to import leakage (m = 0.15).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Under a fixed exchange rate regime, the RBI pegs ₹75/$. Due to a sudden surge in oil prices, India's import bill increases, creating a BoP deficit of $10 billion at the fixed rate. The RBI has $50 billion reserves. (a) How does the RBI defend the peg? (b) What happens to domestic money supply if the RBI does *not* sterilize? (c) If the deficit persists at $10B/quarter, how many quarters until reserves are exhausted? (d) Why might a speculative attack occur *before* reserves hit zero?
STEPS:
- Step 1: At fixed rate ₹75/$, demand for $ > supply by $10B. RBI must supply $10B from reserves to clear market.
- Step 2: RBI sells $10B, receives ₹750 crore (10B × 75) from commercial banks. This drains rupee liquidity.
- Step 3: Money supply (M3) decreases by ₹750 crore (money multiplier effect amplifies this). Interest rates rise automatically.
- Step 4: Reserves fall from $50B to $40B in Q1. At $10B/quarter deficit, reserves last 5 quarters (50/10 = 5) *if no capital flight*.
- Step 5: Speculative attack trigger: When markets perceive reserves < short-term external debt (e.g., $30B), they anticipate devaluation. Exporters delay dollar sales; importers prepay; FIIs sell rupee assets. Capital outflow >> current account deficit.
- Step 6: Attack accelerates reserve loss — $50B could vanish in weeks, not years. RBI forced to devalue or float.
REASONING: Fixed rate defense requires reserve sales, which are contractionary (reducing money supply, raising rates). This hurts domestic growth. The "speculative attack" is a self-fulfilling prophecy: fear of devaluation → capital flight → reserves crash → devaluation becomes inevitable. This is why fixed rates are fragile with open capital accounts.
ANSWER: (a) RBI sells $10B reserves; (b) Money supply falls by ₹750 crore (unsterilized); (c) 5 quarters without attack; (d) Attack occurs when reserves perceived inadequate relative to potential outflows, not at zero.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: India operates a managed float. The rupee faces depreciation pressure due to: (i) US Fed rate hikes (US rates 5.25% vs India 6.5%), (ii) Rising oil prices (import bill +$20B), (iii) FPI outflows ($15B). RBI intervenes by selling $10B and raising repo rate by 50 bps. Analyze the *conflicting* objectives and transmission channels. Use the Impossible Trinity framework.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the trilemma corners India chooses: (1) Free capital mobility (FPI flows allowed), (2) Monetary policy autonomy (RBI sets repo rate for inflation), (3) *Sacrificed:* Fixed exchange rate → Managed float.
- Step 2: Channel 1 (Interest rate differential): US hikes narrow the spread (6.5% - 5.25% = 1.25% vs previous 2.5%). Reduced carry trade incentive → FPI outflows → depreciation pressure.
- Step 3: Channel 2 (Oil shock): Higher oil imports → current account deficit widens → structural depreciation pressure (fundamentals).
- Step 4: RBI action A: Sell $10B reserves → supports rupee *short-term* but depletes buffers, signals concern.
- Step 5: RBI action B: Hike repo rate 50 bps (to 7%) → widens interest differential again → attracts capital inflows → supports rupee *and* fights domestic inflation (dual benefit).
- Step 6: Conflict: Rate hike slows domestic growth (investment, consumption). Reserve sale tightens rupee liquidity (if unsterilized). Both contractionary.
- Step 7: Managed float logic: RBI allows *gradual* depreciation (e.g., ₹82 → ₹83.5) to absorb oil shock, uses reserves/rate hikes only to prevent *disorderly* overshooting. This preserves autonomy while limiting volatility.
REASONING: The Impossible Trinity explains *why* RBI cannot fix the rate *and* keep rates low *and* allow free capital flows. By choosing managed float, RBI retains rate-setting freedom but accepts exchange rate volatility. The policy mix (reserves + rates) targets *excess volatility*, not the level. This is the practical application of the trilemma for emerging markets.
ANSWER: RBI uses mixed strategy: (1) Reserves for smoothing, (2) Rate hike for fundamental support + inflation. Trade-off: Growth slowdown. Managed float = "best of both worlds" attempt — autonomy with speed bumps.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A small open economy with a fixed exchange rate faces a sudden stop in capital inflows. The central bank has $20B reserves, short-term external debt (STED) of $25B (due in 1 year), and a current account deficit of $5B/year. The government refuses to devalue, citing "credibility." Using the concept of reserve adequacy metrics, evaluate whether this stance is sustainable. Calculate the reserve coverage ratio (Reserves / STED) and the Greenspan-Guidotti rule threshold. Explain the likely sequence if the government holds the peg.
COMPETENCY: Applying reserve adequacy metrics to assess fixed exchange rate sustainability
STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate reserve coverage ratio = Reserves / STED = $20B / $25B = 0.8 (80%).
- Step 2: Greenspan-Guidotti rule: Reserves should cover *at least* 100% of STED (i.e., ratio ≥ 1.0). Here, 0.8 < 1.0 → **Inadequate reserves**.
- Step 3: Annual financing need = Current Account Deficit + STED rollover = $5B + $25B = $30B. Reserves = $20B < $30B.
- Step 4: With "sudden stop," no new inflows. Roll-over risk: creditors refuse to renew STED → demand repayment.
- Step 5: Sequence if peg held: (i) RBI sells reserves to pay maturing STED + finance CAD. (ii) Reserves drop rapidly (< $10B in months). (iii) Market sees reserves < STED → speculative attack (capital flight accelerates). (iv) RBI raises rates sharply to defend peg → recession, banking crisis (firms with FX debt default). (v) Eventually forced devaluation/float with *much* larger overshoot than pre-emptive adjustment.
- Step 6: Conclusion: "Credibility" of an *unsustainable* peg is an illusion. Pre-emptive float/devaluation + IMF program is less costly.
ANSWER: Reserve coverage = 80% (below 100% Greenspan-Guidotti threshold). Stance is **unsustainable**. Holding the peg will trigger speculative attack, reserve exhaustion, sharp contractionary policy, and eventual disorderly collapse. Pre-emptive adjustment minimizes total cost.
DIFFICULTY: hard
