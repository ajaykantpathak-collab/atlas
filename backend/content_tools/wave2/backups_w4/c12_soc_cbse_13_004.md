## ELI5
One smartphone: chip designed in the USA, screen from South Korea, assembled in China or India, sold globally. Multinational Corporations (MNCs) — also called Transnational Corporations (TNCs) — are companies producing goods or marketing services in more than one country through a network of subsidiaries coordinated by a central headquarters, oriented to global markets and global profits. They fragment production across borders chasing cheaper labour — Fordism giving way to post-Fordism — restructuring labour, culture and livelihoods unevenly as they go.

## DEFINITION
Multinational Corporations (MNCs) or Transnational Corporations (TNCs) are companies that produce goods or market services in more than one country, operating through a network of subsidiaries and affiliates coordinated by a central headquarters, and oriented towards global markets and global profits rather than solely national ones.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the definition and characteristics of MNCs/TNCs, their role in the new international division of labour (illustrated by Nike and General Motors), the shift to flexible production (post-Fordism), impact on employment patterns (urban middle-class opportunities vs. broader jobless growth), and cultural dimensions (glocalisation, corporate culture, threat to indigenous crafts/knowledge). It does NOT cover liberalisation policy details (node 13_003), international institutions like WTO/IMF (node 13_005), sectoral impacts on agriculture (node 13_007) or labour market flexibility in depth (node 13_008).

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are holding a smartphone: its chip is designed in the USA, screen made in South Korea, assembled in China or India, and sold globally. Ask: "Who coordinates this worldwide production chain?" The answer is an MNC. Begin by defining MNCs using the NCERT criterion: production/services in multiple countries, global profit orientation. Then trace the historical shift: early MNCs produced in home country and exported; modern MNCs fragment production across countries chasing cheaper labour (Nike: Japan → South Korea → Thailand/Indonesia → India). Connect this to the concept of post-Fordism (flexible, dispersed production) versus Fordism (centralised mass production). Finally, analyse the sociological consequences: new service-sector jobs for urban youth (call centres, BPOs) versus the ILO's "disappointing" job growth data, and the cultural dynamics of glocalisation (McDonald's vegetarian menu) versus threats to traditional livelihoods (Mumbai mill workers, Sircilla weavers, dombari acrobats). The key insight is that MNCs are not just economic actors; they restructure social relations, labour, and culture unevenly across the globe.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Define a Transnational Corporation (TNC) and give two examples from the NCERT text.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1

A wrong answer (e.g., "any big Indian company" or "a company that only exports") reveals the student confuses MNCs with large domestic firms or exporters, missing the core feature of multi-country production/operations.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain the difference between Fordism and post-Fordism in the context of MNC production strategies. Why does the Nike example illustrate post-Fordism?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Fordism = centralised mass production at a single location (e.g., early automobile plants). Post-Fordism = flexible, dispersed production across multiple global sites to minimise costs. Nike illustrates post-Fordism because it shifted production from Japan → South Korea → Thailand/Indonesia → India as labour costs rose, with no permanent factories — production moves to wherever labour is cheapest. A wrong answer that describes Nike as simply "outsourcing" without mentioning the *systematic shifting* of production bases in response to labour costs misses the structural logic of post-Fordism.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Using the General Motors (Pontiac Le Mans) example from Box 6.4, calculate the percentage of the showroom price that goes to the United States. Then explain what this breakdown reveals about the new international division of labour.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Showroom price = $20,000. Amount to Americans = $7,600. Percentage = (7600/20000) × 100 = 38%. The breakdown reveals that a single "American" car is actually a global product: 48% to South Korea (labour/assembly), 28% to Japan (advanced components), 12% to Germany (design), 7% to Taiwan/Singapore (small components), 4% to UK (marketing), 1% to Barbados/Ireland (data processing). This shows MNCs fragment production across countries based on comparative advantage (cheap labour, advanced tech, design skills), creating a new international division of labour. A wrong answer that only gives the percentage without interpreting the global fragmentation misses the sociological significance.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the impact of MNC-driven globalisation on two groups: (a) urban middle-class youth in India and (b) traditional weavers in Sircilla/Dubakka (Andhra Pradesh). Use specific examples from the text.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
(a) Urban middle-class youth: new career opportunities in IT, call centres/BPOs, shopping malls, restaurants — "high-flying professionals with exorbitant salaries" driving consumer culture. (b) Sircilla/Dubakka weavers: unable to invest in technology, outcompeted by power looms, leading to suicides. A wrong answer that claims "globalisation benefits everyone" or "only harms the poor" fails to capture the *uneven, class-specific* impact central to sociological analysis.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Critique the statement: "Glocalisation is merely a marketing strategy adopted by MNCs to sell products; no genuine cultural synthesis occurs." Use evidence from the text to support your position.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The text presents glocalisation as a *strategy* (McDonald's vegetarian menu, Navaratri specials, MTV/Star using Indian languages) but also notes *organic* cultural mixing: Bhangra pop, Indi pop, fusion music, remixes, and Bollywood stories shifting from foreign locations to diaspora narratives. A weak answer takes only one side (purely commercial OR purely organic) without recognising the dialectic: MNCs *initiate* glocalisation for profit, but local actors *appropriate* it, creating new cultural forms beyond corporate control.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often equate MNCs with *foreign direct investment (FDI)* or *international institutions (WTO/IMF)*, treating them as synonyms for "globalisation policies." They write: "MNCs like WTO impose rules on India." This is wrong because MNCs are *corporate actors* (profit-driven firms like Nike, Coke), while WTO/IMF are *intergovernmental organisations* (rule-making bodies). The confusion arises because both appear in the same chapter and both drive globalisation, but their *nature, agency, and accountability* differ fundamentally. MNCs respond to market incentives; WTO/IMF set regulatory frameworks. Students also assume all MNCs are Western — the text notes Indian firms (Tata, Infosys) are becoming transnational, a nuance often missed.

## ON_FAILURE
If the student conflates MNCs with international institutions or FDI policies: (1) Have them re-read the NCERT subsection "The transnational corporations" (page 76-77) and "Globalisation of finance" (page 77-78) side by side, listing *actors* (firms vs. institutions) and *actions* (production/investment vs. rule-making/loans). (2) Ask them to classify these entities: Coca Cola, WTO, IMF, Tata Motors, Infosys, World Bank — into "MNC/TNC" or "International Institution." (3) Then assign a 150-word answer: "Explain why an MNC might lobby the WTO, but is not the WTO." This forces distinction between corporate agency and institutional authority.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A sociologist wants to study the impact of an MNC's entry on a traditional craft cluster (e.g., handloom weaving in Varanasi). Using the Nike case study as a model, outline the steps to trace the "commodity chain" from raw material to final consumer, identifying where the MNC captures value and where local artisans lose autonomy.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Map the existing local commodity chain — identify raw material suppliers (cotton/silk farmers), spinners, dyers, weavers, master weavers/traders, local retailers, and consumers. Note the social relations (caste, kinship, credit) binding each link.
- Step 2: Identify the MNC's entry point — does it buy directly from weavers (bypassing master weavers), contract through a local subsidiary, or import machine-made substitutes? In Nike's case, the MNC *contracted* factories in Asia but owned design/marketing.
- Step 3: Trace value capture — calculate the final retail price of an MNC-branded handloom product (e.g., a scarf sold globally for ₹5,000). Estimate the weaver's share (e.g., ₹200). The difference (₹4,800) goes to branding, logistics, retail, design, profit — controlled by the MNC.
- Step 4: Analyse autonomy loss — under the old system, weavers had some bargaining power within local networks. Under MNC contracts, specifications (design, quality, deadline) are set globally; non-compliance means contract termination. The "flexibility" is one-sided.
- Step 5: Document social consequences — interview weavers who shifted to MNC contract work versus those who stayed independent. Look for deskilling, loss of design creativity, indebtedness to meet MNC standards, and gendered impacts (women spinners displaced first, as in Bihar silk).
REASONING: This mirrors the Nike trajectory: production shifts to low-cost sites, but *control* (design, branding, finance) remains at headquarters. The commodity chain method reveals how MNCs reorganise *social relations of production*, not just technology. The NCERT text emphasises that globalisation "affects us all but affects us differently" — this method makes that visible.
ANSWER: A five-step commodity chain analysis linking local craft production to global MNC control, showing value capture (weaver gets <5% of retail price) and autonomy loss (design/deadlines dictated externally).
