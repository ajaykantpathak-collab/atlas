## ELI5
A landline call reserves a copper wire end to end — even through silence; a WhatsApp voice note travels as independent packets over shared links. Circuit switching establishes a dedicated end-to-end physical path for the entire communication session, reserving bandwidth exclusively; packet switching breaks data into independent packets routed individually by destination address, enabling statistical multiplexing. Circuit guarantees performance per connection; packet maximizes total utilization — the Internet's choice.

## DEFINITION
Circuit switching establishes a dedicated end-to-end physical path between sender and receiver for the entire communication session, reserving bandwidth exclusively. Packet switching breaks data into independent packets that travel via shared network resources, each routed individually based on destination address, allowing statistical multiplexing of bandwidth.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you're calling a friend on a landline (circuit switched) versus sending a WhatsApp voice note (packet switched). In the landline call, the telephone exchange physically connects a copper wire from your home to your friend's home for the entire conversation — even during silence. In WhatsApp, your voice is chopped into small packets, each traveling independently through routers over shared fiber cables, reassembled at the destination. Ask: "Why does the Internet use the WhatsApp model?" Guide students to see that circuit switching wastes bandwidth during idle periods (silence in calls, think-time in browsing) and cannot scale to billions of intermittent connections. Packet switching shares links statistically, enabling the Internet's explosive growth. Key insight: circuit switching guarantees performance per connection; packet switching maximizes total network utilization.

Takeaway: mastery means the five comparison rows filled — setup, allocation, reliability, latency, efficiency. Exam moves: comparison questions tabulate the two switchings; justification questions argue why the Internet chose packets despite circuit's guaranteed QoS; waste questions quantify idle bandwidth. Method: run the two calls — one silent landline, one chatty voice note — and count the wasted copper. Drill: five dimensions, one verdict, one waste story. Students miss it because "dedicated" sounds better — idle time is the bill.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What are the three distinct phases of a circuit-switched communication session, and what happens in each phase?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The three phases are: (1) Connection Setup — a dedicated path is established end-to-end via signaling; (2) Data Transfer — data flows continuously over the reserved path with constant latency; (3) Connection Teardown — the path is released and resources freed. A wrong answer (e.g., "setup, routing, teardown") reveals confusion with packet switching's per-packet routing. Students often think circuit switching involves routing decisions during transfer — it does not; the route is fixed at setup.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why packet switching is more efficient than circuit switching for bursty data traffic like web browsing.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Web browsing is bursty: short data bursts (HTTP requests/responses) separated by long idle periods (user reading). Circuit switching reserves bandwidth for the entire session, wasting it during idle times. Packet switching shares links statistically — packets from many users interleave on the same link, so idle periods of one user are filled by others' packets. A wrong answer ("packet switching is faster") misses the core concept: efficiency comes from statistical multiplexing, not speed. Students often confuse "efficiency" with "latency" — packet switching actually has higher per-packet latency due to store-and-forward delays.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A 1 Mbps link is shared by 10 users. Each user is active 10% of the time at 100 kbps when active. Calculate the probability that more than 10 users are simultaneously active (assume independence). Would circuit switching or packet switching handle this better? (Use binomial approximation: P(X>10) ≈ 0 for n=10, p=0.1)

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Circuit switching requires dedicating 100 kbps per user → 10 users need 1 Mbps exactly. But if even one extra user becomes active (11 users), demand = 1.1 Mbps > 1 Mbps → blocking. Probability of >10 active users is effectively 0 for n=10, so circuit switching works here. However, packet switching handles it better because it allows statistical multiplexing: average load = 10 users × 0.1 × 100 kbps = 100 kbps, far below 1 Mbps. Even with bursts, queues absorb temporary overload. A wrong answer ("circuit switching is better because no queueing") ignores that circuit switching would require 10 Mbps capacity to guarantee no blocking for 10 users at peak — a 10× overprovisioning. Students often forget circuit switching must provision for peak rate, not average rate.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the impact of a single link failure on an ongoing circuit-switched call versus a packet-switched TCP connection. Which recovers faster and why?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Circuit switching: the dedicated path is broken → call drops immediately. Recovery requires new connection setup (signaling, path reservation) — takes seconds. Packet switching: TCP detects loss via timeout/duplicate ACKs, retransmits lost packets, and routing protocols (OSPF/BGP) converge to a new path — recovery in milliseconds to seconds. Packet switching wins because routing is decentralized and per-packet; no end-to-end reservation to tear down. A wrong answer ("both drop equally") misses that packet switching's connectionless network layer (IP) reroutes around failures transparently to TCP. Students often think TCP is circuit-switched because it's "connection-oriented" — but TCP connections are logical, not physical reservations.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate this claim: "Packet switching is always better than circuit switching for real-time applications like VoIP because it uses bandwidth more efficiently." Defend or refute with two specific technical arguments.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Refute. Argument 1: Packet switching introduces variable delay (jitter) due to queueing at routers, which degrades VoIP quality unless complex QoS (priority queuing, jitter buffers) is added. Circuit switching provides constant latency and zero jitter by design. Argument 2: Packet switching has no admission control — congestion can cause packet loss and uncontrolled delay. Circuit switching uses admission control (busy signal) to guarantee QoS for admitted calls. A wrong answer ("packet switching is better because it's modern") shows no understanding of QoS trade-offs. Students often equate "efficient" with "better for all applications" — but real-time apps need predictability, not just average efficiency.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a hybrid network scenario where circuit switching and packet switching coexist optimally. Specify which traffic uses which, and justify with one quantitative metric for each.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Example: A corporate WAN with dedicated leased lines (circuit switched) for ERP database replication (needs guaranteed 50 Mbps, <5 ms latency, zero loss) and IPsec VPN over Internet (packet switched) for email/web (bursty, tolerant of 100 ms latency). Metric for circuit: committed information rate (CIR) = 50 Mbps with 99.999% availability. Metric for packet: average throughput > 10 Mbps with <1% packet loss. A poor answer ("use circuit for voice, packet for data") is vague — must specify traffic characteristics and measurable QoS targets. Students often propose hybrids without quantitative justification, missing that engineering decisions require metrics.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students believe "packet switching = Internet, circuit switching = telephone" and that they are mutually exclusive technologies. In reality, modern networks layer them: MPLS creates virtual circuits (LSPs) over packet-switched IP/MPLS cores, and LTE/5G use circuit-scheduled radio resources (OFDMA time-frequency blocks) for packet data. The misconception arises because textbooks present them as historical alternatives, not as complementary techniques in protocol stacks. Students then fail to answer questions like "Does VoIP use circuit or packet switching?" — it uses packet switching (RTP/UDP/IP) but often runs over MPLS circuits in provider backbones.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A company has a 2 Mbps leased line. They need to support 20 voice calls (each 64 kbps constant bit rate) and bursty data traffic averaging 500 kbps. Should they use circuit switching (TDM) or packet switching? Calculate maximum simultaneous calls for each and explain.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Circuit switching (TDM) allocates fixed 64 kbps slots. 2 Mbps / 64 kbps = 32 slots. So max 32 calls. But 20 calls use 20×64 = 1280 kbps, leaving 720 kbps for data — sufficient for average 500 kbps.
- Step 2: Packet switching shares 2 Mbps statistically. Voice needs 64 kbps each with low jitter. Data is bursty. With 20 calls, average voice load = 1280 kbps. Data average = 500 kbps. Total average = 1780 kbps < 2 Mbps. But bursts may cause congestion.
- Step 3: For voice quality, circuit switching guarantees zero jitter. Packet switching needs QoS (priority queue) to protect voice packets from data bursts.
- Step 4: Decision: Use circuit switching for voice (20 dedicated slots), packet switching for data over remaining bandwidth. Or use packet switching with strict priority queuing for voice.
- Step 5: Verify: Circuit switching wastes bandwidth during silence (voice activity ~40%). Packet switching with silence suppression (VAD) saves more bandwidth but adds complexity.
REASONING: Circuit switching provides deterministic QoS for constant-bit-rate voice. Packet switching maximizes utilization for variable-bit-rate data. Hybrid approach (TDM for voice, packet for data) is common in enterprise leased lines.
ANSWER: Circuit switching supports up to 32 calls; 20 calls leave 720 kbps for data. Packet switching supports 20 calls + data statistically but requires QoS for voice quality. Hybrid TDM+packet is optimal.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Compare end-to-end delay for a 1000-byte packet over 3 hops (each link 10 Mbps, propagation delay 5 ms) in packet switching vs a 1000-byte message in circuit switching (setup time 200 ms, same links). Assume store-and-forward packet switching, no queueing.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Packet switching: Transmission delay per hop = packet size / bandwidth = 8000 bits / 10 Mbps = 0.8 ms. Propagation delay per hop = 5 ms. Store-and-forward: packet must be fully received before forwarding. Total delay = 3 × (transmission + propagation) = 3 × (0.8 + 5) = 17.4 ms. (No setup time for datagram; virtual circuit adds setup.)
- Step 2: Circuit switching: Setup time = 200 ms. Then message transmission: 1000 bytes = 8000 bits. Since circuit is dedicated, transmission is continuous: transmission delay = 8000 bits / 10 Mbps = 0.8 ms (only at first link? Actually, in circuit switching, bits propagate continuously; the first bit arrives at destination after propagation delay of 3 hops = 15 ms, last bit after 15 + 0.8 = 15.8 ms). Total = setup + transmission + propagation = 200 + 0.8 + 15 = 215.8 ms.
- Step 3: For short messages, packet switching wins (17.4 ms vs 215.8 ms). For long sessions, circuit setup amortizes.
- Step 4: If packet switching uses virtual circuit (setup required), add setup time similar to circuit switching.
- Step 5: Interpret: Circuit switching has high setup overhead; packet switching has per-hop store-and-forward overhead. For bursty traffic, packet switching avoids setup per burst.
REASONING: Circuit switching shifts delay to connection setup; packet switching distributes delay per hop via store-and-forward. The crossover point depends on message size and session duration.
ANSWER: Packet switching delay = 17.4 ms. Circuit switching delay = 215.8 ms. Packet switching better for short messages; circuit switching better for long continuous streams.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A packet switch has 4 input ports, each receiving packets at 1 Gbps. Output port is 1 Gbps. Packets are 1500 bytes. Calculate maximum throughput and average queueing delay if all 4 inputs send continuously (worst-case). Use M/D/1 queue approximation: average delay = (ρ / (2μ(1-ρ))) where ρ = λ/μ, λ = arrival rate (packets/sec), μ = service rate (packets/sec).
STEPS:
- Step 1: Packet size = 1500 bytes = 12000 bits. Service rate μ = output bandwidth / packet size = 1 Gbps / 12000 bits = 83,333 packets/sec.
- Step 2: Total arrival rate λ = 4 inputs × (1 Gbps / 12000 bits) = 4 × 83,333 = 333,333 packets/sec.
- Step 3: Utilization ρ = λ/μ = 333,333 / 83,333 = 4.0 > 1 → unstable! Queue grows infinitely. Throughput limited to μ = 83,333 packets/sec = 1 Gbps. 75% of packets dropped or delayed indefinitely.
- Step 4: To avoid loss, need output bandwidth ≥ 4 Gbps or input rate limiting. In practice, TCP congestion control reduces input rates.
- Step 5: This shows packet switching's statistical multiplexing gain: if inputs are bursty (not continuous), average λ < μ and queues stay bounded. Circuit switching would require 4 Gbps output to guarantee no loss for 4×1 Gbps inputs.
REASONING: Packet switching exploits burstiness — average rate matters, not peak. Circuit switching must provision for peak. The M/D/1 formula quantifies delay when ρ<1; here ρ>1 shows congestion collapse without rate control.
ANSWER: Maximum throughput = 1 Gbps (output capacity). Average queueing delay → infinite (ρ=4>1). Requires congestion control or output speedup.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Analyze this network design flaw: "A university uses circuit-switched TDM over 1 Gbps fiber for all traffic: 500 VoIP phones (64 kbps each), 2000 student laptops (bursty web), and 50 research servers (continuous 100 Mbps backups at night). The TDM frame has 1000 slots." Identify two fundamental mismatches and propose a packet-switched alternative with one configuration parameter.
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction in system design
STEPS:
- Step 1: Mismatch 1: VoIP uses 500×64 kbps = 32 Mbps constant, but TDM allocates fixed slots — wasted during silence (40% activity). Mismatch 2: Research backups need 100 Mbps for hours at night — TDM would require dedicating 100 slots permanently, starving day traffic.
- Step 2: Packet switching alternative: Use 1 Gbps Ethernet with VLANs and QoS. VoIP in high-priority queue (DSCP EF), web in best-effort, backups in low-priority scavenger class (DSCP CS1).
- Step 3: Configuration parameter: Weighted Fair Queuing (WFQ) weights — VoIP: 50%, Web: 40%, Backup: 10%. Or use token bucket policers: VoIP 64 kbps guaranteed, Backup 100 Mbps max at night via time-based policy.
- Step 4: Verify: Daytime: VoIP gets 32 Mbps guaranteed, Web shares rest. Night: Backup gets up to 100 Mbps (scavenger yields to VoIP/Web). No fixed slots, full statistical multiplexing.
- Step 5: Explain why TDM fails: It provisions for peak of all services simultaneously (32M + 100M + web peak) > 1 Gbps, requiring overprovisioning. Packet switching provisions for statistical peaks.
ANSWER: Two mismatches: (1) TDM wastes bandwidth on silent VoIP calls (no voice activity detection). (2) TDM cannot time-share backup bandwidth — night backups would require permanent slot reservation. Packet-switched fix: 1 Gbps Ethernet with DSCP-based WFQ. Config parameter: time-based policy map — backup class gets 100 Mbps 22:00-06:00, else 10 Mbps.
DIFFICULTY: hard
