## ELI5
A Class 11 Secret Santa: students on one side, gifts under 200 rupees on the other. A relation is not the draw - it is the final list of who-gives-what, a curated subset of all possible pairings. Check any list: every first element from the students, every second from the gifts, and it qualifies. From one list you read the domain (the givers), the range (the gifts actually given) and the co-domain (every gift on the table) - and the range is always a subset of the co-domain, never to be confused with it.

## DEFINITION
A relation \( R \) from a non-empty set \( A \) to a non-empty set \( B \) is a subset of the Cartesian product \( A \times B \). If \( (a, b) \in R \), then \( b \) is called the image of \( a \) under \( R \), and we write \( a \, R \, b \). The set of all first elements is the **domain**, the set of all second elements is the **range**, and the set \( B \) is the **co-domain** (with Range \( \subseteq \) Co-domain).

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can, given any rule or set of ordered pairs between two sets, correctly write the relation in roster form, draw its arrow diagram, and explicitly identify the domain, range, and co-domain — distinguishing range from co-domain every time.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the definition of a relation as a subset of a Cartesian product, the concepts of domain, range, co-domain, image, and the representation of relations (roster form, set-builder form, arrow diagram). It does **not** cover types of relations (reflexive, symmetric, transitive, equivalence) or functions — those are in the next node (c11_app_cbse_04_005).

## WALKTHROUGH
Start with a relatable scenario: "You're organising a Class 11 Secret Santa. There are 6 students (Set A) and 10 possible gifts under ₹200 (Set B). You randomly draw chits to decide who gives a gift to whom — but wait, a relation is just the *list* of who-gives-what, not the drawing process." Ask: "If I write down {(Rahul, Book), (Priya, Pen), (Aman, Book)}, is this a relation from Students to Gifts?" Guide them to check: Are all first elements from Students? All second from Gifts? Is it a subset of Students × Gifts? Yes. Then extract domain {Rahul, Priya, Aman}, range {Book, Pen}, co-domain = all 10 gifts. Key insight: **A relation is just a curated subset of all possible pairings — the "rule" picks which pairs make the cut.**

Takeaway: mastery means writing any relation as a subset of the Cartesian product and reading domain, range and co-domain off it without mixing range into co-domain. Exam moves: identify-the-three questions and count-possible-relations items on the Secret-Santa scene. Check yourself: the co-domain is the whole gift table; the range is only what got given - the subset relation between them is the definition, not a coincidence. Students miss it because all three sound like the sets involved, when the who-gives-what list shows the design: domain collects first elements, range collects second elements, and the co-domain waits untouched - the relation's rule, not the sets' size, decides which pairs make the cut.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Let \( A = \{1, 2, 3\} \) and \( B = \{4, 5\} \). Is \( R = \{(1, 4), (2, 5), (3, 4), (1, 5)\} \) a relation from \( A \) to \( B \)? Justify your answer in one sentence.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Yes, because every ordered pair in \( R \) has its first element from \( A \) and second from \( B \), so \( R \subseteq A \times B \). A wrong answer (e.g., "No, because 1 appears twice") reveals the student confuses **relations** with **functions** — they think each element of the domain must have a unique image, which is a function requirement, not a relation requirement.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
A student writes: "For the relation \( R = \{(x, y) : y = x^2, x \in \{-2, -1, 0, 1, 2\}, y \in \mathbb{N}\} \), the co-domain is \( \{0, 1, 4\} \)." Is this correct? Explain why or why not.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
No, it is incorrect. The co-domain is the set \( \mathbb{N} \) (natural numbers) as defined in the problem; the **range** is \( \{0, 1, 4\} \). A wrong answer reveals the student conflates **co-domain** (the "target set" declared upfront) with **range** (the "actual outputs" generated by the rule). This is the #1 confusion in Class 11 relations.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Let \( A = \{a, b, c\} \) and \( B = \{1, 2\} \). Define a relation \( R \) from \( A \) to \( B \) by \( R = \{(x, y) : x \text{ is a vowel}, y = 1\} \). Write \( R \) in roster form and find its domain and range.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Identify vowels in \( A \): only \( a \) (assuming English vowels). Step 2: Apply rule: \( y = 1 \) when \( x \) is a vowel. So \( R = \{(a, 1)\} \). Step 3: Domain = \( \{a\} \), Range = \( \{1\} \). A wrong answer (e.g., \( R = \{(a,1), (b,1), (c,1)\} \)) reveals the student ignores the condition "\( x \) is a vowel" and applies \( y=1 \) to all \( x \in A \) — they treat the rule as unconditional.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Two relations are defined on \( A = \{1, 2, 3\} \):
\( R_1 = \{(1,1), (2,2), (3,3)\} \) and \( R_2 = \{(1,2), (2,3), (1,3)\} \).
Compare their domains and ranges. What does this tell you about the connection between the "rule" defining a relation and its domain/range?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
\( R_1 \): Domain = Range = \( \{1,2,3\} \). \( R_2 \): Domain = \( \{1,2\} \), Range = \( \{2,3\} \). The domain/range depend **entirely** on which ordered pairs satisfy the rule — not on the full sets \( A \) or \( B \). A wrong answer (e.g., "Both have domain \( \{1,2,3\} \) because \( A = \{1,2,3\} \)") reveals the student thinks domain = set \( A \) always, confusing **domain of a relation** with **domain of a function** (where every element of \( A \) must appear).

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
"Every subset of \( A \times B \) is a valid relation from \( A \) to \( B \), including the empty set." Defend or critique this statement with a concrete example.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Defend: By definition, a relation is **any** subset of \( A \times B \). The empty set \( \varnothing \subseteq A \times B \), so \( \varnothing \) is a relation (the **empty relation**). Example: \( A = \{1\}, B = \{2\} \), \( A \times B = \{(1,2)\} \). Subsets: \( \varnothing, \{(1,2)\} \) — both are relations. A wrong critique (e.g., "Empty set isn't a relation because no elements are related") reveals the student thinks a relation must have at least one pair — confusing "non-empty relation" with "relation".

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a real-life scenario (Indian context) involving two sets \( A \) and \( B \) where a relation \( R \) from \( A \) to \( B \) is **not** a function. Explicitly state \( A, B, R \), and explain why \( R \) fails to be a function.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Example: \( A = \{\text{Rainy, Sunny, Cloudy}\} \) (weather types in Mumbai), \( B = \{\text{Umbrella, Sunglasses, Raincoat, Hat}\} \) (accessories). \( R = \{(\text{Rainy, Umbrella}), (\text{Rainy, Raincoat}), (\text{Sunny, Sunglasses}), (\text{Cloudy, Umbrella}), (\text{Cloudy, Hat})\} \). Rainy maps to two accessories → not a function. A poor answer (e.g., \( R \) where every element of \( A \) maps to exactly one in \( B \)) reveals the student cannot distinguish relations from functions — they default to functional thinking.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
**Misconception**: "The domain of a relation from \( A \) to \( B \) is always \( A \), and the co-domain is always the range."  
**Why it happens**: In Class 10, students mostly see functions \( f: A \to B \) where every \( a \in A \) has an image, so domain = \( A \). They also rarely distinguish co-domain vs range because textbook problems often set co-domain = range.  
**Concrete error**: Given \( A = \{1,2,3\}, B = \{4,5\}, R = \{(1,4), (2,4)\} \), a student writes Domain = \( \{1,2,3\} \), Co-domain = \( \{4\} \). Correct: Domain = \( \{1,2\} \), Range = \( \{4\} \), Co-domain = \( \{4,5\} \).

## ON_FAILURE
1. **Re-anchor definitions**: Have the student write the three definitions side-by-side: Domain = {first elements of pairs in R}, Range = {second elements of pairs in R}, Co-domain = B (the set given in "R from A to B").  
2. **Contrast with function**: Show a function \( f: A \to B \) (every \( a \in A \) has exactly one image) and a relation \( R \) from \( A \) to \( B \) where some \( a \in A \) have no image. Ask: "What is the domain of each?"  
3. **Drill with empty domain**: Give \( R = \varnothing \) from \( A = \{1,2\} \) to \( B = \{3\} \). Ask for domain, range, co-domain. Answer: Domain = \( \varnothing \), Range = \( \varnothing \), Co-domain = \( \{3\} \). This breaks the "domain = A" habit.  
4. **Visual cue**: In arrow diagrams, domain = nodes with **outgoing** arrows; range = nodes with **incoming** arrows; co-domain = **all** nodes in the right set.

## THIS_FORMULA
Number of relations from set A to set B = \( 2^{n(A) \times n(B)} \) (since each relation is a subset of \( A \times B \), and \( A \times B \) has \( n(A)n(B) \) elements).

## FORMULA_VARIABLES
- \( n(A) \): number of elements in set A
- \( n(B) \): number of elements in set B
- \( n(A \times B) = n(A) \times n(B) \): number of ordered pairs in the Cartesian product

## FORMULA_UNITS
N/A (counts of elements, dimensionless)

## COMPUTATION_TYPE

c11_app_cbse_06_001 | Permutations and Combinations (Chapter 6): The formula \( 2^n \) for number of subsets is a combinatorial result (sum of \( \binom{n}{r} \)); relations provide a concrete application of subset counting.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A school has 4 clubs: Science (S), Arts (A), Sports (P), Coding (C). A survey asks each club to pick their favourite festival from {Diwali, Holi, Eid, Christmas}. The results are: Science → Diwali, Arts → Holi, Sports → Diwali, Coding → Eid. Represent this as a relation \( R \) from Clubs to Festivals. Write \( R \) in roster form, draw the arrow diagram (describe it), and find domain, range, co-domain.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify sets: \( A = \{S, A, P, C\} \) (clubs), \( B = \{Diwali, Holi, Eid, Christmas\} \) (festivals).
- Step 2: Translate each "Club → Festival" into an ordered pair: (S, Diwali), (A, Holi), (P, Diwali), (C, Eid).
- Step 3: Write \( R \) in roster form: \( R = \{(S, Diwali), (A, Holi), (P, Diwali), (C, Eid)\} \). Verify each pair has first element from \( A \), second from \( B \) → \( R \subseteq A \times B \).
- Step 4: Domain = set of first elements = \( \{S, A, P, C\} \). Range = set of second elements = \( \{Diwali, Holi, Eid\} \). Co-domain = \( B = \{Diwali, Holi, Eid, Christmas\} \).
- Step 5: Note that Christmas is in co-domain but not in range — no club chose it. This illustrates Range ⊆ Co-domain.

REASONING: A relation is defined by the actual pairings that occur. The domain is exactly the clubs that participated (all 4 here). The range is the festivals actually chosen. The co-domain is the full list of options provided in the survey.

ANSWER: \( R = \{(S, Diwali), (A, Holi), (P, Diwali), (C, Eid)\} \); Domain = \( \{S, A, P, C\} \); Range = \( \{Diwali, Holi, Eid\} \); Co-domain = \( \{Diwali, Holi, Eid, Christmas\} \).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Let \( A = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\} \). Define a relation \( R \) on \( A \) (i.e., from \( A \) to \( A \)) by \( R = \{(x, y) : x + y = 6\} \). Write \( R \) in roster form. Find domain, range, and co-domain. How many total relations are possible from \( A \) to \( A \)?

STEPS:
- Step 1: Since \( R \) is on \( A \), both coordinates come from \( A = \{1,2,3,4,5\} \). We need all pairs \( (x, y) \) with \( x, y \in A \) and \( x + y = 6 \).
- Step 2: Systematically check \( x = 1 \) to \( 5 \):
  \( x=1 \Rightarrow y=5 \) (valid, both in A) → (1,5)
  \( x=2 \Rightarrow y=4 \) → (2,4)
  \( x=3 \Rightarrow y=3 \) → (3,3)
  \( x=4 \Rightarrow y=2 \) → (4,2)
  \( x=5 \Rightarrow y=1 \) → (5,1)
- Step 3: Roster form: \( R = \{(1,5), (2,4), (3,3), (4,2), (5,1)\} \).
- Step 4: Domain = \( \{1,2,3,4,5\} \) (all elements appear as first coordinate). Range = \( \{1,2,3,4,5\} \) (all appear as second coordinate). Co-domain = \( A = \{1,2,3,4,5\} \).
- Step 5: Total relations from \( A \) to \( A \): \( n(A) = 5 \), so \( n(A \times A) = 25 \). Number of subsets = \( 2^{25} \).

REASONING: The rule \( x+y=6 \) acts as a filter on \( A \times A \). We test each possible \( x \) to see if the required \( y \) stays in \( A \). Domain and range are derived from the pairs that survive the filter. The total number of relations uses the subset formula.

ANSWER: \( R = \{(1,5), (2,4), (3,3), (4,2), (5,1)\} \); Domain = Range = Co-domain = \( \{1,2,3,4,5\} \); Total relations = \( 2^{25} \).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Let \( A = \{2, 3, 4, 5\} \) and \( B = \{3, 6, 7, 10\} \). Define \( R = \{(x, y) \in A \times B : x \text{ divides } y\} \). (i) Write \( R \) in roster form. (ii) Find domain and range. (iii) Is \( R \) a function from \( A \) to \( B \)? Justify.

STEPS:
- Step 1: List all pairs in \( A \times B \) mentally or on paper: 4 × 4 = 16 pairs. We only keep those where \( x \mid y \) (x divides y).
- Step 2: Test each \( x \in A \):
  \( x=2 \): divides 6, 10 → (2,6), (2,10)
  \( x=3 \): divides 3, 6 → (3,3), (3,6)
  \( x=4 \): divides none of 3,6,7,10 → no pairs
  \( x=5 \): divides 10 → (5,10)
- Step 3: Roster form: \( R = \{(2,6), (2,10), (3,3), (3,6), (5,10)\} \).
- Step 4: Domain = \( \{2, 3, 5\} \) (4 has no image). Range = \( \{3, 6, 10\} \). Co-domain = \( B = \{3,6,7,10\} \).
- Step 5: Check function condition: Does every element of domain have a **unique** image? No — 2 maps to both 6 and 10; 3 maps to both 3 and 6. So \( R \) is **not** a function.
- Step 6: Note: Even if we restrict to domain \( \{2,3,5\} \), 2 and 3 still have multiple images → not a function.

REASONING: The divisibility rule generates multiple pairs for the same \( x \). A function requires exactly one image per domain element; a relation allows zero or many. This example shows how a natural mathematical rule (divisibility) creates a non-functional relation.

ANSWER: (i) \( R = \{(2,6), (2,10), (3,3), (3,6), (5,10)\} \); (ii) Domain = \( \{2,3,5\} \), Range = \( \{3,6,10\} \), Co-domain = \( \{3,6,7,10\} \); (iii) Not a function because 2 has two images (6,10) and 3 has two images (3,6).

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A relation \( R \) is defined from \( A = \{1, 2, 3, 4\} \) to \( B = \{1, 4, 9, 16, 25\} \) by \( R = \{(x, y) : y = x^2\} \). A student claims: "Since every \( x \in A \) has exactly one \( y \in B \), this relation is a function, and therefore its domain is \( A \) and its range is \( B \)." Evaluate the student's claim. Identify which parts are correct and which are incorrect, with justification.

COMPETENCY: Critical evaluation of domain/range vs co-domain in a functional relation

STEPS:
- Step 1: Verify the rule: For \( x \in A \), compute \( x^2 \): 1→1, 2→4, 3→9, 4→16. All results are in \( B \).
- Step 2: Check function condition: Each \( x \in A \) maps to exactly one \( y \) → Yes, \( R \) is a function.
- Step 3: Domain of \( R \): Set of first elements = \( \{1,2,3,4\} = A \). Student is correct here.
- Step 4: Range of \( R \): Set of second elements = \( \{1,4,9,16\} \). Co-domain = \( B = \{1,4,9,16,25\} \). Student incorrectly says range = \( B \); 25 is in co-domain but not in range.
- Step 5: Conclusion: The relation is a function, domain = A, but range ⊂ co-domain (proper subset). The student confused co-domain with range.

ANSWER: The student is correct that \( R \) is a function and that domain = \( A \). However, the range is \( \{1, 4, 9, 16\} \), not \( B \). The element 25 ∈ B has no pre-image in A, so it is in the co-domain but not the range. Range ⊆ Co-domain, not necessarily equal.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Let \( A = \{1, 2, 3, \dots, 10\} \). Define a relation \( R \) on \( A \) by \( R = \{(x, y) \in A \times A : |x - y| \text{ is a multiple of } 3\} \). (i) Find the number of elements in \( R \). (ii) Determine whether \( R \) is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. (iii) Based on (ii), what type of relation is \( R \)? (Note: This connects to the next node on types of relations.)

COMPETENCY: Multi-concept synthesis — counting relation elements + analysing properties (reflexive/symmetric/transitive) as a preview of equivalence relations

STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand the rule: \( x \, R \, y \) iff \( 3 \mid (x - y) \), i.e., \( x \equiv y \pmod{3} \). This partitions \( A \) into residue classes mod 3.
- Step 2: Residue classes in \( A = \{1..10\} \):
  Class 0: \( \{3, 6, 9\} \) (3 elements)
  Class 1: \( \{1, 4, 7, 10\} \) (4 elements)
  Class 2: \( \{2, 5, 8\} \) (3 elements)
- Step 3: Count pairs in \( R \): Within each class, every element relates to every element (including itself). So \( |R| = 3^2 + 4^2 + 3^2 = 9 + 16 + 9 = 34 \).
- Step 4: Check reflexive: For all \( x \in A \), \( |x-x|=0 \) (multiple of 3) → \( (x,x) \in R \). Yes, reflexive.
- Step 5: Check symmetric: If \( |x-y| \) is multiple of 3, then \( |y-x| = |x-y| \) is also a multiple of 3 → \( (y,x) \in R \). Yes, symmetric.
- Step 6: Check transitive: If \( 3 \mid (x-y) \) and \( 3 \mid (y-z) \), then \( 3 \mid [(x-y)+(y-z)] = (x-z) \) → \( (x,z) \in R \). Yes, transitive.
- Step 7: Since \( R \) is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, it is an **equivalence relation** on \( A \).

ANSWER: (i) 34 elements in \( R \). (ii) Reflexive: Yes. Symmetric: Yes. Transitive: Yes. (iii) \( R \) is an equivalence relation on \( A \).
