## ELI5
Same workshop, two eras: in 1990 you need a government license to produce, quotas choke raw material imports, machinery carries 150% tariffs, and the RBI fixes the rupee's value. Your cousin in 2024 needs no license, imports at low tariffs, sells globally through digital platforms, and the market sets the exchange rate. Between them stands the New Economic Policy of 1991 on three pillars: Liberalisation removing government controls, Privatisation reducing state ownership through disinvestment, Globalisation integrating India with world markets - born of a crisis and a conditional $7 billion loan.

## DEFINITION
The New Economic Policy (NEP) of 1991 is a comprehensive set of economic reforms structured around three pillars — Liberalisation (removing government controls on private enterprise), Privatisation (reducing state ownership through disinvestment and strategic sales), and Globalisation (integrating the domestic economy with global markets through trade and capital flow liberalisation) — initiated to resolve the Balance of Payments crisis and transition India from a controlled, inward-looking mixed economy to a market-oriented, globally competitive system.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why did the NEP 1991 classify reforms into "stabilisation measures" and "structural reform measures"? Give one example of each with its time horizon.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Stabilisation measures are short-term (1-2 years) to fix the immediate BoP crisis and control inflation — e.g., rupee devaluation (July 1991) to boost exports and compress imports. Structural reform measures are long-term (5-10+ years) to improve efficiency and competitiveness — e.g., abolishing industrial licensing for all but 18 industries. A wrong answer (swapping examples or time horizons) shows the student misses the core logic: stabilisation buys time; structural reform changes the economy's supply-side capacity.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the pre-1991 "License-Permit Raj" industrial regime with the post-1991 liberalised regime on three dimensions: (a) entry barriers, (b) production capacity decisions, (c) price determination. Which dimension saw the MOST radical change?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Pre-1991: (a) Industrial license required for entry, expansion, new products; (b) Government fixed maximum capacity (MRTP Act); (c) Administered prices for key goods (cement, steel, aluminium). Post-1991: (a) Licensing abolished except 6 sectors (alcohol, cigarettes, hazardous chemicals, industrial explosives, electronics, aerospace, drugs/pharma); (b) Firms decide capacity based on market demand; (c) Market-determined prices in most industries. Most radical change: (a) entry barriers — because removing the "license to exist" enabled all other freedoms. A student identifying price determination as most radical misses that price controls were already easing in the 1980s; the license abolition was the structural break.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students equate "Liberalisation" with "Privatisation" — thinking both mean "selling government companies." In reality, Liberalisation = removing restrictions on private activity (entry, capacity, pricing, imports); Privatisation = transferring ownership of existing public assets. Example: Allowing a private firm to start a telecom company without license is Liberalisation; selling VSNL (a government telecom company) to Tata is Privatisation. This confusion persists because both increase private sector role, but the mechanism (new entry vs asset transfer) and fiscal implication (no revenue vs one-time revenue) differ fundamentally.

## ON_FAILURE
If a student conflates the two, do this 3-step fix: (1) Draw two columns: "New Private Firm Enters" vs "Govt Sells Existing Firm." List 3 examples each (e.g., new private bank license vs disinvestment of IDBI Bank; new private airline vs sale of Air India). (2) Ask: "Which generates revenue for government?" Only Privatisation. (3) Ask: "Which increases competition immediately?" Both, but Liberalisation does so by expanding the player pool; Privatisation does so by changing incentives of an existing player. Use the Navratna example (Box 3.1): granting autonomy is Liberalisation-lite; selling equity is Privatisation.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: In 1990-91, India's import tariff on capital goods averaged 120%. A textile firm wanted to import a spinning machine costing $100,000 (FOB). Post-reforms, the tariff was reduced to 25% by 1997-98. Calculate the tariff savings per machine and explain how this fits the Liberalisation pillar's "reduction of cost of capital" rationale.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the economic concept — import tariff as a tax on capital goods raises the domestic price of investment, discouraging modernisation.
- Step 2: Recall the formula: Tariff payment = FOB price × Tariff rate. Pre-reform rate = 120% = 1.2; Post-reform rate = 25% = 0.25.
- Step 3: Calculate pre-reform tariff = $100,000 × 1.2 = $120,000. Post-reform tariff = $100,000 × 0.25 = $25,000.
- Step 4: Tariff savings = $120,000 - $25,000 = $95,000 per machine. At 1991 exchange rate (~₹25/$), this is ₹2.375 crore saved.
- Step 5: Interpret economically — the firm can now afford 4.8 machines for the same landed cost of 1 machine pre-reform, directly lowering cost of capital and boosting competitiveness. This is a structural reform (permanent rule change), not stabilisation.

REASONING: The example connects a specific Liberalisation measure (tariff reduction) to firm-level investment incentives. The step-by-step calculation shows how policy translates to price signals, which is the core mechanism of liberalisation.

ANSWER: Tariff savings = $95,000 per machine (₹2.375 crore at 1991 rate), enabling ~4.8x more capital formation per rupee spent.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: The government set a disinvestment target of ₹2,500 crore for 1991-92 but mobilised ₹3,040 crore. In 2022-23, the target was ₹65,000 crore but actual mobilisation was ₹46,000 crore. Calculate the achievement percentage for both years and analyse the trend in disinvestment credibility.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the concept — disinvestment target vs actual measures government's commitment and market appetite for PSU equity.
- Step 2: Formula: Achievement % = (Actual Mobilisation / Target) × 100.
- Step 3: 1991-92: (₹3,040 / ₹2,500) × 100 = 121.6% (exceeded target). 2022-23: (₹46,000 / ₹65,000) × 100 = 70.8% (missed target).
- Step 4: Trend analysis — early years saw over-achievement (low-hanging fruit, minority stake sales in profitable PSUs); recent years show under-achievement (strategic sales like Air India/BPCL are complex, market volatility, valuation disputes).
- Step 5: Connect to NCERT critique — "assets undervalued" (1990s) vs "proceeds used for revenue deficit" (ongoing). The declining achievement % reflects both tougher sale structures and fiscal dependence on disinvestment.

REASONING: This example uses real fiscal data to evaluate Privatisation's fiscal dimension. The percentage calculation is simple arithmetic, but the economic interpretation requires understanding the shift from minority stake sales (easy, quick) to strategic sales (hard, slow) — a key nuance in c11_eco_cbse_03_007.

ANSWER: 1991-92: 121.6% (exceeded); 2022-23: 70.8% (missed). Trend: declining achievement % indicates increasing difficulty of strategic disinvestment and persistent fiscal reliance on asset sales.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Combine LPG concepts with prior knowledge. India's FDI inflows rose from $100 million (1990-91) to $83.6 billion (2021-22). During the same period, the share of services sector in GDP grew from 44% to 54%, while manufacturing stagnated at ~16%. Using the Globalisation pillar rationale ("efficiency of local industries and adoption of modern technologies"), explain why FDI flowed disproportionately to services (IT, finance, telecom) rather than manufacturing.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Recall Globalisation pillar objective from NCERT: "increase international competitiveness of industrial production and also foreign investments and technology into the economy... promote efficiency of local industries and adoption of modern technologies."
- Step 2: Identify FDI determinants — services (IT, BPO, finance) require low tariff barriers (already reduced), skilled English-speaking labour (India's comparative advantage), and digital infrastructure (improved post-1991). Manufacturing requires land, labour law flexibility, power, logistics — where reforms were slower (labour laws, land acquisition, infrastructure bottlenecks).
- Step 3: Apply prior concept from c9_eco_ch1_5 (How to Produce: choice of production techniques) — services use human capital-intensive techniques; manufacturing uses physical capital-intensive techniques. India's factor endowment (abundant skilled labour, scarce reliable power/land) favours services FDI.
- Step 4: Apply c10_eco_cbse_04_003 (Globalisation factors) — technology (internet) made services tradable globally; India's time-zone advantage for BPO/IT created natural comparative advantage.
- Step 5: Synthesise — Globalisation opened the door, but Liberalisation's incomplete industrial reforms (labour, land, power) meant manufacturing couldn't walk through. FDI followed the path of least resistance: services.
- Step 6: Policy implication — "Make in India" (2014) attempts to address the manufacturing gap by targeting the missing Liberalisation reforms.

REASONING: This example forces integration across three nodes: LPG rationale (this node), factor endowment theory (Class 9), and globalisation drivers (Class 10). It demonstrates that FDI pattern is not automatic — it reflects the *selective* success of Liberalisation across sectors.

ANSWER: FDI concentrated in services due to (1) lower reform barriers (no land/labour law hurdles), (2) India's skilled English-speaking labour endowment matching services' factor intensity, (3) digital tradability of services via internet. Manufacturing FDI lagged due to incomplete Liberalisation in physical infrastructure and factor markets.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A Class 11 student argues: "The NEP 1991 was imposed by the IMF/World Bank — India had no choice. Therefore, the LPG framework lacks domestic legitimacy." Using the distinction between stabilisation measures (conditionalities) and structural reform measures (domestic policy choices), evaluate this argument. Cite two specific reforms that were IMF conditionalities and two that were autonomous domestic choices.

COMPETENCY: Evaluating policy autonomy vs external conditionality in reform design

STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand the context — the $7 billion IMF/World Bank loan came with conditionalities (stabilisation: fiscal deficit reduction, rupee devaluation, trade liberalisation).
- Step 2: Identify IMF conditionalities — (i) Rupee devaluation (July 1991, two-step 18-19% depreciation), (ii) Reduction of import tariffs and removal of quantitative restrictions (trade policy reform).
- Step 3: Identify autonomous structural reforms — (i) Industrial delicensing (abolished for all but 18 industries) — not an IMF condition; driven by domestic consensus on License Raj inefficiency. (ii) Financial sector reforms (RBI role shift, private bank entry, FII entry) — homegrown, based on Narasimham Committee (1991) recommendations.
- Step 4: Evaluate — Stabilisation was externally driven (crisis management); structural reforms had strong domestic intellectual roots (e.g., Raj Committee 1972, Chakravarty Committee 1985, Narasimham 1991). The LPG *framework* was negotiated, but its *content* blended external pressure and domestic agenda.
- Step 5: Conclude — The argument is partially true for stabilisation, false for structural reform. Legitimacy comes from subsequent democratic endorsement (every government since 1991 continued reforms).

ANSWER: The argument confuses crisis-triggered conditionalities (stabilisation: devaluation, trade liberalisation) with domestically rooted structural reforms (industrial delicensing, financial sector autonomy). IMF mandated BoP correction; India chose *how* to restructure the supply side. Democratic continuity since 1991 confirms domestic legitimacy.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Table 3.1 shows services sector growth averaged 8.2% (1992-2001) vs 6.7% (1980-91), while agriculture declined from 3.6% to 3.3%. A policymaker claims: "LPG reforms caused the services boom and agriculture decline." Using the concept of "composition effect" vs "causal effect," and the NCERT assessment (Section 3.6), critique this claim with three specific counter-arguments.

COMPETENCY: Distinguishing correlation from causation in sectoral growth analysis

STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand the claim — it attributes sectoral growth divergence directly to LPG reforms (post hoc ergo propter hoc).
- Step 2: Counter-argument 1 (Pre-trend): Services growth was already accelerating pre-1991 (6.7% in 1980-91 vs ~4.5% in 1970s) due to domestic demand expansion and early 1980s liberalisation (c11_eco_cbse_03_001 mentions "a few liberalisation measures were introduced in 1980s"). LPG amplified, not initiated, the trend.
- Step 3: Counter-argument 2 (Neglect, not reform): NCERT Section 3.6 states agriculture declined due to "public investment in agriculture... fallen," "partial removal of fertiliser subsidy," "reduction in import duties on agricultural products" — these are *specific policy omissions/commissions*, not LPG per se. The reform *package* included agriculture-neglecting choices; LPG didn't mandate them.
- Step 4: Counter-argument 3 (Globalisation asymmetry): NCERT notes "developing countries are compelled to open up... but developed countries maintain high non-tariff barriers" (e.g., US textile quotas). Agriculture faced import competition without export market access — a Globalisation *governance failure*, not an inherent LPG outcome.
- Step 5: Synthesise — The sectoral divergence reflects *asymmetric reform design* (industry/services liberalised, agriculture protected then exposed) and *global rule asymmetry*, not LPG as a monolithic cause.

ANSWER: The claim oversimplifies. (1) Services growth pre-dated 1991; LPG accelerated an existing trend. (2) Agriculture decline resulted from specific policy choices (investment withdrawal, subsidy cuts, import exposure without export access) — not LPG inherently. (3) Globalisation's asymmetric market access (developed country barriers) hurt agriculture disproportionately. Causality runs through *design choices within LPG*, not LPG itself.
