## ELI5
Three ways to run a marketplace's price tag: nail it to the counter (fixed - the government pegs the rate and defends it with reserves), let haggling decide (flexible - demand and supply alone set it), or mostly haggle with a referee who steps in during brawls (managed floating). The peg requires the central bank to buy or sell foreign exchange endlessly; the float lets the price move; the hybrid smooths the volatility. Each choice trades certainty against reserves and policy freedom.

## DEFINITION
A fixed exchange rate system is one where the government or central bank pegs the domestic currency to another currency (or a basket of currencies) at a specific value and maintains that rate by buying or selling foreign exchange reserves. A flexible (floating) exchange rate system allows the exchange rate to be determined solely by market forces of demand and supply for foreign currency without central bank intervention. Managed floating is a hybrid where the exchange rate is primarily market-determined but the central bank occasionally intervenes to smooth excessive volatility or influence the level.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are an importer in Mumbai needing to pay $1 million for machinery from the US. You go to your bank to buy dollars. If India has a fixed exchange rate (say ₹75/$), the bank will sell you dollars at that rate because the RBI stands ready to supply dollars from its reserves to keep the rate at ₹75. If India has a flexible rate, the bank will quote the market rate — maybe ₹83/$ today, ₹82.5/$ tomorrow — depending on how many other importers and exporters are buying/selling dollars. Start by recalling the demand-supply framework for foreign exchange (node c12_eco_cbse_11_010). Then ask: who ensures the market clears at a particular price? In fixed regime, the central bank does; in flexible regime, the price (exchange rate) moves to clear the market. This distinction drives all the differences in reserve requirements, automatic adjustment, and policy autonomy.

Takeaway first: the regimes differ on who clears the market - the central bank's reserves under a peg, the price itself under a float, and usually the price under managed floating. Exam moves: distinguish questions on the three systems via reserves and market forces, adjustment questions after a BoP surplus or deficit under each, and evaluation questions weighing certainty against autonomy for a developing economy. Method: rerun the importer's dollar purchase under all three regimes and watch who quotes the rate - the RBI's vault, the market's mood, or the mood with a steadying hand. Check yourself: India's described regime is managed floating - the exam expects the hybrid, not a pure float. Drill: narrate one BoP deficit's fate under each of the three regimes.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Define a fixed exchange rate system and state the specific obligation it imposes on the central bank.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
A fixed exchange rate system is one where the government/central bank announces an official par value for the domestic currency against a foreign currency (or basket) and commits to buy/sell unlimited amounts of foreign exchange at that rate to maintain the peg. The central bank must hold sufficient foreign exchange reserves to intervene whenever market demand ≠ market supply at the fixed rate. A wrong answer (e.g., "the rate never changes" or "the central bank sets the rate but doesn't have to intervene") reveals confusion between a declared peg and the operational requirement of reserve-backed intervention.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why does a country operating under a fixed exchange rate system need to hold large foreign exchange reserves? Explain the mechanism using the concept of excess demand/supply in the foreign exchange market.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Under a fixed rate, the exchange rate cannot adjust to equate demand and supply. If at the fixed rate there is excess demand for foreign currency (e.g., due to higher imports or capital outflow), the central bank must sell foreign currency from its reserves to meet the gap. Conversely, excess supply requires the central bank to buy foreign currency, adding to reserves. Without adequate reserves, the central bank cannot honor its commitment, leading to a forced devaluation or abandonment of the peg. A wrong answer that says "reserves are needed to pay for imports" confuses the central bank's role with private importers' needs; the key is that reserves back the central bank's promise to clear the market at the fixed price.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Suppose Country X fixes its currency at 10 units per USD. Initially, market demand = market supply = $50 billion at this rate. Due to a sudden rise in foreign interest rates, capital outflows increase the demand for USD to $70 billion while supply remains at $50 billion. Calculate the change in the central bank's foreign exchange reserves and the new reserve level if initial reserves were $30 billion. Can the peg be sustained?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Excess demand = $70b - $50b = $20b. The central bank must sell $20b from reserves to meet this demand. New reserves = $30b - $20b = $10b. The peg can be sustained only if reserves remain adequate to cover future imbalances; at $10b, the country is vulnerable. A wrong answer that calculates excess supply or ignores the direction of reserve change shows misunderstanding of which side of the market the central bank operates on.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the automatic adjustment mechanism of a balance of payments deficit under a flexible exchange rate system with the adjustment process under a fixed exchange rate system. Use the terms "depreciation", "expenditure switching", and "reserve loss" in your answer.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Under flexible rates, a BoP deficit means excess demand for foreign currency → domestic currency depreciates → exports become cheaper, imports dearer → net exports rise (expenditure switching) → deficit self-corrects without reserve loss. Under fixed rates, the same deficit creates excess demand → central bank sells reserves to maintain the peg → reserves fall → money supply contracts (if unsterilized) → domestic income and imports fall → deficit corrects but at the cost of reserve depletion and potential output loss. A wrong answer that claims fixed rates also use depreciation confuses the two regimes; fixed rates by definition prevent exchange rate movement.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate the statement: "A flexible exchange rate system is superior to a fixed exchange rate system for a developing economy like India because it provides monetary policy autonomy and eliminates the need for large foreign exchange reserves." Consider both the benefits and the risks (e.g., volatility, speculation) in your judgment.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is partially correct but incomplete. Flexible rates do grant monetary policy autonomy (central bank can set interest rates for domestic goals without defending a peg) and reduce the need for large reserves (no obligation to intervene at a fixed price). However, developing economies often face high exchange rate volatility due to thin markets, speculative attacks, and liability dollarization (firms borrow in foreign currency). Sharp depreciation can trigger balance sheet crises and imported inflation. India's managed floating regime attempts to capture the benefits of flexibility while using intervention to curb excessive volatility. A wrong answer that uncritically endorses either extreme misses the nuanced trade-offs and India's actual policy choice.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a policy response for a country with a fixed exchange rate that is experiencing a persistent balance of payments deficit and dwindling reserves. Your response must include (a) an expenditure-reducing policy, (b) an expenditure-switching policy that does not involve changing the peg, and (c) a condition under which devaluation becomes inevitable.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
(a) Expenditure-reducing: tighten fiscal policy (cut government spending/raise taxes) or monetary policy (raise interest rates) to reduce aggregate demand and imports. (b) Expenditure-switching without devaluation: implement import tariffs/quotas or export subsidies to shift demand toward domestic goods. (c) Devaluation becomes inevitable when reserves fall below a critical threshold (e.g., less than 3 months of imports) and the government cannot or will not implement sufficient (a) and (b) due to political/economic costs. A poor answer that suggests "just devalue immediately" ignores the policy sequence and the fact that devaluation is a last resort under a fixed regime.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think "fixed exchange rate means the exchange rate never changes." In reality, a fixed rate can be changed by official action — devaluation (deliberate downward adjustment) or revaluation (upward adjustment). The defining feature is not immutability but the central bank's commitment to defend the current parity using reserves. Another error: confusing managed floating with fixed rate because "the central bank intervenes." In managed floating, intervention is discretionary and aims to smooth volatility, not to maintain a specific level; there is no announced par value and no obligation to intervene at a predetermined rate.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: India operates a managed floating exchange rate regime. The current market exchange rate is ₹83.00 per USD. The RBI considers ₹82.50–₹83.50 as an acceptable band. Due to a sudden surge in portfolio outflows, the demand for USD increases sharply. At the current rate, excess demand is $2 billion. The RBI decides to intervene by selling dollars from its reserves. If the RBI sells exactly $2 billion, what will be the new exchange rate? Explain the economic reasoning.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the regime — managed floating means the RBI intervenes to limit volatility, not to fix a specific rate. The band ₹82.50–₹83.50 indicates the RBI's comfort zone.
- Step 2: Recognize that excess demand of $2 billion at ₹83.00 would, without intervention, push the rate above ₹83.00 (depreciation).
- Step 3: The RBI sells $2 billion from reserves to meet the excess demand. This increases the supply of USD in the market by exactly the amount of excess demand.
- Step 4: With the additional supply, the market clears at the original rate ₹83.00 (assuming the intervention is fully effective and no further shifts occur).
- Step 5: Interpret: Reserves fall by $2 billion; the exchange rate remains within the band. If outflows persist, reserves will keep falling, and the RBI may eventually allow the rate to move toward the upper band or beyond.
REASONING: In managed floating, the central bank uses reserves to smooth temporary fluctuations, not to maintain a rigid peg. The intervention amount equals the excess demand at the target rate, preventing the rate from moving. This is different from a fixed rate where the central bank must intervene at a single announced price regardless of band.
ANSWER: The new exchange rate remains ₹83.00 per USD (within the band). RBI reserves decrease by $2 billion.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Country Y has a fixed exchange rate of 20 Y-currency units per USD. The market demand and supply functions for USD are: Qd = 500 - 10e, Qs = 100 + 5e, where e is the exchange rate (Y-currency per USD). (a) Verify that the fixed rate is not the market-clearing rate. (b) Calculate the excess demand or supply at the fixed rate. (c) Determine the change in central bank reserves required to maintain the peg.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Find the market-clearing (flexible) exchange rate by setting Qd = Qs: 500 - 10e = 100 + 5e → 400 = 15e → e = 26.67. The flexible rate would be 26.67, but the fixed rate is 20. So the fixed rate is below the market rate (currency is overvalued).
- Step 2: At e = 20, calculate Qd = 500 - 10(20) = 300. Qs = 100 + 5(20) = 200.
- Step 3: Excess demand = Qd - Qs = 300 - 200 = 100 units of USD.
- Step 4: To maintain the peg, the central bank must supply 100 units of USD from its reserves. Reserves fall by 100 units.
- Step 5: Economic interpretation: The overvalued fixed rate makes imports artificially cheap and exports dearer, creating a structural excess demand for foreign currency. Persistent reserve loss will eventually force a devaluation.
REASONING: The fixed rate creates a disequilibrium because it does not equal the market-clearing rate. The central bank's reserve loss is exactly the quantity gap at that price. This illustrates why overvalued fixed rates are unsustainable without capital controls or large initial reserves.
ANSWER: (a) Market-clearing rate = 26.67 ≠ 20. (b) Excess demand = 100 units of USD. (c) Central bank reserves decrease by 100 units of USD.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Compare the impact of an increase in domestic income (leading to higher imports) on the exchange rate and foreign exchange reserves under (i) a fixed exchange rate system and (ii) a flexible exchange rate system. Assume initial equilibrium at e = ₹75/$, Q = $100 billion. The income increase shifts the demand curve rightward by $20 billion at every exchange rate. Use diagrams and/or algebraic functions to show the outcomes.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Set up initial equilibrium. Demand: Qd = a - be, Supply: Qs = c + de. At e=75, Q=100. (Exact parameters not needed for qualitative comparison.)
- Step 2: Fixed rate system: The exchange rate is fixed at ₹75. The demand shift creates excess demand of $20 billion at e=75. The central bank must sell $20 billion from reserves to meet this demand. Reserves fall by $20 billion. Exchange rate remains ₹75.
- Step 3: Flexible rate system: The exchange rate adjusts to clear the market. The new demand curve intersects the unchanged supply curve at a higher exchange rate (depreciation). Suppose the new equilibrium is e = ₹80. At this rate, quantity demanded = quantity supplied = new equilibrium quantity (say $110 billion). Reserves unchanged.
- Step 4: Compare: Fixed rate → reserve loss, no exchange rate change, imports remain relatively cheap (no expenditure switching). Flexible rate → depreciation, expenditure switching (imports become costlier, exports cheaper), reserves intact.
- Step 5: Policy implication: Under fixed rate, the country loses reserves and may need contractionary policy to reduce imports. Under flexible rate, the depreciation automatically dampens the import surge.
REASONING: This example combines the demand-shift analysis from Chapter 5 (Market Equilibrium) with the exchange rate regime mechanics. It shows the core trade-off: fixed rates absorb shocks through reserves; flexible rates absorb shocks through price adjustment.
ANSWER: (i) Fixed rate: Exchange rate unchanged at ₹75; reserves fall by $20 billion. (ii) Flexible rate: Exchange rate depreciates (e.g., to ₹80); reserves unchanged; equilibrium quantity rises (e.g., to $110 billion).

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Read the following case and answer the questions that follow.
"Since 1994, India has followed a market-determined exchange rate system (managed floating). The RBI intervenes in the foreign exchange market to curb excess volatility but does not target a specific level. In 2013, during the 'taper tantrum', the rupee depreciated sharply from ₹55/$ to ₹68/$. The RBI sold dollars and raised interest rates to stabilize the currency. In 2022, after the Russia-Ukraine war, the rupee crossed ₹80/$ for the first time. The RBI again intervened, but the rupee was allowed to find its market level."
(a) Identify the exchange rate regime described. Justify your answer with two pieces of evidence from the text.
(b) Explain why the RBI's sale of dollars in 2013 did not constitute a fixed exchange rate policy.
(c) If India had a fixed exchange rate at ₹55/$ in 2013, what would have been the consequence for RBI reserves and domestic interest rates?
COMPETENCY: Analyzing real-world policy regimes and distinguishing between managed floating and fixed exchange rates using textual evidence.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the regime from the description — "market-determined exchange rate system (managed floating)" and "RBI intervenes to curb excess volatility but does not target a specific level."
- Step 2: Evidence 1: "rupee depreciated sharply from ₹55/$ to ₹68/$" — the rate was allowed to move significantly. Evidence 2: "RBI intervened, but the rupee was allowed to find its market level" — no commitment to a par value.
- Step 3: In a fixed rate, intervention is mandatory at the announced price; here intervention is discretionary and the rate moved freely.
- Step 4: Under a fixed rate at ₹55/$, the massive capital outflow would have created huge excess demand for dollars. RBI would have had to sell massive reserves to maintain ₹55, likely depleting reserves rapidly. To stem the outflow without devaluing, RBI would have had to raise interest rates sharply (as it did), but the reserve loss would have been far larger and unsustainable.
ANSWER: (a) Managed floating. Evidence: (i) Large depreciation allowed (₹55 to ₹68), (ii) RBI explicitly does not target a specific level. (b) Fixed rate requires intervention at a pre-announced price; here intervention was discretionary and the rate changed substantially. (c) Reserves would have fallen dramatically (by tens of billions of dollars); domestic interest rates would have been raised sharply to defend the peg, hurting domestic growth.
DIFFICULTY: medium

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Assertion (A): Under a flexible exchange rate system, a country can pursue an independent monetary policy because it does not need to use interest rates to defend a fixed exchange rate parity.
Reason (R): In a flexible exchange rate system, the central bank does not hold foreign exchange reserves.
Choose the correct option:
(a) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A.
(b) Both A and R are true but R is NOT the correct explanation of A.
(c) A is true but R is false.
(d) A is false but R is true.
COMPETENCY: Evaluating the relationship between exchange rate regime, monetary policy autonomy, and reserve holding — a classic JEE/NEET/CUET assertion-reason pattern.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Assess Assertion A. True — the "impossible trinity" (Mundell-Fleming) states that with free capital mobility, a country cannot simultaneously have fixed exchange rate, independent monetary policy, and open capital account. Flexible rates allow monetary autonomy because the exchange rate adjusts instead of reserves/interest rates.
- Step 2: Assess Reason R. False — central banks under flexible rates DO hold foreign exchange reserves (for intervention in managed floating, for emergency liquidity, for sovereign wealth, etc.). They just don't need to hold them to defend a fixed parity.
- Step 3: Since A is true and R is false, the correct option is (c).
- Step 4: Note that R is a common misconception: students think flexible rate = zero reserves. The correct link is: fixed rate requires reserves to defend the peg; flexible rate does not require reserves for that purpose, but reserves are still held for other reasons.
ANSWER: (c) A is true but R is false.
DIFFICULTY: hard
