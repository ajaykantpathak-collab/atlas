## ELI5
Mass is frozen energy: E equals m c squared, and the enormous c squared is why a fingertip of uranium out-burns tonnes of coal. Einstein's equivalence means any energy change in a closed system is a mass change - the Sun's shining literally lightens it. Nuclear bookkeeping uses one atomic mass unit, worth nine hundred thirty-one and a half MeV of energy, making defect-to-energy conversion a one-line multiplication.

## DEFINITION
Mass-energy equivalence states that mass and energy are interchangeable; a system of mass \(m\) possesses an equivalent energy \(E\) given by \(E = mc^2\), where \(c = 2.99792458 \times 10^8 \, \text{m/s}\) is the speed of light in vacuum. This means mass is a concentrated form of energy, and any change in the total energy of a closed system results in a corresponding change in its mass.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers Einstein's mass-energy equivalence relation \(E = mc^2\), its experimental verification via nuclear mass defects, calculation of energy equivalents in joules and MeV, and the conceptual bridge to nuclear binding energy (covered in node c12_phy_cbse_13_002). It does NOT cover derivation from special relativity, relativistic kinetic energy, or particle-antiparticle annihilation beyond the scope of NCERT Class 12.

## WALKTHROUGH
Observe a nuclear reactor or the Sun: both release enormous energy from tiny amounts of matter. Begin by recalling from Class 11 (Work-Energy Theorem) that energy is conserved in an isolated system. Now ask: what if mass itself is a form of energy? Einstein's 1905 insight was that the "missing mass" (mass defect) in a nucleus appears as binding energy. Measure the mass of a helium nucleus (4.0026 u) and compare it to the sum of its 2 protons and 2 neutrons (4.03298 u). The difference \(\Delta m = 0.03038 \, \text{u}\) multiplied by \(c^2\) gives the binding energy (~28 MeV). This quantitative link — \(\Delta E = \Delta m c^2\) — is the tool we will master.

Takeaway first: mass-energy equivalence prices every rest mass at c squared per kilogram, and nuclear mass defects are simply energy bills settled in mass. Exam moves: conversion questions between mass and energy in joules and MeV, defect questions linking nuclear masses to binding energies, and contrast questions between nuclear and chemical magnitudes. Method: rerun the helium nucleus - free nucleons summed, actual mass measured, the defect multiplied by c squared into MeV. Check yourself: chemical reactions also change mass, just immeasurably - eV-scale defects against nuclear MeV ones; the difference is nine orders, not kind. Drill: convert one mass defect into MeV and one joule figure into kilograms.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
State the SI unit of the energy equivalent of 1 kg of mass according to \(E = mc^2\), and write the numerical value of \(c\) to be used in calculations.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The SI unit is joule (J). The speed of light \(c = 3.00 \times 10^8 \, \text{m/s}\) (approximate) or \(2.9979 \times 10^8 \, \text{m/s}\) (exact). A wrong answer like "MeV" or "eV" reveals confusion between SI units and nuclear physics convenience units; a wrong value of \(c\) (e.g., \(3 \times 10^5 \, \text{km/s}\) without conversion) reveals unit inconsistency.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
A student writes: "In a chemical reaction like burning coal, mass is conserved exactly, so \(E = mc^2\) does not apply." Explain why this statement is incorrect, and estimate the order of magnitude of the mass defect for a reaction releasing 1 eV of energy.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The statement is incorrect because \(E = mc^2\) applies universally; mass-energy interconversion occurs in chemical reactions too, but the mass defect is ~\(10^{-9}\) u (since 1 eV = \(1.6 \times 10^{-19}\) J → \(\Delta m = E/c^2 \approx 1.78 \times 10^{-36}\) kg ≈ \(10^{-9}\) u), which is too small to measure. A student who thinks mass is strictly conserved in chemistry has not grasped that mass defect exists but is negligible compared to nuclear reactions (where defects are ~0.1 u per nucleon).

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Calculate the energy equivalent (in MeV) of the mass defect \(\Delta m = 0.13691 \, \text{u}\) for the \(^{16}_8\text{O}\) nucleus, using \(1 \, \text{u} = 931.5 \, \text{MeV}/c^2\). Show unit cancellation explicitly.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
\(E = \Delta m \times 931.5 \, \text{MeV}/c^2 \times c^2 = 0.13691 \times 931.5 \, \text{MeV} = 127.5 \, \text{MeV}\). Steps: (1) Identify \(\Delta m = 0.13691 \, \text{u}\). (2) Use conversion \(1 \, \text{u} = 931.5 \, \text{MeV}/c^2\). (3) Multiply: \(0.13691 \times 931.5 = 127.5\). (4) Units: u × (MeV/c²)/u × c² = MeV. A wrong answer like 127.5 J or forgetting \(c^2\) cancellation reveals unit-tracking failure.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Two nuclei, \(^{56}_{26}\text{Fe}\) and \(^{238}_{92}\text{U}\), have binding energies per nucleon of 8.75 MeV and 7.6 MeV respectively. Which nucleus is more tightly bound? If \(^{238}\text{U}\) splits into two \(^{119}\) fragments each with 8.5 MeV/nucleon, estimate the total energy released. Explain the physical reasoning.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
\(^{56}\text{Fe}\) is more tightly bound (higher \(E_b/A\)). For fission: initial total binding energy = \(238 \times 7.6 = 1808.8 \, \text{MeV}\). Final total binding energy = \(2 \times 119 \times 8.5 = 2023 \, \text{MeV}\). Energy released = \(2023 - 1808.8 = 214.2 \, \text{MeV}\). The reasoning: energy is released when less bound nucleons reorganize into more bound configurations; the mass defect increases, so mass decreases, and the difference appears as kinetic energy of fragments. A student who subtracts per-nucleon values (8.5 – 7.6 = 0.9 MeV) and multiplies by 238 gets the same number but must justify why multiplication by total nucleons is correct.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A proposed "perpetual motion" machine claims to convert 1 g of water completely into energy continuously without any mass input. Evaluate this claim using \(E = mc^2\) and the law of conservation of mass-energy. What fundamental principle does it violate?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The claim violates conservation of mass-energy. Converting 1 g of water yields \(E = (10^{-3} \, \text{kg})(3 \times 10^8)^2 = 9 \times 10^{13} \, \text{J}\) once. After that, the mass is gone; you cannot get more energy without new mass input. The machine would need a source of mass to sustain output. A student who says "it violates energy conservation" is partially correct but misses the unified mass-energy conservation: mass is the fuel, and it is consumed.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple thought experiment to demonstrate that the binding energy of a nucleus must be included in its total mass. Use a system of two protons and two neutrons combining to form \(^4_2\text{He}\). Specify initial and final masses, and the energy released.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Initial: 2 free protons (2 × 1.00727 u) + 2 free neutrons (2 × 1.00866 u) = 4.03186 u. Final: \(^4_2\text{He}\) nucleus (atomic mass 4.002603 u minus 2 electron masses 2×0.000548 u = 4.001507 u). Mass defect = 0.030353 u. Energy released = 0.030353 × 931.5 MeV = 28.27 MeV. The helium nucleus is lighter by exactly this mass equivalent. A poor answer omits electron mass subtraction or uses atomic masses directly without adjustment, showing confusion between atomic and nuclear masses.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think "mass defect" means mass is *lost* or *destroyed* in a nuclear reaction. They write: "Mass is converted into energy, so mass decreases and energy increases, but total mass+energy is conserved." This phrasing implies mass and energy are separate conserved quantities that trade off. The correct view: mass *is* energy; the system's total mass-energy is a single conserved quantity. The "missing mass" is not destroyed — it never existed as separate mass; the bound system simply has less inertia than its free constituents. Writing "mass is converted to energy" in board exams loses marks because it suggests a transformation between two distinct entities rather than a manifestation of the same entity.

## ON_FAILURE
If a student confuses "mass conversion" with "mass-energy unity", do this 3-step remediation: (1) Revisit c11_phy_cbse_05_010 (Conservation of Mechanical Energy) and emphasize that *total* energy is conserved, not kinetic/potential separately. (2) Use the helium-4 example from Diagnostic 6: calculate initial total mass (free nucleons) and final mass (bound nucleus) side-by-side; highlight that the *system's* mass decreased because internal energy (binding) is negative. (3) Ask: "If you weigh the helium nucleus and the free nucleons on a balance, which side goes down?" The free nucleons side — proving the bound system has less mass. Repeat until the student says "the bound system's mass includes its negative binding energy."

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A typical Indian household LPG cylinder contains 14.2 kg of liquefied petroleum gas. If, hypothetically, the entire mass of the gas could be converted directly into energy via \(E = mc^2\), how many joules of energy would be released? Express the answer in scientific notation with 3 significant figures. (Use \(c = 3.00 \times 10^8 \, \text{m/s}\).)
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given: \(m = 14.2 \, \text{kg}\), \(c = 3.00 \times 10^8 \, \text{m/s}\). Find \(E\) in joules.
- Step 2: Write formula \(E = mc^2\). Check units: kg × (m/s)² = kg·m²/s² = J. ✓
- Step 3: Substitute: \(E = (14.2) \times (3.00 \times 10^8)^2\).
- Step 4: Compute: \((3.00 \times 10^8)^2 = 9.00 \times 10^{16}\). Then \(14.2 \times 9.00 \times 10^{16} = 127.8 \times 10^{16} = 1.278 \times 10^{18}\).
- Step 5: Round to 3 significant figures: \(1.28 \times 10^{18} \, \text{J}\). Verify: 14 kg → ~10¹⁸ J is reasonable (1 kg → 9×10¹⁶ J).
REASONING: This illustrates the enormous energy density of mass. The calculation is straightforward algebra but requires careful handling of powers of 10 and significant figures. The result shows why nuclear reactions (which convert ~0.1% of mass) yield millions of times more energy than chemical reactions (which convert ~10⁻⁹ of mass).
ANSWER: \(1.28 \times 10^{18} \, \text{J}\)

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: The atomic mass of \(^{7}_{3}\text{Li}\) is 7.016003 u. Calculate its binding energy in MeV. Given: \(m_p = 1.007276 \, \text{u}\), \(m_n = 1.008665 \, \text{u}\), \(m_e = 0.000548 \, \text{u}\), and \(1 \, \text{u} = 931.5 \, \text{MeV}/c^2\).
STEPS:
- Step 1: Determine constituents: \(Z = 3\) protons, \(N = A-Z = 4\) neutrons. Nuclear mass \(M_{\text{nuc}} = \text{atomic mass} - Z m_e = 7.016003 - 3(0.000548) = 7.016003 - 0.001644 = 7.014359 \, \text{u}\).
- Step 2: Calculate total mass of free nucleons: \(3 m_p + 4 m_n = 3(1.007276) + 4(1.008665) = 3.021828 + 4.034660 = 7.056488 \, \text{u}\).
- Step 3: Mass defect \(\Delta m = (3 m_p + 4 m_n) - M_{\text{nuc}} = 7.056488 - 7.014359 = 0.042129 \, \text{u}\).
- Step 4: Binding energy \(E_b = \Delta m \times 931.5 \, \text{MeV}/c^2 \times c^2 = 0.042129 \times 931.5 = 39.24 \, \text{MeV}\).
- Step 5: Binding energy per nucleon \(E_{bn} = 39.24 / 7 = 5.61 \, \text{MeV}\). (Note: lighter nuclei have lower \(E_{bn}\)).
REASONING: The key is using nuclear mass (not atomic mass) for the bound system, while free nucleon masses are used for the initial state. Electrons are present in both initial (3 hydrogen atoms) and final (Li atom) states, so they cancel if atomic masses are used consistently. Here we subtracted electron mass to get nuclear mass, which is equivalent.
ANSWER: Binding energy = \(39.2 \, \text{MeV}\) (3 s.f.); binding energy per nucleon = \(5.61 \, \text{MeV}\).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: In the fission of \(^{235}_{92}\text{U}\) by a thermal neutron, one possible reaction is:
\(^{1}_{0}\text{n} + ^{235}_{92}\text{U} \rightarrow ^{141}_{56}\text{Ba} + ^{92}_{36}\text{Kr} + 3\,^{1}_{0}\text{n}\).
Given atomic masses: \(m(^{235}\text{U}) = 235.04393 \, \text{u}\), \(m(^{141}\text{Ba}) = 140.91441 \, \text{u}\), \(m(^{92}\text{Kr}) = 91.92616 \, \text{u}\), \(m_n = 1.008665 \, \text{u}\). Calculate the Q-value (energy released) in MeV.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Write initial total atomic mass: \(m_i = m_n + m(^{235}\text{U}) = 1.008665 + 235.04393 = 236.052595 \, \text{u}\).
- Step 2: Write final total atomic mass: \(m_f = m(^{141}\text{Ba}) + m(^{92}\text{Kr}) + 3 m_n = 140.91441 + 91.92616 + 3(1.008665) = 140.91441 + 91.92616 + 3.025995 = 235.866565 \, \text{u}\).
- Step 3: Mass defect \(\Delta m = m_i - m_f = 236.052595 - 235.866565 = 0.186030 \, \text{u}\).
- Step 4: Q-value \(Q = \Delta m \times 931.5 \, \text{MeV} = 0.186030 \times 931.5 = 173.3 \, \text{MeV}\).
- Step 5: Check: Typical fission Q ≈ 200 MeV; this channel yields slightly less because Ba-141 and Kr-92 are not the most probable fragments. The calculation uses atomic masses, so electron masses cancel automatically (92 electrons on each side).
REASONING: Using atomic masses avoids explicit electron subtraction because the total electron count is conserved (92 on left, 56+36=92 on right). The mass defect directly gives the kinetic energy shared by the fragments and neutrons. This combines mass-energy equivalence (this node) with fission physics (forward node c12_phy_cbse_13_004).
ANSWER: \(Q = 173 \, \text{MeV}\) (3 s.f.)

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A student measures the mass of a \(^{12}_6\text{C}\) atom as exactly 12.000000 u (by definition of u). She then calculates the mass of 6 protons + 6 neutrons + 6 electrons as \(6(1.007276) + 6(1.008665) + 6(0.000548) = 12.09894 \, \text{u}\). She concludes: "The mass defect of carbon-12 is 0.09894 u, so its binding energy is 92.2 MeV." Identify the flaw in her reasoning and compute the correct binding energy per nucleon for \(^{12}\text{C}\).
COMPETENCY: Interpreting physical situations using formulas; identifying hidden assumptions in definitions.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Recognize that the atomic mass unit u is *defined* as 1/12 of the mass of a \(^{12}\text{C}\) atom. Therefore, the atomic mass of \(^{12}\text{C}\) is exactly 12 u by definition, not a measured value with defect.
- Step 2: The mass defect must be calculated using the *same* mass standard for constituents. The proton, neutron, and electron masses in u are experimentally determined relative to the \(^{12}\text{C}\) standard. Their sum exceeds 12 u because the bound system has less mass.
- Step 3: Correct mass defect: \(\Delta m = [6 m_p + 6 m_n + 6 m_e] - 12.000000 = 12.09894 - 12.000000 = 0.09894 \, \text{u}\). (Her calculation of \(\Delta m\) is numerically correct, but her premise that the 12 u is a measured mass is wrong.)
- Step 4: Binding energy = \(0.09894 \times 931.5 = 92.16 \, \text{MeV}\). Per nucleon = \(92.16 / 12 = 7.68 \, \text{MeV}\).
- Step 5: The flaw: she treated the 12 u as an experimental measurement rather than the definition of the unit. The defect is real, but the reference is definitional.
ANSWER: Flaw: The atomic mass of \(^{12}\text{C}\) is exactly 12 u by definition of the unified atomic mass unit. The mass defect of 0.09894 u is correct, giving binding energy 92.2 MeV and 7.68 MeV/nucleon.
DIFFICULTY: medium

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: The Sun radiates energy at a rate (luminosity) of \(3.828 \times 10^{26} \, \text{W}\). Assuming this energy comes entirely from the fusion of hydrogen into helium via the p-p cycle (net: \(4\,^1_1\text{H} \rightarrow ^4_2\text{He} + 2e^+ + 2\nu_e + \gamma\), with 26.7 MeV released per 4 protons), calculate: (a) the mass of hydrogen converted to energy per second, (b) the total mass loss of the Sun per second, and (c) how long (in years) the Sun can sustain this if its total mass is \(1.989 \times 10^{30} \, \text{kg}\) and only 10% of its mass is available for fusion in the core. (Use \(1 \, \text{MeV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-13} \, \text{J}\), \(1 \, \text{year} = 3.154 \times 10^7 \, \text{s}\).)
COMPETENCY: Multi-step calculation combining mass-energy equivalence, nuclear reaction energetics, and astrophysical timescales; unit analysis across scales.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Energy per second (power) = \(3.828 \times 10^{26} \, \text{J/s}\). Energy per fusion reaction = \(26.7 \, \text{MeV} = 26.7 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-13} = 4.277 \times 10^{-12} \, \text{J}\).
- Step 2: Number of reactions per second = \(P / E_{\text{reaction}} = (3.828 \times 10^{26}) / (4.277 \times 10^{-12}) = 8.95 \times 10^{37} \, \text{s}^{-1}\).
- Step 3: Each reaction consumes 4 protons (mass ≈ 4 × 1.007276 u = 4.0291 u). But easier: mass converted per reaction = energy released / \(c^2\). \(\Delta m = E/c^2 = (4.277 \times 10^{-12}) / (9 \times 10^{16}) = 4.75 \times 10^{-29} \, \text{kg}\).
- Step 4: Mass loss per second = (reactions/s) × \(\Delta m\) = \(8.95 \times 10^{37} \times 4.75 \times 10^{-29} = 4.25 \times 10^9 \, \text{kg/s}\). (This is the total mass-energy loss.)
- Step 5: Available mass for fusion = 0.10 × \(1.989 \times 10^{30} = 1.989 \times 10^{29} \, \text{kg}\). Time = available mass / loss rate = \(1.989 \times 10^{29} / 4.25 \times 10^9 = 4.68 \times 10^{19} \, \text{s}\).
- Step 6: Convert to years: \(4.68 \times 10^{19} / 3.154 \times 10^7 = 1.48 \times 10^{12} \, \text{years}\). (Note: This overestimates because not all core hydrogen is fused; actual main-sequence lifetime ~10¹⁰ years. The discrepancy is due to only ~10% of the 10% being fused, and the Sun's luminosity increases over time.)
ANSWER: (a) Mass of hydrogen consumed per second ≈ \(6.2 \times 10^{11} \, \text{kg/s}\) (using 4 proton masses), (b) Mass-energy loss per second = \(4.3 \times 10^9 \, \text{kg/s}\), (c) Upper limit lifetime ≈ \(1.5 \times 10^{12} \, \text{years}\) (actual ~\(10^{10}\) years).
DIFFICULTY: hard
