## ELI5
A policy analyst reads India's COP28 submission: per capita emissions of 1.8 tonnes against a world average of 4.6, yet India ranks 7th in climate risk. Global environmental issues are planetary-scale disruptions - climate change from greenhouse gas accumulation, ozone depletion from halogenated compounds - that ignore borders and demand coordinated response. The asymmetry is the point: historical cumulative emissions, a stock created elsewhere, drive today's warming while India's flow is development-driven. Hence common but differentiated responsibilities - renewable transition, efficiency missions, climate justice - for a commons already overshot.

## DEFINITION
Global environmental issues refer to planetary-scale ecological disruptions—primarily anthropogenic climate change driven by greenhouse gas accumulation and stratospheric ozone depletion caused by halogenated compounds—that transcend national boundaries and require coordinated international mitigation and adaptation responses, with India pursuing a "common but differentiated responsibilities" approach through renewable energy transition, energy efficiency missions, and equity-based climate justice frameworks.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Define "radiative forcing" in the context of climate change and state its unit of measurement.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Radiative forcing is the change in net irradiance (difference between incoming solar radiation and outgoing longwave radiation) at the tropopause due to a change in an external driver (e.g., CO2 concentration), measured in watts per square metre (W/m²). A wrong answer (e.g., "temperature rise" or "greenhouse gas concentration") reveals confusion between the *driver* (forcing), the *response* (temperature), and the *state variable* (concentration)—a critical distinction for understanding why mitigation targets are framed in concentration (ppm) or forcing (W/m²) terms, not temperature directly.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why the Montreal Protocol (1987) succeeded in phasing out CFCs while the Kyoto Protocol (1997) struggled to achieve comparable global emission reductions, using the concepts of "availability of substitutes" and "distributional equity."

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Montreal succeeded because: (1) technically feasible, cost-effective substitutes (HFCs, HCFCs) existed for CFCs in refrigeration/foams, lowering compliance costs; (2) the Multilateral Fund provided financial/technical transfer to developing countries, addressing equity; (3) industry supported transition due to patent opportunities. Kyoto struggled because: (1) no ready substitutes for fossil fuels at scale in 1997; (2) no equivalent financial mechanism; (3) Annex I/non-Annex I divide created free-rider incentives. A wrong answer focusing only on "US non-ratification" misses the structural political economy differences—substitute availability and equity mechanisms are the deeper explanatory variables.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
India's 2030 NDC targets: (a) reduce emission intensity of GDP by 45% from 2005 levels, (b) achieve 50% cumulative electric power installed capacity from non-fossil sources. If India's 2005 emission intensity was 0.35 kg CO2/₹ GDP (2011-12 prices) and 2023 GDP is ₹270 lakh crore with emissions of 2.9 Gt CO2, calculate the 2023 emission intensity and the maximum allowable 2030 emissions if GDP grows at 6.5% annually. Show all steps.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: 2023 emission intensity = 2.9 Gt CO2 / ₹270 lakh crore = 2.9×10⁹ tonnes / 270×10¹² ₹ = 0.01074 kg CO2/₹ = 10.74 g CO2/₹.  
Step 2: 2005 intensity = 0.35 kg CO2/₹ = 350 g CO2/₹.  
Step 3: 45% reduction target → 2030 target intensity = 350 × (1 - 0.45) = 192.5 g CO2/₹.  
Step 4: 2030 GDP = 270 × (1.065)^7 = 270 × 1.554 = ₹419.6 lakh crore.  
Step 5: Max 2030 emissions = 192.5 g/₹ × 419.6×10¹² ₹ = 80.77×10¹² g = 80.77 Gt CO2.  
(Note: This exceeds current global emissions—revealing the intensity target alone permits absolute emission growth. A wrong answer calculating 2.9×0.55=1.6 Gt confuses intensity reduction with absolute reduction, a common error in interpreting NDCs.)

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Analyze the following statement: "India's International Solar Alliance (ISA) is primarily a climate mitigation initiative." Identify two dimensions where ISA also serves energy access and energy security objectives, with specific mechanisms.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The statement is incomplete. ISA serves three co-benefits:  
(1) Energy access: By aggregating demand for solar pumps, mini-grids, and rooftop across 120+ member countries (mostly tropical developing nations), ISA drives down technology costs via economies of scale, making decentralized solar affordable for unelectrified populations (e.g., PM-KUSUM in India).  
(2) Energy security: Solar reduces import dependence on oil/gas—India imports 85% of crude oil; every GW of solar displaces ~1.5 MMT oil equivalent annually. ISA's "One Sun One World One Grid" (OSOWOG) vision enables cross-border solar trade, diversifying supply.  
A wrong answer treating ISA as only mitigation misses the development-climate nexus central to India's CBDR position—climate action must deliver development dividends.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate the ethical and economic justification for India's "net-zero by 2070" pledge versus the IPCC's "global net-zero by 2050" pathway, using the carbon budget concept and historical responsibility data (USA: 25%, EU+UK: 22%, China: 13%, India: 3% of cumulative 1850-2019 CO2 emissions).

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
India's 2070 target is ethically defensible and economically rational because:  
(1) Carbon budget equity: Remaining 1.5°C budget (~400 Gt CO2 from 2020) allocated by population gives India ~70 Gt (17% share); by historical responsibility, India is a net creditor. Developed nations' 2050 net-zero still exceeds their fair share if consumption-based accounting used.  
(2) Development space: India's per capita income ($2,400) is 1/30th of USA; premature net-zero locks in energy poverty. The 20-year lag allows green industrialization (green hydrogen, batteries) to mature.  
(3) Credibility: India's 2030 targets are on track (emission intensity down 33% from 2005; 44% non-fossil capacity), unlike many 2050 pledges lacking near-term policy.  
A wrong answer calling 2070 "unambitious" ignores the carbon budget arithmetic and confounds *flow* targets with *stock* justice.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simplified "climate damage function" for Indian agriculture linking temperature anomaly (ΔT in °C) to wheat yield loss (% from baseline), incorporating: (a) a threshold effect (no loss below 1°C), (b) accelerating losses above 2°C, (c) CO2 fertilization offset (5% gain per 100 ppm CO2 rise). Write the piecewise function and calculate yield change for ΔT=2.5°C, CO2=450 ppm (baseline 280 ppm).

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Function:  
Yield loss % = 0 for ΔT ≤ 1  
= 3×(ΔT - 1) for 1 < ΔT ≤ 2  
= 3 + 5×(ΔT - 2)² for ΔT > 2  
CO2 fertilization gain = 5 × ((450-280)/100) = 5 × 1.7 = 8.5%  

For ΔT=2.5°C:  
Thermal loss = 3 + 5×(0.5)² = 3 + 1.25 = 4.25%  
Net change = -4.25% + 8.5% = +4.25% (gain)  

For ΔT=3.5°C:  
Thermal loss = 3 + 5×(1.5)² = 3 + 11.25 = 14.25%  
Net change = -14.25% + 8.5% = -5.75% (loss)  

A poor answer using linear function misses the non-linearity (tipping points) central to climate economics—Stern Review and IPCC emphasize convex damage curves. The CO2 fertilization offset is temporary (nutrient limitations) and declines at higher ΔT, a nuance needed for dynamic assessment.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students conflate global warming and ozone depletion as the same problem ("ozone hole causes global warming"). The exact error: they believe CFCs cause warming *because* they deplete ozone, and that the Montreal Protocol solved climate change. In reality: (1) CFCs are potent GHGs (10,000x CO2) *and* ozone depleters—two distinct mechanisms; (2) Montreal Protocol's climate co-benefit came from phasing out CFCs (GHGs), not from ozone recovery; (3) HFC substitutes solved ozone but created a new warming problem (Kigali Amendment addresses this). This confusion leads to wrong policy analogies—e.g., "we fixed ozone, we can fix climate the same way"—ignoring that fossil fuel substitution is orders of magnitude harder than refrigerant substitution.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: India's 2005 emission intensity was 0.35 kg CO2/₹ (2011-12 series). In 2022-23, real GDP was ₹160.7 lakh crore and total GHG emissions (excluding LULUCF) were 2.9 Gt CO2e. The 2030 NDC target is 45% reduction in emission intensity from 2005 level. Assuming 6.5% annual real GDP growth, calculate: (a) 2022-23 emission intensity, (b) whether India is on track for the 2030 target, (c) the implied annual rate of intensity improvement needed from 2023-2030.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Compute 2022-23 emission intensity = Total emissions / Real GDP = 2.9 Gt CO2e / ₹160.7 lakh crore. Convert units: 2.9×10⁹ tonnes / 160.7×10¹² ₹ = 0.01804 kg CO2/₹ = 18.04 g CO2/₹.
- Step 2: Compute 2005 base intensity in same units: 0.35 kg CO2/₹ = 350 g CO2/₹. Target 2030 intensity = 350 × (1 - 0.45) = 192.5 g CO2/₹.
- Step 3: Compare current (18.04 g) vs target (192.5 g) — current intensity is ALREADY far below target. This suggests either: (i) base year data mismatch (2005 series vs 2011-12 series), (ii) GDP rebasing effect, or (iii) significant decoupling already achieved. Check: 2005 GDP (2004-05 series) was ₹36.9 lakh crore; 2005 emissions ~1.3 Gt → intensity ~35 g/₹. The 350 g figure likely uses older GDP series.
- Step 4: Recalculate with consistent series. Assume 2005 intensity = 35 g/₹ (more plausible). Target = 35 × 0.55 = 19.25 g/₹. Current = 18.04 g/₹. India has ALREADY exceeded the 2030 intensity target.
- Step 5: Project 2030 GDP = 160.7 × (1.065)^7 = 160.7 × 1.554 = ₹249.7 lakh crore. At current intensity (18.04 g), 2030 emissions = 4.5 Gt. At target intensity (19.25 g), 2030 emissions = 4.8 Gt. India can afford slight intensity increase and still meet target.
- Step 6: Required annual intensity improvement rate from 2023-2030: (18.04/19.25)^(1/7) - 1 = -0.9% per year (i.e., intensity can worsen 0.9%/year and still meet target). This reveals the intensity target's weakness: it permits absolute emission growth.

REASONING: Emission intensity targets decouple mitigation from absolute emission reductions. A growing economy can meet intensity targets while emissions rise. This is why climate economists prefer carbon budgets (absolute cumulative limits). The calculation shows India's 2030 intensity target is not ambitious relative to achieved decoupling — a critical insight for evaluating NDC adequacy.

ANSWER: (a) 18.04 g CO2/₹ (using 2011-12 series); (b) Already exceeded target if 2005 base = 35 g/₹; (c) -0.9%/year (intensity can deteriorate slightly).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: The Montreal Protocol phased out CFCs with a Multilateral Fund (MLF) providing $3.9 billion (1991-2020) to Article 5 (developing) countries. India received $280 million for 350 projects, phasing out 25,000 ODP tonnes of CFCs. Calculate: (a) Cost-effectiveness of India's phase-out ($/ODP tonne), (b) Global average MLF cost-effectiveness, (c) If HFC-134a (GWP=1430) replaced CFC-12 (GWP=10,900) in 50% of applications, calculate the avoided CO2e emissions from India's phase-out over 20 years, assuming 1 ODP tonne CFC-12 ≈ 1 tonne mass.

STEPS:
- Step 1: India's cost-effectiveness = $280 million / 25,000 ODP tonnes = $11,200 per ODP tonne.
- Step 2: Global MLF cost-effectiveness = $3.9 billion / (total ODP phased out). Total ODP phased out by MLF ~470,000 ODP tonnes (UNEP data). Global average = $3.9B / 470,000 = $8,298/ODP tonne. India's cost was ~35% higher — likely due to larger industrial base and technology choices.
- Step 3: Avoided emissions calculation. CFC-12 phased out = 50% of 25,000 = 12,500 tonnes. HFC-134a replacement = 12,500 tonnes (1:1 mass replacement typical).
- Step 4: Annual CO2e from CFC-12 if not phased out = 12,500 t × 10,900 = 136.25 Mt CO2e. Annual CO2e from HFC-134a = 12,500 t × 1,430 = 17.88 Mt CO2e.
- Step 5: Annual avoided CO2e = 136.25 - 17.88 = 118.37 Mt CO2e. Over 20 years (atmospheric lifetime ~100 years for CFC-12, ~14 years for HFC-134a; simplified 20-year horizon): Total avoided = 118.37 × 20 = 2,367 Mt CO2e = 2.37 Gt CO2e.
- Step 6: Cost per tonne CO2e avoided = $280M / 2.37 Gt = $0.12/t CO2e — exceptionally low, showing ozone treaty's massive climate co-benefit. Compare to current carbon prices ($10-100/t CO2e).

REASONING: The Montreal Protocol's climate co-benefit is often overlooked. By phasing out high-GWP CFCs, it avoided ~135 Gt CO2e globally by 2010 (Velders et al. 2007) — more than Kyoto's first commitment period target. The Kigali Amendment (2016) now targets HFCs directly. This example demonstrates how ozone and climate regimes intersect, and why "co-benefits" matter in policy design.

ANSWER: (a) $11,200/ODP tonne; (b) $8,298/ODP tonne; (c) 2.37 Gt CO2e avoided over 20 years at $0.12/t CO2e.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: India's National Solar Mission (NSM) targets 280 GW solar by 2030 (part of 500 GW non-fossil). Current installed solar (March 2024): 82 GW. Assume: (a) Solar capacity factor = 19%, (b) Grid emission factor = 0.71 t CO2/MWh (Indian grid average), (c) Solar LCOE = ₹2.50/kWh, coal LCOE = ₹4.50/kWh, (d) Annual capacity addition follows arithmetic progression: 15 GW in 2025, increasing by 3 GW/year. Calculate: (1) Annual solar generation at 280 GW, (2) Annual CO2 avoidance vs coal, (3) Annual cost savings vs coal, (4) Year when cumulative additions reach 280 GW, (5) Total investment needed at ₹5 Cr/MW.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Annual generation at 280 GW = 280 GW × 8760 h × 0.19 = 465,672 GWh = 465.7 TWh.
- Step 2: CO2 avoidance = 465.7 TWh × 0.71 t CO2/MWh = 465,700,000 MWh × 0.71 = 330.6 Mt CO2/year.
- Step 3: Cost savings per MWh = ₹4.50 - ₹2.50 = ₹2.00/kWh = ₹2000/MWh. Annual savings = 465.7 TWh × ₹2000/MWh = ₹931,400 million = ₹93,140 Cr/year.
- Step 4: AP: a=15, d=3. Sum S_n = n/2 [2a + (n-1)d] = 280 - 82 = 198 GW to add. Solve n/2 [30 + 3(n-1)] = 198 → n/2 (3n + 27) = 198 → 3n² + 27n - 396 = 0 → n² + 9n - 132 = 0 → n = [-9 + √(81+528)]/2 = [-9 + √609]/2 = [-9 + 24.68]/2 = 7.84 → 8 years. So by 2025+8 = 2033 (slight overshoot). Check: S_8 = 4[30+21] = 204 GW. Target met in 2032-33.
- Step 5: Total new capacity = 198 GW (to reach 280 from 82). Investment = 198,000 MW × ₹5 Cr/MW = ₹990,000 Cr = ₹9.9 lakh crore. At ₹93,140 Cr/year savings, payback = 10.6 years (ignoring discounting, O&M, grid integration costs).
- Step 6: Sensitivity: If capacity factor drops to 15% (dust, curtailment), generation = 368 TWh, avoidance = 261 Mt, savings = ₹73,600 Cr, payback = 13.4 years. If grid emission factor falls to 0.5 (grid greening), avoidance = 184 Mt.

REASONING: This integrates arithmetic progression (Class 9/10 math), capacity factor engineering, emission factor accounting, and LCOE economics — exactly the multi-disciplinary analysis needed for energy transition planning. The AP assumption for capacity addition reflects real policy: ramp-up phase (supply chains, land, grid) then acceleration. The payback calculation shows solar is economically viable without carbon pricing, but grid integration costs (storage, balancing) are the hidden variable.

ANSWER: (1) 465.7 TWh/year; (2) 330.6 Mt CO2/year; (3) ₹93,140 Cr/year; (4) 2032-33 (8 years from 2025); (5) ₹9.9 lakh crore investment, 10.6-year simple payback.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2

Reframe: Compare on *energy* basis with SCC, or on *capacity* basis with capacity credit. Use energy basis for LCOE.
- Step 3: Coal LCOE (energy): Capital recovery factor (CRF) = 0.08/(1-1.08^-40) = 0.0839. Annualized capex = 2000 × 7 × 0.0839 = ₹1,174.6 Cr/yr. O&M = 2000 × 0.5 = ₹1,000 Cr/yr. Fuel = 14.89 TWh × ₹2.50/kWh = ₹37,225 Cr/yr. SCC = 14.89 TWh × 0.9 t/MWh × ₹4,150/t = ₹55,570 Cr/yr. Total annual cost = ₹94,969.6 Cr. LCOE = ₹94,969.6 Cr / 14.89 TWh = ₹6.38/kWh.
- Step 4: Solar+battery LCOE (energy): Solar capex = 2000 × 5 = ₹10,000 Cr. Battery capex = 2000 × 4 = ₹8,000 Cr. Total = ₹18,000 Cr. CRF (25 yr) = 0.08/(1-1.08^-25) = 0.0937. Annualized capex = 18,000 × 0.0937 = ₹1,686.6 Cr/yr. O&M = 2000 × 0.3 = ₹600 Cr/yr. Fuel = 0. SCC = 0. Total annual cost = ₹2,286.6 Cr. Generation = 6.25 TWh. LCOE = ₹2,286.6 Cr / 6.25 TWh = ₹3.66/kWh.
- Step 5: Solar+battery is cheaper by ₹2.72/kWh INCLUDING social cost of carbon. Even without SCC: Coal LCOE = (1,174.6+1,000+37,225)/14.89 = ₹2.64/kWh. Solar+battery without SCC = ₹3.66/kWh. *But* coal plant runs 85% PLF; solar+battery delivers only 6.25/14.89 = 42% equivalent energy. For fair capacity comparison, need 4× solar+battery to match coal energy → capex 4×, LCOE ~₹3.66/kWh still (linear scaling). Coal wins on pure LCOE without SCC, but loses with SCC.
- Step 6: Key insight: SCC internalizes the externality. At $50/t CO2, coal's externality cost (₹3.73/kWh) exceeds its private LCOE (₹2.64/kWh). This is the economic case for carbon pricing.

ANSWER: With SCC: Solar+battery LCOE = ₹3.66/kWh, Coal LCOE = ₹6.38/kWh. Solar+battery cheaper by ₹2.72/kWh. Without SCC: Coal ₹2.64/kWh, Solar+battery ₹3.66/kWh. Coal cheaper by ₹1.02/kWh. The crossover demonstrates why carbon pricing is essential for efficient decarbonization.
