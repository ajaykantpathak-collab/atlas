## ELI5
A family with one month's salary and a hundred needs asks three questions before shopping: what do we buy, how do we buy it (online, cash, instalments), and who gets how much of the basket. Every economy - household to nation - asks the same trio: What to produce, How to produce, and For whom to produce. Scarcity makes the questions unavoidable; the price mechanism answers them in a market economy, central authority in a planned one, and a mix of both in a mixed economy like India.

## DEFINITION
The central problems of an economy — **What to produce**, **How to produce**, and **For whom to produce** — arise because resources are scarce relative to unlimited human wants. These three interdependent questions define the allocation of scarce resources (land, labour, capital, entrepreneurship) among competing uses: **What** determines the combination and quantities of goods/services; **How** chooses the production technique (labour-intensive vs capital-intensive); **For whom** decides the distribution of output among households based on factor incomes (wages, rent, interest, profit). In a market economy, these problems are resolved through the price mechanism; in a planned economy, by central authority; in a mixed economy like India, by a combination of both.

## WALKTHROUGH

She faces **What to produce** — which crop mix maximises her income given water, soil, and market prices? **How to produce** — should she hire more labourers or rent a tractor? **For whom to produce** — will she sell to the local mandi, a contract-farming company, or an export aggregator? These are the same questions India's economy answers at aggregate level every day. ## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Define the three central problems of an economy and state which scarce resource each problem primarily concerns.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The three central problems are: (1) **What to produce** — concerns allocation of **land and capital** among alternative goods; (2) **How to produce** — concerns choice of technique, i.e., combination of **labour and capital**; (3) **For whom to produce** — concerns distribution of output, determined by ownership of **factors of production** (land → rent, labour → wages, capital → interest, entrepreneurship → profit). A wrong answer that lists only "money" or "resources" without linking each problem to specific factors reveals the student has memorised the names but not the underlying factor-income connection.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why does the problem "For whom to produce" not disappear in a market economy even though prices allocate goods? Explain using the concept of factor ownership.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
In a market economy, goods go to those who can pay the market price. But the ability to pay depends on **factor incomes** (wages, rent, interest, profit) which are determined by the **ownership of scarce factors**. Households with more land/capital/skills earn higher factor incomes and thus command more output. The market allocates goods to *effective demand* (backed by purchasing power), not to *need*. A student who says "market solves it because prices ration goods" misses that the distribution of purchasing power itself is a result of the **For whom** problem — it is not automatically fair or efficient.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A village panchayat has 100 hectares of irrigated land. It can allocate the land to either wheat (yield 4 tonnes/hectare, requires 2 labourers/hectare) or cotton (yield 2 tonnes/hectare, requires 5 labourers/hectare). The village has 300 labourers available. If the panchayat wants to maximise total output in tonnes, how many hectares should be allocated to each crop? Show the steps.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Let \(x\) = hectares of wheat, \(y\) = hectares of cotton. Constraints: \(x + y = 100\) (land), \(2x + 5y \le 300\) (labour). Objective: maximise \(Z = 4x + 2y\). Substitute \(x = 100 - y\): labour constraint → \(2(100-y) + 5y \le 300\) → \(200 + 3y \le 300\) → \(y \le 33.33\). So max integer \(y = 33\), \(x = 67\). Output = \(4(67) + 2(33) = 268 + 66 = 334\) tonnes. If student ignores labour constraint and puts all land to wheat (400 tonnes), they violate the labour limit. If they ignore land constraint, they mis-specify the problem. This tests ability to translate **What** and **How** into a constrained optimisation.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare how a pure market economy, a pure planned economy, and India's mixed economy resolve the "How to produce" problem. Give one real Indian example for each.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
**Market economy**: Firms choose the cost-minimising technique based on relative factor prices (wage/rental rate). Example: A textile firm in Tamil Nadu adopts automated looms because capital is cheap relative to skilled labour. **Planned economy**: Central planning bureau assigns techniques based on social priorities (e.g., employment generation). Example: Soviet-style five-year plans mandating labour-intensive methods in agriculture. **Mixed economy (India)**: Government influences technique via subsidies (e.g., capital subsidy for MSMEs under PMEGP), labour laws (mandating certain labour standards), and public sector choices (e.g., Indian Railways using labour-intensive track maintenance). A student who says "India is market so only prices decide" ignores the role of MGNREGA (labour-intensive by design) or PLI schemes (capital-intensive by incentive).

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate the statement: "In a market economy, the 'For whom to produce' problem is solved efficiently because goods go to those who value them most." Use the concepts of willingness to pay vs ability to pay, and give an Indian context example.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement confuses **willingness to pay** (marginal utility) with **ability to pay** (income). A poor farmer may value a life-saving drug more than a rich urbanite (higher marginal utility), but cannot pay the market price. Market allocates to *effective demand* (ability to pay), not *need*. Example: During COVID-19, oxygen concentrators were sold at high prices — they went to hospitals/wealthy families, not necessarily to the most critical patients in rural areas. Efficiency in economics (Pareto efficiency) does not guarantee equity. A student who agrees without qualification misses the normative dimension: markets solve *allocation* efficiently only under perfect competition and initial endowment fairness, which rarely hold.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple production plan for a school canteen that must decide: (a) What snacks to offer (samosas, sandwiches, fruit), (b) How to prepare them (in-house cooking vs outsourcing), (c) For whom (students, staff, or both). List the scarce resources, the decision criteria for each problem, and one trade-off for each.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
**Scarce resources**: Kitchen space, cooking staff time, budget (capital), raw materials, serving time.  
(a) **What**: Criteria — student demand (survey), profit margin, nutrition guidelines. Trade-off: Samosas high demand but low nutrition; fruit high nutrition but low demand/perishable.  
(b) **How**: Criteria — cost per unit, quality control, hygiene standards. Trade-off: In-house gives control but uses staff time; outsourcing saves labour but raises cost and reduces quality oversight.  
(c) **For whom**: Criteria — affordability (subsidised for students), inclusivity (staff pay full price). Trade-off: Subsidising students reduces revenue; cross-subsidising from staff may cause resentment.  
A poor answer lists only "money" as resource or ignores trade-offs. Good answer shows each problem involves a distinct choice with opportunity cost.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think the three central problems are **sequential** — first decide **What**, then **How**, then **For whom**. In reality, they are **simultaneous and interdependent**. Choosing **What** (e.g., capital-intensive steel) determines **How** (blast furnace vs electric arc) and influences **For whom** (capital owners earn more profit, labour gets less wage share). Another error: "Market economy solves all three perfectly." Markets only coordinate through prices; they do not guarantee *equitable* distribution (For whom) or *socially optimal* technique (How) if externalities exist (e.g., pollution from capital-intensive method). Students also confuse "For whom to produce" with "who consumes" — it is about **factor income distribution**, not final consumption allocation alone.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A small-scale textile unit in Surat has 50 workers and 20 power looms. It can produce either cotton fabric (requires 2 workers + 1 loom per 100 metres) or blended fabric (requires 1 worker + 2 looms per 100 metres). The unit wants to maximise total output in metres. How many 100-metre units of each fabric should it produce? Identify which central problem(s) this illustrates and the trade-off involved.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the scarce resources — labour (50 workers) and capital/looms (20 looms). These are the constraints.
- Step 2: Define decision variables — let x = units of cotton fabric (100m each), y = units of blended fabric (100m each).
- Step 3: Write resource constraints — Labour: 2x + 1y ≤ 50; Looms: 1x + 2y ≤ 20. Non-negativity: x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0.
- Step 4: Objective — maximise total output Z = x + y (since each unit is 100 metres).
- Step 5: Solve graphically or by corner points. Feasible region corners: (0,0), (20,0), (0,10), intersection of 2x+y=50 and x+2y=20 → solving: multiply second by 2: 2x+4y=40, subtract first: 3y=-10 → no positive intersection. So binding constraints are looms (x+2y=20) and labour (2x+y=50). Check corners: (20,0) → Z=20; (0,10) → Z=10; (0,0) → Z=0. Maximum at (20,0): produce 20 units of cotton fabric (2000 metres), 0 blended.
- Step 6: Interpret economically — This illustrates **What to produce** (choice between two goods) and **How to produce** (technique: cotton uses more labour per loom, blended uses more looms per worker). The trade-off: using looms for cotton gives higher total output because looms are the scarcer resource (only 20 vs 50 workers). "For whom" is implicit: workers get wages, loom owners get rental income.

REASONING: The problem translates the abstract central problems into a constrained optimisation. The firm (entrepreneur) decides **What** (product mix) and **How** (factor proportions) to maximise output given factor endowments. The solution shows that the scarcer factor (looms) determines the optimal product choice — a key insight for PPF later.

ANSWER: Produce 20 units (2000 metres) of cotton fabric, 0 units of blended fabric. Maximum output = 2000 metres. Illustrates "What" and "How" simultaneously; trade-off is between labour-intensive and capital-intensive techniques.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: The Government of India allocates ₹1,00,000 crore for rural development. It can spend on (A) MGNREGA (labour-intensive, generates 50 person-days employment per crore, builds water conservation assets) or (B) PM-KISAN (direct income support, transfers ₹6,000/year to each farmer, covers 1 crore farmers per ₹60,000 crore). If the goal is to maximise employment generation, how should the budget be split? Which central problem does this primarily address? What is the opportunity cost of choosing one over the other?

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the decision-maker (Government) and the goal (maximise employment). This is a "For whom" and "What" problem at macro level — distribution of public resources and choice of public goods.
- Step 2: Define variables — let x = crore rupees on MGNREGA, y = crore rupees on PM-KISAN. Budget constraint: x + y = 1,00,000.
- Step 3: Employment generated — MGNREGA: 50 person-days/crore → 50x person-days. PM-KISAN: direct transfer, negligible direct employment (assume 0 for this calculation).
- Step 4: Objective — maximise E = 50x subject to x + y = 1,00,000, x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0.
- Step 5: Solution — to maximise E, set x = 1,00,000, y = 0. All budget to MGNREGA → 50 × 1,00,000 = 50 lakh person-days employment.
- Step 6: Opportunity cost — choosing MGNREGA fully means giving up income support to 1 crore farmers (since ₹1,00,000 crore / ₹60,000 crore per crore farmers ≈ 1.67 crore farmers could have been covered). This is the **What** trade-off: employment-generating assets vs direct income transfers. Also illustrates **For whom** — MGNREGA targets landless labourers (wage income), PM-KISAN targets land-owning farmers (transfer income).

REASONING: Government budget allocation is a classic "What" (which public goods) and "For whom" (which households benefit) problem. The opportunity cost is measured in the forgone benefit of the next best alternative. Here, the objective (employment) makes MGNREGA dominant, but a different objective (poverty reduction, equity) might change the choice.

ANSWER: Allocate entire ₹1,00,000 crore to MGNREGA → 50 lakh person-days employment. Opportunity cost: income support to ~1.67 crore farmers forgone. Primarily addresses "What" (public good choice) and "For whom" (beneficiary group).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: An Indian farmer in Punjab has 10 hectares. He can grow wheat (yield 5 tonnes/ha, price ₹2,200/tonne, variable cost ₹30,000/ha) or rice (yield 6 tonnes/ha, price ₹2,000/tonne, variable cost ₹35,000/ha). Wheat requires 2 irrigations/ha, rice requires 15 irrigations/ha. Water is scarce: only 100 irrigations available. Labour is abundant. (a) Which crop mix maximises profit? (b) How does the water constraint change the "How" decision? (c) If the government imposes a minimum support price (MSP) for wheat at ₹2,400/tonne, how does the optimal mix change? Show all calculations.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate profit per hectare for each crop at market prices.  
  Wheat: Revenue = 5 × 2,200 = ₹11,000; Variable cost = ₹30,000 → Wait, revenue < cost? That can't be right. Let's re-read: yield 5 tonnes/ha, price ₹2,200/tonne → revenue = ₹11,000/ha. Variable cost ₹30,000/ha → loss of ₹19,000/ha. That seems unrealistic. Maybe price is ₹22,000/tonne? No, typical wheat MSP ~₹2,200/quintal = ₹22,000/tonne. Let's assume price is per quintal (100 kg). 5 tonnes = 50 quintals. Price ₹2,200/quintal → revenue = 50 × 2,200 = ₹1,10,000/ha. Cost ₹30,000 → profit ₹80,000/ha. Rice: 6 tonnes = 60 quintals, price ₹2,000/quintal → revenue ₹1,20,000; cost ₹35,000 → profit ₹85,000/ha. We'll use per quintal pricing as standard in India.
- Step 2: Define variables — x = hectares wheat, y = hectares rice. Land: x + y ≤ 10. Water: 2x + 15y ≤ 100.
- Step 3: Profit function (market prices): P = 80,000x + 85,000y. Maximise P subject to constraints.
- Step 4: Find corner points of feasible region.  
  (0,0): P=0  
  (10,0): land binding, water 20 ≤ 100 ok → P = 8,00,000  
  (0, 6.67): water binding (15y=100 → y=6.67), land ok → P = 85,000 × 6.67 ≈ 5,66,950  
  Intersection: x + y = 10 and 2x + 15y = 100 → substitute x=10-y: 2(10-y)+15y=100 → 20+13y=100 → y=80/13≈6.15, x=3.85. P = 80,000(3.85) + 85,000(6.15) = 3,08,000 + 5,22,750 = 8,30,750.
- Step 5: Maximum profit at (3.85, 6.15) ≈ 3.85 ha wheat, 6.15 ha rice. Profit ≈ ₹8,30,750. This is the **What** and **How** decision: water scarcity forces a mix; rice is more water-intensive (15 vs 2 irrigations) but slightly more profitable per hectare. The "How" is the irrigation technique (flood irrigation for rice vs limited for wheat).
- Step 6: With MSP for wheat at ₹2,400/quintal → wheat revenue = 50 × 2,400 = ₹1,20,000/ha; profit = ₹90,000/ha. New profit function: P' = 90,000x + 85,000y. Re-evaluate corners:  
  (10,0): P' = 9,00,000  
  (0,6.67): P' = 5,66,950  
  (3.85,6.15): P' = 90,000(3.85) + 85,000(6.15) = 3,46,500 + 5,22,750 = 8,69,250.  
  Now (10,0) gives highest profit. Optimal mix shifts to 10 ha wheat, 0 ha rice.
- Step 7: Policy impact — MSP changes relative profitability, altering **What** (crop choice) and **How** (water use per hectare drops from 2(3.85)+15(6.15)=99.95 to 2(10)=20 irrigations). "For whom" — farmer gains higher profit; government incurs procurement cost; consumers may face higher prices.

REASONING: This multi-step problem combines all three central problems with real Indian data (MSP, irrigation constraints, per quintal pricing). It shows how a policy (MSP) changes the market signals (prices) that guide **What** and **How**, and has distributional consequences (**For whom**). The water constraint makes "How" (irrigation intensity) a binding factor.

ANSWER: (a) At market prices: 3.85 ha wheat, 6.15 ha rice → profit ₹8.31 lakh. (b) Water constraint forces substitution away from water-intensive rice despite its higher per-hectare profit. (c) With wheat MSP ₹2,400/quintal: 10 ha wheat, 0 ha rice → profit ₹9 lakh. Water use falls from ~100 to 20 irrigations.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A coastal village in Kerala has 200 fishing households. Each household owns a boat (capital) and provides family labour. They can either do traditional fishing (low capital, high labour, catch 50 kg/day, sells at ₹200/kg) or deep-sea fishing (requires ₹5 lakh loan for engine, 2 hired labourers, catch 200 kg/day, sells at ₹180/kg). The village has a credit cooperative offering loans at 8% interest.  
(a) Which central problem does the choice between traditional and deep-sea fishing represent?  
(b) If a household takes the loan for deep-sea fishing, how does the "For whom" problem change for that household and for the hired labourers?  
(c) Suppose the government introduces a subsidy of 50% on engine cost. How does this affect the "How" decision at the village level? Explain using factor prices.

COMPETENCY: Analyzing market relationships and policy impacts on production decisions

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the choice — technique of production (traditional vs deep-sea) → this is **How to produce**.
- Step 2: For whom — traditional: household keeps all profit (owner-labourer). Deep-sea: household becomes capitalist (earns profit after paying wage to hired labour and interest on loan). Hired labourers get wage income. Distribution shifts from self-employed to wage labour + profit.
- Step 3: Subsidy reduces effective capital cost (engine price falls 50%). This lowers the rental price of capital relative to wage. More households will find deep-sea fishing profitable → village shifts to capital-intensive technique. This is a change in **How** driven by policy-altered factor prices.

ANSWER: (a) **How to produce** — choice between labour-intensive traditional and capital-intensive deep-sea technique. (b) Traditional: household is both labourer and capitalist (earns mixed income). Deep-sea: household becomes pure capitalist (profit after wage + interest); hired labourers get wages. Income distribution shifts from household to hired workers. (c) Subsidy lowers cost of capital → capital becomes relatively cheaper than labour → more households adopt deep-sea (capital-intensive) technique. The "How" decision responds to changed factor price ratio.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: During the COVID-19 lockdown, India's urban informal sector (street vendors, construction workers, domestic help) lost incomes while the formal sector (IT, finance, government) largely continued. The government announced a ₹20 lakh crore stimulus (Atmanirbhar Bharat) including free food grains (PMGKAY), credit guarantees for MSMEs, and MGNREGA enhancement.  
(a) Which central problem does the *design* of the stimulus package primarily address? Justify.  
(b) The free food grain programme (PMGKAY) distributes 5 kg/person/month to 80 crore beneficiaries. If the economic cost of wheat to FCI is ₹25/kg and rice ₹35/kg, and the ratio is 60:40 wheat:rice, calculate the monthly fiscal cost.  
(c) Critics argue the stimulus was "supply-side heavy" (credit guarantees) rather than "demand-side" (cash transfers). Using the three central problems, explain why this distinction matters for "For whom" and "What".

COMPETENCY: Interpreting economic data and evaluating policy impacts

STEPS:
- Step 1: Stimulus design chooses **What** (which goods/services to provide: food grains, credit, employment) and **For whom** (target beneficiaries: poor households, MSMEs, rural workers). It is a government answer to the central problems.
- Step 2: Monthly cost calculation. Total beneficiaries = 80 crore. Per person = 5 kg. Total grain = 400 crore kg = 40 lakh tonnes. Wheat: 60% = 24 lakh tonnes = 2400 crore kg? Wait: 400 crore kg × 0.6 = 240 crore kg wheat. Rice: 160 crore kg. Cost: Wheat 240 crore kg × ₹25/kg = ₹6,000 crore. Rice 160 crore kg × ₹35/kg = ₹5,600 crore. Total = ₹11,600 crore/month.
- Step 3: Supply-side (credit) vs demand-side (cash/food). Credit guarantees help firms (**What** to produce, **How** to finance) but require viable demand. Cash/food transfers boost **For whom** (poor households' purchasing power) which then signals **What** firms should produce (essential goods). If poor have no money, firms won't produce for them even with credit. So demand-side directly addresses **For whom** → effective demand → **What**.

ANSWER: (a) Primarily **What** (composition of public support: food, credit, employment) and **For whom** (targeting poor, MSMEs, rural workers). (b) Monthly fiscal cost = ₹11,600 crore. (c) Supply-side measures (credit) assume firms will produce and hire if financed — but if poor households lack purchasing power (**For whom** unsolved), there is no effective demand for **What** to produce. Demand-side transfers (cash/food) directly raise ability to pay of poor, creating market signals for firms to produce essential goods. Thus, addressing **For whom** is prerequisite for **What** to respond.
