## ELI5
A photograph and a home video: the photograph freezes one instant, the video records a whole afternoon - both true, neither interchangeable. Stock variables are photographs: capital, money supply, wealth - measured at a point in time, no calendar attached. Flow variables are videos: income, investment, consumption - measured per period, meaningless without one. The bank passbook shows both: the balance on the thirty-first is a stock; the salary credited in April is a flow. And every flow quietly edits the stock it runs into.

## DEFINITION
A stock variable is an economic quantity measured at a specific point in time (e.g., capital stock, money supply, wealth on 31st March), while a flow variable is measured over a period of time (e.g., income, investment, consumption during 2023-24). Stocks have no time dimension; flows have a time dimension (per year, per quarter).

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you check your bank balance on 31st March — that ₹50,000 is a **stock** (snapshot). Now look at your passbook: ₹10,000 salary credited in April, ₹3,000 rent paid in May — these are **flows** (streams over time). Start by asking: "Is this measured *at a moment* or *over a period*?" Connect to Class 11 accounting (c11_acc_cbse_01_008: assets/liabilities are stocks; revenue/expense are flows). The key principle: every flow changes a stock; every stock is the accumulation of past flows.

Takeaway first: the test question is always 'measured when?' - a moment means stock, a span means flow - and the accounting bridge is that the change in a stock equals the net flow over the interval. Exam moves: classify questions on any given macro variable (debt is stock, deficit is flow), explain questions via the water-tank analogy, and identify questions on stock-flow pairs inside the national income identities. Method: sort the passbook's lines into snapshots and streams, then link each month's flows to the balance's change. Check yourself: 'money supply in March' is a stock even though money flows daily - the wording, not the word, decides. Drill: classify ten macro variables in two columns and state the stock each flow would change.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
**UNDERSTAND:** "India's foreign exchange reserves stood at $640 billion on 31st March 2024. During 2023-24, the current account deficit was $23 billion." Identify the stock and the flow. Explain why the change in reserves is NOT exactly equal to the current account deficit.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
**Correct:** Reserves ($640B) = stock (point in time). CAD ($23B) = flow (over FY24). Change in reserves ≠ CAD because reserves also change due to capital account flows (FDI, FII, loans), valuation changes (exchange rate), and gold revaluation.  
**Wrong answer reveals:** Student thinks BoP identity means reserves change = current account balance, ignoring capital/financial account and valuation effects. They treat stocks and flows as directly convertible without netting all flows.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
**ANALYZE:** "Net investment = Gross investment – Depreciation. Capital stock at end of year = Capital stock at start + Net investment."  
If capital stock on 1st April 2023 = ₹200 lakh crore, gross investment during 2023-24 = ₹30 lakh crore, depreciation = ₹8 lakh crore, calculate capital stock on 31st March 2024. Explain why depreciation is subtracted from gross investment to get the flow that changes the stock.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
**Correct:** Net investment = 30 – 8 = ₹22 lakh crore. End stock = 200 + 22 = ₹222 lakh crore. Depreciation is the wear-and-tear flow that reduces the existing stock; gross investment adds new capital. Only the *net* addition (gross – depreciation) increases the stock.  
**Wrong answer reveals:** Student adds gross investment (30) to get 230, ignoring that depreciation simultaneously reduces the stock. They treat depreciation as a separate stock reduction rather than a flow netting against gross investment.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
**EVALUATE:** "A country's money supply (M3) increased from ₹180 lakh crore to ₹200 lakh crore in one year. Therefore, the flow of money supply during that year was ₹20 lakh crore." Critique this statement.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
**Correct:** The statement is conceptually wrong. Money supply is a **stock**; it has no "flow". The ₹20 lakh crore is the **change in stock** (ΔM), not a flow variable. Flows that cause this change are: net bank credit to government, bank credit to commercial sector, net foreign exchange assets, etc. (sources of money supply).  
**Wrong answer reveals:** Student treats the change in a stock as a flow variable itself, confusing "change in stock" (which is a flow-equivalent) with a genuine flow like "money creation by RBI". They miss that stocks change *due to* flows.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students think "GDP is a stock because it measures the total size of the economy" — they confuse the *level* of activity (flow per year) with a *snapshot* of assets. Similarly, they call "National Wealth" a flow because "it changes over time". The error: ignoring the **time dimension in the definition** (per year vs. on a date). In board exams, they write "Money supply is a flow because money flows in the economy" — confusing the *verb* "flow" with the *noun* "flow variable".

## THIS_FORMULA
ΔStock = Net Flow over period  (i.e., Stock_end = Stock_start + Inflows - Outflows)

## FORMULA_VARIABLES
Stock_end = value at end of period; Stock_start = value at beginning; Inflows = sum of flow variables adding to stock; Outflows = sum of flow variables reducing stock.

## FORMULA_UNITS
Stock: rupees (₹), tonnes, number of machines, dollars ($). Flow: rupees per year (₹/year), tonnes per quarter, machines per month.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
**PROBLEM:** On 1st April 2023, a firm's capital stock (machinery) was valued at ₹50 crore. During 2023-24, it purchased new machinery worth ₹12 crore and sold old machinery (book value ₹3 crore) for ₹2 crore. Depreciation for the year was ₹4 crore. Calculate the capital stock on 31st March 2024 and identify all stock and flow variables.

**STEPS:**
- Step 1: Identify opening stock = ₹50 crore (capital stock on 1 Apr 2023).
- Step 2: Identify inflows (gross additions) = purchase of new machinery = ₹12 crore (flow during the year).
- Step 3: Identify outflows = sale of old machinery (book value ₹3 crore) + depreciation ₹4 crore = ₹7 crore (flows reducing stock).
- Step 4: Apply stock-flow identity: Closing Stock = Opening Stock + Inflows - Outflows = 50 + 12 - 7 = ₹55 crore.
- Step 5: Classify: Stocks — Capital stock (opening/closing). Flows — Gross investment (₹12 cr), Disinvestment/sale (₹3 cr), Depreciation (₹4 cr). Net investment = 12 - 3 - 4 = ₹5 crore = change in stock.

**REASONING:** The stock-flow identity must hold for every asset. Depreciation and disinvestment are outflows; purchases are inflows. The sale proceeds (₹2 cr) are a financial flow, not a real capital flow — the real capital outflow is the book value given up (₹3 cr).

**ANSWER:** Capital stock on 31st March 2024 = ₹55 crore. Stocks: Opening capital stock (₹50 cr), Closing capital stock (₹55 cr). Flows: Gross investment (₹12 cr), Disinvestment (₹3 cr), Depreciation (₹4 cr), Net investment (₹5 cr).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
**PROBLEM:** RBI's Monthly Bulletin shows: Money Supply (M3) on 31st March 2023 = ₹185.2 lakh crore; on 31st March 2024 = ₹202.8 lakh crore. During 2023-24, Net Bank Credit to Government = ₹14.5 lakh crore, Bank Credit to Commercial Sector = ₹16.2 lakh crore, Net Foreign Exchange Assets = ₹8.3 lakh crore, Government's Currency Liabilities to Public = ₹1.2 lakh crore, Net Non-Monetary Liabilities = ₹10.4 lakh crore. Verify the stock-flow consistency using the money multiplier identity: ΔM3 = ΔNFA + ΔNCG + ΔNCC - ΔNNML - ΔCurrency.

**STEPS:**
- Step 1: Compute ΔM3 (change in stock) = 202.8 - 185.2 = ₹17.6 lakh crore.
- Step 2: List all flow sources (given as changes during the year): ΔNFA = +8.3, ΔNCG = +14.5, ΔNCC = +16.2, ΔNNML = +10.4 (increase in non-monetary liabilities reduces money supply), ΔCurrency = +1.2 (increase in currency with public reduces bank deposits, hence M3).
- Step 3: Apply identity: RHS = 8.3 + 14.5 + 16.2 - 10.4 - 1.2 = 27.4 lakh crore.
- Step 4: Compare: LHS (ΔM3) = 17.6, RHS = 27.4. Discrepancy = 9.8 lakh crore.
- Step 5: Explain discrepancy: The identity holds by construction in RBI data; the mismatch here is because the given flows are *not* the only components (e.g., net RBI credit to banks, valuation changes, errors/omissions). In reality, RBI's "Sources of Money Supply" table balances exactly.

**REASONING:** Money supply (stock) changes only through identified flows (sources). The identity is an accounting tautology: every rupee increase in M3 must come from one of the sources. The exercise teaches that stocks are *exactly* the accumulation of flows — no more, no less.

**ANSWER:** ΔM3 = ₹17.6 lakh crore. Sum of given flows = ₹27.4 lakh crore. The difference (₹9.8 lakh crore) is explained by omitted flows (e.g., net RBI credit to banks, valuation adjustments). In actual RBI data, the identity balances to zero discrepancy.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
**PROBLEM:** Combine stock-flow with circular flow (nodes 003, 004). In a two-sector economy, households own the capital stock. Firms produce output using capital. Given: Capital stock on 1 Jan 2024 = ₹1,000 crore. Annual depreciation rate = 10%. Gross investment during 2024 = ₹150 crore. Households save 20% of their income. Firms' profits = ₹200 crore (all distributed). Wages = ₹800 crore. Assume no government/foreign sector. Calculate: (a) Capital stock on 31 Dec 2024. (b) Household income, saving, and change in financial wealth (stock). (c) Verify that household financial wealth accumulation equals firm's net investment (since firms issue no equity).

**STEPS:**
- Step 1: (a) Depreciation = 10% of 1,000 = ₹100 crore (flow). Net investment = Gross - Depreciation = 150 - 100 = ₹50 crore. Closing capital stock = 1,000 + 50 = ₹1,050 crore.
- Step 2: (b) Household income = Wages + Profits = 800 + 200 = ₹1,000 crore (flow). Saving = 20% of 1,000 = ₹200 crore (flow). Change in financial wealth = Saving = ₹200 crore (since no other outflows).
- Step 3: (c) Firms' net investment = ₹50 crore (real asset accumulation). But households' financial wealth increased by ₹200 crore. Discrepancy! Why? Because firms also accumulated financial assets (e.g., retained earnings? but profits fully distributed). Actually, in this simplified model, household saving = firm investment + firm financial accumulation. Since firm investment = 50, the remaining 150 must be firm's financial asset accumulation (e.g., cash, receivables) or household lending to firms beyond equity. But the problem says "firms issue no equity". So household saving (200) = firm net investment (50) + firm financial asset accumulation (150). The stock-flow consistency requires: ΔHousehold Financial Wealth = ΔFirm Real Capital + ΔFirm Financial Assets. Here ΔFirm Financial Assets = 150.
- Step 4: Check circular flow: Income (1000) = Consumption (800) + Saving (200). Expenditure = Consumption (800) + Investment (150) = 950. Wait — expenditure ≠ income! This violates the circular flow identity. The problem data is inconsistent. To fix: either investment = saving (200) or profits adjust. This reveals that stock-flow consistency *enforces* macroeconomic equilibrium.

**REASONING:** This example combines stock-flow accounting (capital stock, financial wealth) with circular flow identities (income = expenditure). Inconsistent data breaks the fundamental macro identity: S = I in a two-sector closed economy. The stock-flow framework exposes the inconsistency: household financial saving (200) must equal firm's total asset accumulation (real + financial). If firm's real investment is only 50, firms must be accumulating 150 of financial assets (e.g., holding cash, buying bonds), which means households are lending 150 to firms — but then household financial wealth change is still 200 (50 equity + 150 debt). The circular flow requires planned saving = planned investment for equilibrium.

**ANSWER:** (a) Capital stock 31 Dec 2024 = ₹1,050 crore. (b) Household income = ₹1,000 cr, Saving = ₹200 cr, ΔFinancial Wealth = ₹200 cr. (c) Data is inconsistent with circular flow equilibrium (S ≠ I). For consistency, either gross investment must be ₹200 cr (so net = 100, closing stock = 1,100) or saving must be ₹50 cr. Stock-flow consistency reveals macro equilibrium conditions.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
**QUESTION:** Read the following statements and identify the error in each:  
(i) "India's GDP in 2023-24 was ₹270 lakh crore, so the country's wealth increased by ₹270 lakh crore."  
(ii) "The fiscal deficit for 2023-24 was 5.8% of GDP; therefore, the debt-to-GDP ratio increased by 5.8 percentage points."  
(iii) "Money supply grew by 12% last year, so the flow of money supply was 12%."  
Explain the correct stock-flow relationship in each case.

**COMPETENCY:** Analyzing macroeconomic statements for stock-flow consistency (Critical Thinking / Conceptual Understanding)

**STEPS:**
- Step 1: Identify the stock and flow in each statement.
- Step 2: Explain why the statement confuses stock with flow or misstates the relationship.
- Step 3: State the correct relationship (e.g., GDP is a flow; it adds to wealth but wealth change ≠ GDP because of depreciation, capital gains/losses, etc.).
- Step 4: Write corrected version.

**ANSWER:**  
(i) Error: GDP (flow) ≠ change in wealth (stock). Wealth change = Net Domestic Product (GDP - depreciation) + net capital gains - net capital transfers.  
(ii) Error: Fiscal deficit (flow) adds to debt (stock), but debt-to-GDP ratio change depends on *both* numerator (debt increase) and denominator (GDP growth). Δ(Debt/GDP) ≈ (Deficit - g×Debt)/GDP, not simply deficit/GDP.  
(iii) Error: "Flow of money supply" is meaningless; money supply is a stock. The 12% is the *growth rate of the stock*, not a flow. Flows are the sources (credit, forex, etc.).

**DIFFICULTY:** medium

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
**QUESTION:** The Government of India's "Status Paper on Government Debt" (March 2024) reports:  
- Total outstanding liabilities (stock) on 31st March 2023: ₹155.6 lakh crore  
- Total outstanding liabilities on 31st March 2024: ₹171.8 lakh crore  
- Fiscal deficit during 2023-24: ₹17.8 lakh crore  
- Primary deficit during 2023-24: ₹8.5 lakh crore  
- Interest payments during 2023-24: ₹9.3 lakh crore  

(a) Verify the stock-flow identity: Closing Debt = Opening Debt + Fiscal Deficit. Does it hold exactly? If not, explain the discrepancy using the concept of "other liabilities" and "cash balance changes".  
(b) Derive the relationship between fiscal deficit, primary deficit, and interest payments.  
(c) If nominal GDP growth in 2023-24 was 10.5% and GDP in 2022-23 was ₹269.5 lakh crore, calculate the debt-to-GDP ratio for both years and comment on whether the debt ratio is sustainable (use the condition: primary deficit/GDP < (g - r) × (Debt/GDP), where g = nominal GDP growth rate, r = effective interest rate = interest payments / opening debt).

**COMPETENCY:** Applying stock-flow identities to real Indian fiscal data; evaluating debt sustainability (Quantitative Reasoning / Policy Evaluation)

**STEPS:**
- Step 1: (a) Compute expected closing debt = 155.6 + 17.8 = 173.4. Actual = 171.8. Discrepancy = -1.6 lakh crore. Explain: Fiscal deficit is net borrowing; but debt also changes due to small savings, provident fund, reserve fund, and cash balance drawdown (which reduce outstanding liabilities without being in fiscal deficit).
- Step 2: (b) Fiscal Deficit = Primary Deficit + Interest Payments. Check: 8.5 + 9.3 = 17.8. Holds.
- Step 3: (c) GDP 2023-24 = 269.5 × 1.105 = ₹297.8 lakh crore (approx). Debt/GDP 2023 = 155.6/269.5 = 57.7%. Debt/GDP 2024 = 171.8/297.8 = 57.7% (stable). Effective interest rate r = 9.3/155.6 = 5.98%. g - r = 10.5% - 5.98% = 4.52%. (g-r)×(Debt/GDP) = 0.0452 × 0.577 = 2.61%. Primary deficit/GDP = 8.5/297.8 = 2.85%. Since 2.85% > 2.61%, debt ratio would rise *if sustained* — marginally unsustainable.

**ANSWER:** (a) Identity holds approximately; discrepancy due to non-deficit debt operations. (b) FD = PD + IP (17.8 = 8.5 + 9.3). (c) Debt/GDP stable at ~57.7% in 2024, but primary deficit (2.85% of GDP) slightly exceeds the sustainable threshold (2.61%), implying debt ratio would rise if continued.

**DIFFICULTY:** hard
