## ELI5
The street's eldest shopkeeper treats each neighbour by that neighbour's conduct: aid lorries for the flooded shop next door, a locked counter and guarded gate for the feuding rival, patient renegotiation of an old lease with the hillside tenant. India's Neighbourhood First policy runs that differentiated ledger across South Asia — sovereignty and non-interference as the base rules, the Gujral Doctrine's non-reciprocity as generosity on offer — with history, security and trade deciding each neighbour's page.

## DEFINITION
India's role and relations with its South Asian neighbours refers to the set of foreign policy principles, bilateral engagements, and regional initiatives (primarily through SAARC) that guide India's interactions with Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Bhutan, Maldives, and Afghanistan. It is shaped by historical legacies (Partition, wars), security imperatives (terrorism, nuclear deterrence), economic cooperation (trade, connectivity), and the aspiration for regional stability under the "Neighbourhood First" policy and the Gujral Doctrine's principles of non-reciprocity and non-interference.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY

It does NOT cover India's relations with major powers outside South Asia (USA, China, Russia) except where they directly impact regional dynamics, nor does it cover the internal politics of neighbour countries beyond their impact on bilateral ties.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Name the seven countries that constitute India's "South Asian neighbourhood" in the official foreign policy sense, and state the guiding principle announced in 1996 that governs India's approach to them.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The seven countries are Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Bhutan, Maldives, and Afghanistan. The guiding principle is the Gujral Doctrine (1996), which enunciates five principles: (1) non-reciprocity in giving concessions, (2) non-interference in internal affairs, (3) respect for territorial integrity, (4) peaceful settlement of disputes, and (5) cooperation for mutual benefit. A wrong answer that lists only SAARC members but omits Afghanistan (joined 2007) or cites "Panchsheel" (which governs India-China relations) reveals confusion between regional grouping membership and the specific neighbourhood policy framework.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why the Indus Waters Treaty (1960) is often cited as a rare success of India-Pakistan cooperation despite multiple wars, and identify the third-party broker that made it possible.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The treaty succeeded because it allocated waters of the six Indus basin rivers (three eastern to India, three western to Pakistan) with a clear, technical formula that removed political discretion, and it established a Permanent Indus Commission for dispute resolution that survived wars. The World Bank brokered the negotiations. A wrong answer that credits the UN or Shimla Agreement shows misunderstanding of the treaty's unique institutional design — it is a functional, river-basin management arrangement insulated from political fluctuations, unlike most India-Pakistan agreements.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A new government in Nepal announces it will review the 1950 Treaty of Peace and Friendship, calling it "unequal." As an Indian diplomat, which two specific provisions of the treaty would you anticipate being contested, and what principle from the Gujral Doctrine would you invoke in the renegotiation?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The contested provisions are Article 2 (obligation to inform each other of serious friction with any foreign power) and Article 7 (national treatment for citizens in residence, property, business, and movement). Nepal views these as infringing on its sovereignty and creating an open border it cannot fully control. The Gujral Doctrine principle to invoke is "non-reciprocity" — India can offer concessions (e.g., revised transit arrangements, investment) without demanding immediate quid pro quo, demonstrating that the relationship is not transactional. A wrong answer focusing only on the open border or water sharing misses the treaty's security and citizenship dimensions that are the core of Nepal's grievance.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the drivers of India's relations with Bangladesh and Sri Lanka. In what way did the 1971 war create a fundamentally different starting point for India-Bangladesh ties compared to India-Sri Lanka ties, and how did the 13th Amendment (1987) shape the latter?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
India-Bangladesh relations began with India's military intervention leading to Bangladesh's birth, creating a "liberation dividend" of goodwill despite later irritants (water sharing, border killings, migration). India-Sri Lanka relations were shaped by the ethnic conflict: India's 1987 intervention (IPKF) and the Indo-Sri Lanka Accord, which mandated the 13th Amendment (provincial councils for Tamil areas), made India a direct party to Sri Lanka's internal power-sharing — a role Bangladesh never assigned India. The key difference: Bangladesh sees India as a midwife; Sri Lanka sees India as both a partner and a potential intervener in its domestic ethnic balance. A wrong answer that treats both as "small neighbours with ethnic minorities" conflates the structural asymmetry: Bangladesh's creation involved Indian troops; Sri Lanka's crisis involved Indian troops fighting Tamil militants.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate the statement: "SAARC has failed because it excludes contentious bilateral issues from its agenda." Use the SAARC Charter and at least two summit outcomes to support your judgment.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is partially correct but incomplete. The SAARC Charter (Article X) explicitly excludes "bilateral and contentious issues" from deliberations, which protects consensus but prevents the forum from addressing the region's core conflicts (Kashmir, Sir Creek, Teesta). However, SAARC has achieved functional cooperation in non-political areas: the South Asian Free Trade Area (SAFTA, 2004), the SAARC Food Bank (2007), and the COVID-19 Emergency Fund (2020). The 2014 Kathmandu Summit's motor vehicles and rail agreements showed progress on connectivity. The deeper failure is not the exclusion clause but the lack of political will to implement agreements — e.g., SAFTA's sensitive lists cover 40% of tariff lines. A wrong answer that calls SAARC "totally useless" ignores its role in building epistemic communities (officials, scholars) and its utility as a crisis-management backchannel (e.g., 2020 video conference on COVID).

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe "Neighbourhood First" means India gives unilateral concessions to neighbours who then take them for granted. In reality, the Gujral Doctrine's non-reciprocity was a strategic choice: India, as the largest economy and military power, absorbed short-term costs (e.g., sharing Ganga waters in 1996, opening borders to Nepalese goods) to build long-term goodwill and deny external powers (especially China) strategic space. The misconception arises from conflating "non-reciprocity" with "weakness" — but the 1996 Ganga Treaty gave Bangladesh guaranteed dry-season flow while India retained upstream control; the 2015 Nepal blockade was India's *leverage*, not concession. Students also wrongly assume SAARC is a "talk shop" with zero outcomes, ignoring the 2011 SAARC Agreement on Rapid Response to Natural Disasters that enabled India's 2015 Nepal earthquake relief within hours.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: In 2021, India and Pakistan announced a surprise ceasefire along the LoC via their DGMOs. Analyze this event using the four components of India's security strategy (military capability, international norms/institutions, internal unity, economic development) from the NCERT text.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the event — DGMO hotline agreement (Feb 2021) recommitting to 2003 ceasefire.
- Step 2: Map to military capability component — ceasefire reduces kinetic escalation, preserves military resources for modernization (e.g., Rafale induction, S-400 deployment) rather than attritional LoC firefights.
- Step 3: Map to international norms/institutions — the hotline itself is a CBM (Confidence Building Measure) rooted in the 1988 India-Pakistan agreement on non-attack on nuclear facilities; the ceasefire aligns with UNSC resolutions urging bilateral dialogue.
- Step 4: Map to internal unity — ceasefire lowers domestic pressure for retaliation after Pulwama (2019), giving political space for welfare focus; but also creates expectation of reciprocal Pakistani action on terror infrastructure (internal unity requires perceived fairness).
- Step 5: Map to economic development — reduced border tension could enable trade normalization (Pakistan's 2019 suspension of trade after Art 370), but India linked trade to "terror-free environment" — showing economic component is conditional on security.
- Step 6: Synthesize — the ceasefire is a tactical pause enabled by military deterrence (nuclear parity) and diplomatic backchannels, not a strategic shift; it serves India's economic development priority by lowering conflict costs, but remains fragile without progress on Component 3 (internal unity via terror cessation).
REASONING: The NCERT's four-component framework forces analysis beyond "peace is good" — it reveals how each component constrains and enables the other. The ceasefire is not an end but a means to reallocate resources from Component 1 to Component 4, conditional on Component 3.
ANSWER: The 2021 ceasefire reflects a calculated alignment of India's security strategy components: military deterrence holds, institutional CBMs function, internal unity is managed by deferring the terror issue, and economic development gains breathing space — but the absence of structural change in Pakistan's terror policy means the ceasefire is a tactical pause, not a strategic breakthrough.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Sri Lanka faces its worst economic crisis in 2022. India provides $4 billion in credit lines, currency swaps, and fuel/fertilizer aid. Explain why this response is consistent with the Gujral Doctrine's "non-reciprocity" principle, and identify one risk if Sri Lanka perceives this as "debt-trap diplomacy" by India.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Recall Gujral Doctrine Principle 1 — "With neighbours like Nepal, Bangladesh, Bhutan, Maldives, Sri Lanka, India does not ask for reciprocity but gives all that it can in good faith and trust."
- Step 2: Characterize India's 2022 aid — granted without demanding immediate policy concessions (e.g., no base access, no treaty revision), delivered via existing mechanisms (SAARC currency swap framework, EXIM Bank lines).
- Step 3: Contrast with China's Hambantota port lease (99-year lease for debt equity) — India's aid is grant/soft-loan based, not asset-seizing.
- Step 4: Identify the risk — if Sri Lanka's domestic politics frames Indian aid as "strategic encirclement" (e.g., opposition parties linking Trincomalee oil farm lease to aid), the "goodwill" capital erodes, violating Principle 2 (non-interference) because India's assistance becomes a political football in Sri Lanka's internal affairs.
- Step 5: Mitigation — India's transparent disbursal through Sri Lankan government systems (not parallel NGO channels) and joint monitoring committees align with Principle 5 (cooperation for mutual benefit) by making aid visible and accountable.
REASONING: Non-reciprocity is not charity; it is strategic investment in regional public goods (stability, goodwill). The risk arises when the recipient's internal politics reframes the gesture — a dynamic the Gujral Doctrine anticipated by emphasizing "trust" as a two-way street.
ANSWER: The 2022 aid exemplifies non-reciprocity: India bore disproportionate cost ($4 bn vs Sri Lanka's $85 bn GDP) without quid pro quo. The risk is domestic politicization in Sri Lanka turning aid into a sovereignty issue, which India mitigates by channeling aid through sovereign systems and avoiding conditionalities that could be portrayed as interference.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Nepal's 2020 constitution amendment claimed Kalapani, Limpiyadhura, Lipulekh (areas administered by India). India rejected the claim but agreed to "dialogue." Using the India-Nepal 1950 Treaty, the Gujral Doctrine, and the SAARC Charter, evaluate whether India's response was consistent with its stated neighbourhood principles.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the treaty provision — 1950 Treaty Article 1: "everlasting peace and friendship," Article 2: mutual notification of friction with foreign powers. The map dispute is a "friction" but not with a foreign power — it's bilateral.
- Step 2: Apply Gujral Doctrine Principle 3 — "respect for territorial integrity and sovereignty." India's position: the areas are Indian territory per 1816 Sugauli Treaty and 1961 boundary agreement; Nepal's map change unilaterally alters status quo.
- Step 3: Apply Gujral Principle 4 — "peaceful settlement of disputes." India's offer of dialogue (Foreign Secretary-level) fulfills this, but Nepal's precondition (acceptance of its map) violates the principle.
- Step 4: Check SAARC Charter Article X — bilateral disputes excluded from SAARC. India correctly kept the issue out of SAARC (no Nepal-sponsored agenda item at 2020+ summits).
- Step 5: Assess consistency — India's refusal to legitimize the map change upholds Principle 3; its dialogue offer upholds Principle 4; keeping it bilateral upholds SAARC Charter. However, the 2015 "unofficial blockade" (perceived as coercion) violated Principle 2 (non-interference) and damaged the "trust" foundation of non-reciprocity.
- Step 6: Conclusion — India's 2020 response was tactically consistent with principles but strategically undermined by the 2015 trust deficit. The dispute requires a boundary commission (technical) + political leadership dialogue (strategic), as envisaged in the 1950 Treaty's spirit.
REASONING: This multi-step analysis shows how principles interact — adherence to one (territorial integrity) can appear as violation of another (non-interference) if trust is low. The Gujral Doctrine is a package, not a menu.
ANSWER: India's 2020 response was formally consistent with Gujral Principles 3, 4 and SAARC Charter, but the legacy of the 2015 blockade (perceived as violation of Principle 2) meant Nepal did not view the dialogue offer as credible. Consistency requires sustained practice, not situational invocation.
