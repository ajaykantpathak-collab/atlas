## ELI5
Sell a shirt for $10 and in June 1991 you receive ₹210; after the July devaluation the same $10 brings ₹250 - a 19% jump in rupee revenue with no change in your dollar price. Foreign exchange reforms devalued the rupee by about 18-20% against major currencies and moved India from a fixed exchange rate to a market-determined one, set by demand and supply in the forex market. For a Tiruppur exporter, exports turn cheaper for buyers abroad and imports costlier at home - the shock therapy that stopped a reserve bleed at barely two weeks of import cover.

## DEFINITION
Foreign Exchange Reforms refer to the 1991 policy shift where India devalued the rupee by approximately 18-20% against major currencies and transitioned from a fixed exchange rate regime (pegged to a basket of currencies) to a market-determined exchange rate system, allowing the rupee's value to be set by demand and supply forces in the foreign exchange market, alongside dismantling quantitative restrictions on imports and reducing tariff barriers.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you run a small textile export business in Tiruppur, Tamil Nadu. In June 1991, you sell a shirt for $10 and receive ₹210 (at ₹21/$). After the July 1991 devaluation, the same $10 fetches ₹250 (at ₹25/$). Your revenue in rupees jumps 19% without changing your dollar price — this is the core logic of devaluation. Start by asking: "Why did the government deliberately make the rupee cheaper?" Connect to the BoP crisis (node c11_eco_cbse_03_001): India had only $1.1 billion reserves — barely 2 weeks of imports. Devaluation was the "shock therapy" to stop reserve bleeding. The key economic principle: a weaker domestic currency makes exports cheaper for foreigners (boosting demand) and imports costlier for domestic buyers (reducing demand), correcting the trade deficit.

Takeaway: mastery means the devaluation mechanism running both ways - a weaker rupee makes exports cheaper for foreigners and imports costlier for Indians, so export demand rises while import demand falls, correcting the trade deficit. Exam moves: calculate-the-percentage-devaluation questions and explain-why-devaluation-improves-competitiveness items using the ₹21-to-₹25 arithmetic. Check yourself: the Tiruppur exporter's 19% windfall is the policy's point - revenue rises without touching the dollar price. Students miss it because they read devaluation as national failure, when the definition frames it as deliberate correction: with reserves at $1.1 billion, barely two weeks of imports, the cheaper rupee was the fastest stop to the bleeding.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe "devaluation always causes inflation" as an absolute rule. In 1991, wholesale price index (WPI) inflation was already 13-14% pre-devaluation due to fiscal deficit monetisation and supply shocks. Devaluation added ~2-3 percentage points via import-cost push (especially oil, fertilizers), but the dominant inflation driver was domestic money supply growth. The misconception leads students to oppose devaluation on pure inflation grounds, ignoring the BoP constraint: without devaluation, reserves would have exhausted, forcing even more disruptive import compression.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: An Indian textile exporter sells shirts to a US buyer at $15 per shirt. In June 1991, the exchange rate was ₹21.14/$. After the July 1991 devaluation, the rate became ₹25.80/$. The exporter's production cost is ₹200 per shirt (domestic inputs only). Calculate: (a) Rupee revenue per shirt before and after devaluation, (b) Rupee profit margin before and after, (c) Percentage change in profit margin. Assume the dollar price remains unchanged.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the economic concept — devaluation increases domestic currency value of foreign currency earnings. Given: Dollar price = $15/shirt (constant), E_old = ₹21.14/$, E_new = ₹25.80/$, Cost = ₹200/shirt.
- Step 2: Calculate pre-devaluation rupee revenue: Revenue_old = $15 × ₹21.14 = ₹317.10 per shirt.
- Step 3: Calculate post-devaluation rupee revenue: Revenue_new = $15 × ₹25.80 = ₹387.00 per shirt.
- Step 4: Compute profit margins: Profit_old = ₹317.10 - ₹200 = ₹117.10; Profit_new = ₹387.00 - ₹200 = ₹187.00.
- Step 5: Percentage change in profit = [(187 - 117.10) / 117.10] × 100 = 59.7% increase.
- Step 6: Economic interpretation — devaluation acts as a windfall gain for exporters with domestic cost structures, improving competitiveness without cutting dollar prices.

REASONING: The exporter's dollar revenue is unchanged, but each dollar converts to more rupees. Since costs are in rupees (domestic labour, cotton), the margin expands. This illustrates the "expenditure-switching" effect: devaluation makes domestic goods relatively cheaper abroad, boosting export profitability and incentivising production for export markets.

ANSWER: (a) ₹317.10 → ₹387.00 per shirt; (b) ₹117.10 → ₹187.00 per shirt; (c) 59.7% profit margin increase.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: India's crude oil import bill in 1990-91 was 25 million tonnes at $20/barrel (1 tonne ≈ 7.33 barrels). The exchange rate moved from ₹21.14/$ to ₹25.80/$. Calculate the additional rupee cost of oil imports due solely to devaluation. If oil imports constituted 30% of total import bill, estimate the percentage increase in total import bill in rupee terms assuming non-oil import volumes and dollar prices unchanged.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Convert oil volume to barrels: 25 million tonnes × 7.33 barrels/tonne = 183.25 million barrels.
- Step 2: Calculate dollar value of oil imports: 183.25M barrels × $20/barrel = $3,665 million (≈ $3.665 billion).
- Step 3: Rupee cost pre-devaluation: $3,665M × ₹21.14 = ₹77,478 million (₹7,748 crores).
- Step 4: Rupee cost post-devaluation: $3,665M × ₹25.80 = ₹94,557 million (₹9,456 crores).
- Step 5: Additional rupee cost = ₹9,456 - ₹7,748 = ₹1,708 crores.
- Step 6: Since oil = 30% of import bill, non-oil = 70%. Non-oil rupee cost increases by 22% (same devaluation %). Weighted average increase = (0.3 × 22%) + (0.7 × 22%) = 22%. But oil volume may be inelastic — if oil quantity unchanged, total import bill rises 22% in rupees.
- Step 7: In reality, higher rupee price reduces oil demand somewhat (price elasticity), but 1991 short-run elasticity was low (~0.1-0.2), so import compression was minimal initially.

REASONING: Devaluation raises rupee cost of all imports proportionally. For essential imports with low elasticity (oil, fertilizers, edible oils), the bill rises nearly by the full devaluation percentage, worsening the trade balance initially (J-curve effect). The ₹1,708 crore additional oil cost strained the fiscal budget via higher subsidy burden on kerosene/LPG.

ANSWER: Additional oil import cost = ₹1,708 crores; Total import bill increase ≈ 22% in rupee terms (short-run, assuming inelastic volumes).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Under LERMS (1992), an Indian software firm exports services worth $50,000. The official RBI rate is ₹25.50/$ and the market rate is ₹31.20/$. The firm must surrender 40% at the official rate. Calculate: (a) Total rupees received, (b) Effective exchange rate, (c) Gain compared to full surrender at official rate, (d) Loss compared to full conversion at market rate. Then explain why LERMS was a transitional compromise between fixed and floating regimes.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify LERMS parameters: 40% surrender at official rate (₹25.50), 60% at market rate (₹31.20). Export earnings = $50,000.
- Step 2: Surrender portion: 40% of $50,000 = $20,000 at ₹25.50 = ₹510,000.
- Step 3: Free portion: 60% of $50,000 = $30,000 at ₹31.20 = ₹936,000.
- Step 4: Total rupees = ₹510,000 + ₹936,000 = ₹1,446,000.
- Step 5: Effective rate = ₹1,446,000 / $50,000 = ₹28.92/$.
- Step 6: Full official rate scenario: $50,000 × ₹25.50 = ₹1,275,000. Gain = ₹1,446,000 - ₹1,275,000 = ₹171,000 (13.4% more).
- Step 7: Full market rate scenario: $50,000 × ₹31.20 = ₹1,560,000. Loss = ₹1,560,000 - ₹1,446,000 = ₹114,000 (7.3% less).
- Step 8: Economic rationale — LERMS gave exporters a blended rate better than pure fixed rate, incentivising exports, while RBI retained 40% of forex at below-market rate to finance essential imports (oil, fertilizers) at subsidised rates. It avoided sudden float risks (volatility, speculation) while moving toward market determination.
- Step 9: By 1993, the surrender ratio shifted to 0:100 (unified market rate), achieving full current account convertibility in 1994.

REASONING: LERMS was a "dual exchange rate" system — a classic transitional tool. The 40% surrender requirement taxed exporters implicitly (they got less than market rate on that portion), cross-subsidising essential imports. The effective rate (₹28.92) was a weighted average reflecting policy weights, not market equilibrium. This connects to the broader reform sequence: crisis management (1991 devaluation) → managed transition (LERMS 1992-93) → market unification (1993) → current account convertibility (1994).

ANSWER: (a) ₹1,446,000; (b) ₹28.92/$; (c) Gain ₹171,000 vs full official rate; (d) Loss ₹114,000 vs full market rate.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: The Reserve Bank of India's 1991 Annual Report states: "The exchange rate adjustment of July 1991 was designed to eliminate the premium in the forward market and to unify the exchange rate." Using the concept of covered interest parity (CIP), explain why a large forward premium (forward rate > spot rate) signaled an expected devaluation, and how the actual devaluation affected the forward premium. Assume the 3-month forward rate pre-devaluation was ₹23.50/$ while spot was ₹21.14/$.

COMPETENCY: Analyzing market relationships and expectations in foreign exchange markets

STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand covered interest parity: (F - S)/S ≈ i_d - i_f, where F = forward rate, S = spot rate, i_d = domestic interest rate, i_f = foreign interest rate. A large forward premium (F > S) implies i_d > i_f, but in crisis contexts, it primarily reflects devaluation expectations.
- Step 2: Calculate pre-devaluation forward premium: (23.50 - 21.14) / 21.14 = 11.16% for 3 months → ~44.6% annualised. This huge premium signaled markets expected a large devaluation soon.
- Step 3: The July 1991 devaluation (spot moving to ~₹25.80) was larger than the forward premium implied. Post-devaluation, the forward premium collapsed as the expected devaluation was realised — the "peso problem" resolved.
- Step 4: Unification meant the official rate (previously ₹21.14) was aligned with market reality, eliminating the multiple exchange rate practice that distorted trade incentives.
- Step 5: Economic significance: The forward premium was a market-based early warning indicator. The devaluation validated rational expectations, restoring credibility.

ANSWER: The 11.16% 3-month forward premium (44.6% annualised) reflected market anticipation of devaluation. The actual devaluation (~22%) exceeded the premium, causing the forward rate to adjust downward post-announcement, eliminating the premium and unifying spot/forward rates. This demonstrated that the devaluation was credible and largely anticipated, reducing uncertainty.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Critically evaluate the claim: "India's transition from fixed to market-determined exchange rate (1991-1994) was a textbook case of successful exchange rate regime transition." Use the following data points: (i) Reserve adequacy: Import cover rose from 2 weeks (June 1991) to 12 months (March 1994); (ii) Real effective exchange rate (REER) appreciated 15% during 1993-96 despite nominal depreciation; (iii) Export growth averaged 10% annually in dollar terms during 1992-97; (iv) The 1995-96 "twin deficit" (fiscal + current account) re-emerged. Structure your answer as: Thesis → Evidence → Counter-evidence → Synthesis.

COMPETENCY: Evaluating policy outcomes using multiple economic indicators

STEPS:
- Step 1: Thesis — The transition was largely successful in achieving its immediate objectives: resolving BoP crisis, building reserves, and establishing a market-based system without hyperinflation or financial collapse.
- Step 2: Evidence for success: (i) Reserve adequacy improved dramatically — from 2 weeks to 12 months import cover in 3 years, exceeding IMF adequacy metrics. (iii) Export growth of 10% annually in dollar terms (1992-97) outperformed world trade growth (~6%), indicating competitiveness gains. India's managed float with capital controls proved more resilient than hard pegs or free floats in subsequent crises (synthesis).
