## ELI5
Water a drooping hibiscus and within hours the leaves stand crisp again - the water climbed the whole plant while you looked away. Xylem is the tube-like tissue running that lift, carrying water and dissolved minerals upward from the root hairs to the highest leaf. It is not a pump pushing from below; evaporation from the leaves pulls the column upward, like a paper towel wicking a spill.

## DEFINITION
Xylem is a specialized, tube-like vascular tissue in plants that transports water and dissolved minerals upward from the roots to the stem, leaves, and other aerial parts.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think that water is "pumped" upward by the roots like a mechanical water pump pushing water into an overhead tank. In reality, roots create only a small positive pressure (root pressure), and the primary force driving water to the top of tall trees is the suction created by transpiration (evaporation) at the leaves.
## ON_FAILURE
To correct this, take a dry straw dipped in water and suck from the top to feel how suction pulls liquid upward from above. Then, explain that leaves act like that suction force when water evaporates from their surface, pulling a continuous column of water up through the xylem tubes from below.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A farmer in Punjab notices that during a hot, dry summer afternoon, his young tomato plants temporarily droop, even though the soil is wet. By evening, the plants stand upright again. Explain this phenomenon using the transport system of plants.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the tissues involved in water movement inside the plant (xylem vessels running from roots to leaves).
- Step 2: Recognize the environmental factor acting on the leaves (high temperature and dry air causing rapid evaporation/transpiration).
- Step 3: Compare the rate of water loss from leaves with the rate of water absorption by roots during peak heat (transpiration rate exceeds root absorption rate).
- Step 4: Trace the physical consequence on plant cells (loss of water from cells causes them to lose turgidity, resulting in drooping).
- Step 5: Explain the evening recovery (as temperature drops, transpiration slows down, allowing roots to catch up and restore water column turgidity).
REASONING: Water movement in xylem depends on a balance between loss at the top (transpiration) and gain at the bottom (absorption). When heat accelerates loss beyond replacement capacity, temporary water stress occurs, which resolves when the evaporation rate drops.
ANSWER: The plants droop on hot afternoons because water evaporates from the leaves faster than the roots can supply it through the xylem. In the evening, lower temperatures reduce evaporation, allowing the xylem to restore full water pressure and upright posture.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A science teacher places a leafy twig of a balsam plant into a beaker containing red-colored eosin solution. After two hours, she makes a thin cross-section of the stem and views it under a microscope. Which specific regions appear red, and why?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the main liquid being absorbed by the cut stem (red eosin solution representing water and dissolved minerals).
- Step 2: Trace the pathway of liquid entry into the stem (cut end of stem into vascular bundles).
- Step 3: Identify the specific tissue responsible for upward water conduction within the vascular bundle (xylem vessels).
- Step 4: Correlate microscopic appearance with tissue location (ring or clusters of circular xylem vessels stained red).
- Step 5: Conclude why only these specific cells are colored (they are the specialized hollow tubes through which water travels upward).
REASONING: Xylem tissue consists of hollow, dead tubular cells specialized for upward water transport. When colored water enters the stem, it travels exclusively through these tubes, staining them while surrounding storage and protective tissues remain unstained.
ANSWER: The inner ring of xylem vessels appears red under the microscope because xylem is the specialized tissue responsible for transporting water and dissolved substances upward through the plant stem.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A potted plant is kept in a dark room for 48 hours, and then one of its leaves is coated thoroughly with petroleum jelly (vaseline) on both upper and lower surfaces to block all stomata. The plant is then placed back in sunlight for 6 hours. When tested, that specific leaf shows no starch production and the stem below it shows reduced water movement compared to uncoated leaves. Connect photosynthesis, transpiration, and xylem transport to explain this observation.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Analyze the effect of petroleum jelly on leaf stomata (stomata are sealed shut, blocking gas exchange and stopping transpiration).
- Step 2: Connect stopped transpiration to xylem transport (without transpiration pull, water transport through the xylem to this specific leaf stops or slows drastically).
- Step 3: Connect water availability to photosynthesis (water is a mandatory raw material for photosynthesis along with carbon dioxide).
- Step 4: Analyze gas exchange limitation (sealed stomata also prevent entry of carbon dioxide gas).
- Step 5: Combine effects to explain lack of starch (without water and carbon dioxide due to blocked stomata and halted xylem pull, photosynthesis cannot occur, so no starch is formed).
REASONING: Transpiration pull drives xylem transport to supply water to leaves. Blocking stomata halts transpiration, cutting off the water supply via xylem and preventing CO2 entry, which collectively shuts down photosynthesis and starch formation.
ANSWER: Coating the leaf with petroleum jelly blocks stomata, stopping transpiration pull in the xylem and preventing CO2 entry. Without water from the xylem and carbon dioxide from the air, photosynthesis cannot take place, resulting in no starch production.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: In an experiment, the roots of a living potted plant are carefully washed and submerged in colored water, while the stem and leaves remain in dry air. A student cuts the stem 5 cm above the soil and observes colored liquid slowly oozing out from the cut surface. What biological phenomenon does this demonstrate, and how does it differ from transpiration pull?
COMPETENCY: Interpreting cause-effect in life processes
STEPS:
- Step 1: Analyze the experimental setup (roots in water, stem cut above soil, no leaves present for transpiration).
- Step 2: Identify the force causing liquid to ooze out at the cut stem (root pressure).
- Step 3: Define root pressure as active pressure generated by root cells pushing water upward into the xylem.
- Step 4: Contrast with transpiration pull (root pressure is a push from below active in small plants or moist conditions, whereas transpiration pull is a suction force generated from above by leaves).
ANSWER: This demonstrates "root pressure," which is the positive pressure developed in roots as they absorb water actively from the soil. Unlike transpiration pull, which is a pulling force generated by evaporation from leaves at the top, root pressure pushes water upward from the base, though it is not strong enough on its own to push water to the top of tall trees.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Two identical potted sunflower plants (Plant A and Plant B) are taken. Plant A has its leaves intact, while Plant B has all its leaves coated with a thin layer of waterproof wax. Both are watered equally and placed in a sunny courtyard for 24 hours. Predict the physical state and internal water content of both plants after 24 hours, and explain the role of xylem in this outcome.
COMPETENCY: Predicting consequences of biological changes
STEPS:
- Step 1: Compare the condition of Plant A (leaves intact, normal transpiration and xylem transport occurring) and Plant B (leaves coated with wax, transpiration blocked, xylem transport severely reduced).
- Step 2: Predict the condition of Plant A after 24 hours (healthy, maintaining normal water balance as continuous xylem flow replenishes lost water).
- Step 3: Predict the condition of Plant B (waterlogged roots or internal water stagnation because water cannot exit via transpiration, leading to reduced mineral absorption and potential wilting or root decay due to lack of transpiration-driven flow).
- Step 4: Relate the outcome to xylem function (xylem relies on the continuous flow created by transpiration; stopping transpiration halts the upward stream of water and minerals).
ANSWER: Plant A will remain healthy because continuous transpiration drives water and mineral transport up the xylem. Plant B will suffer because the wax blocks transpiration, stopping the upward pull in the xylem; without this flow, water and mineral transport stalls, which can lead to nutrient deficiency, water stagnation in roots, and wilting despite wet soil.
DIFFICULTY: hard
