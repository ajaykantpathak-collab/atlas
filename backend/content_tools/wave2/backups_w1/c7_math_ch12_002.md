## ELI5
Four notebooks at 24.75 rupees each: multiply as if both were whole numbers, then give the product exactly as many decimal places as the decimal factor wore. The point is not decoration - it is the tally of tenths and hundredths carried through the multiplication. Whole numbers secretly wear no decimal places at all, so the decimal factor sets the count. Multiply first, count places second, drop the point in - and the bill totals to the paisa.

## DEFINITION
Multiplying a decimal number by a whole number involves multiplying the numbers as if they were whole numbers, and then placing the decimal point in the product so that it has the same number of decimal places as the original decimal factor.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
Can multiply any given decimal number (up to three decimal places) by a single or multi-digit whole number, correctly place the decimal point in the final product by counting total decimal places, and apply this operation to solve real-world measurement and money problems.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers the multiplication of terminating decimal numbers by positive whole numbers (including zero and powers of 10). Does NOT cover the multiplication of two decimals with each other or algebraic decimal expressions.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are buying 4 notebooks for school, and each notebook costs ₹24.75. To find the total cost, you need to calculate $4 \times 24.75$, which means adding 24.75 four times. Just as you learned to multiply large numbers and handle fractions in earlier classes, we can look at decimals as fractions with denominators of 10, 100, or 1000. To begin without getting confused by the dot, write down the numbers and multiply them as if they were standard whole numbers. Finally, count how many digits are behind the decimal point in your decimal factor, and place the decimal point in your answer from right to left so it has the exact same count. The key insight is that multiplying a decimal by a whole number scales the decimal value just like scaling a fraction.

**Takeaway:** decimal times whole: multiply whole-style, then place the point to match the decimal factor's places. **In the exam:** count the decimal places first, do the whole-number product, then insert the point that many in. **Check yourself:** compute the notebook bill and check the paise column survived the placement. **Why students miss it:** they place the point by habit at two places, regardless of the factor's actual count.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often align the decimal points vertically as they do in addition and subtraction, or they miscount the total number of decimal places in the product. For instance, when calculating $1.25 \times 3$, they might write down $1.25$ and $3$, misalign or misplace the decimal point to get $37.5$ instead of $3.75$ by treating $1.25$ as $12.5$.
## ON_FAILURE
Remind the student to ignore the decimal point initially and perform standard whole-number multiplication: write $125 \times 3 = 375$. Next, instruct them to count the exact number of digits to the right of the decimal point in the original decimal number (two digits in $1.25$). Finally, have them start from the rightmost digit of the product and count back that exact number of places to insert the decimal point: $3.75$.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A school cooperative store sells a geometry box for ₹54.50. If a teacher purchases 6 geometry boxes for the classroom, what is the total cost?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the given values: cost of one geometry box = ₹54.50, number of boxes = 6.
- Step 2: Set up the multiplication expression: $54.50 \times 6$.
- Step 3: Multiply the numbers without the decimal point: $5450 \times 6$. Compute $5000 \times 6 = 30000$, $400 \times 6 = 2400$, and $50 \times 6 = 300$, giving $30000 + 2400 + 300 = 32700$.
- Step 4: Count the decimal places in the original decimal factor ($54.50$ has two decimal places).
- Step 5: Place the decimal point two places from the right in the product $32700$, resulting in $327.00$, with units in rupees.
REASONING: By temporarily ignoring the decimal point, we convert the decimal multiplication into a standard whole-number computation. Accounting for the two decimal places at the end restores the correct value scale representing rupees and paise.
ANSWER: ₹327.00
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A athletics track lap is 1.25 kilometres long. A runner completes 8 laps during training. What is the total distance covered by the runner?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the given values: length of one lap = 1.25 km, number of laps = 8.
- Step 2: Set up the expression: $1.25 \times 8$.
- Step 3: Treat the decimal as a whole number and multiply: $125 \times 8$. Compute $100 \times 8 = 800$ and $25 \times 8 = 200$, yielding $800 + 200 = 1000$.
- Step 4: Identify the number of decimal places in $1.25$, which is two decimal places.
- Step 5: Place the decimal point two places from the right in $1000$, yielding $10.00$, which simplifies to $10\text{ km}$.
REASONING: Multiplying a decimal distance by a whole number of laps scales the unit distance linearly. Counting the decimal places ensures proper conversion back from the whole-number calculation.
ANSWER: 10 km
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A farmer packs 15 bags of grain, where each bag weighs 42.75 kg. If an empty truck weighs 1250 kg, what is the total weight of the truck loaded with these 15 bags?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate the total weight of the grain bags by setting up $42.75 \times 15$.
- Step 2: Break down the multiplication using distributive property: $42.75 \times (10 + 5) = (42.75 \times 10) + (42.75 \times 5)$.
- Step 3: Compute $42.75 \times 10 = 427.5$ and compute $42.75 \times 5 = 213.75$.
- Step 4: Add the two partial products: $427.50 + 213.75 = 641.25\text{ kg}$ (total weight of grain).
- Step 5: Add the weight of the empty truck to find the grand total: $1250 + 641.25 = 1891.25\text{ kg}$.
REASONING: This multi-step problem combines decimal multiplication with whole-number addition, requiring us to compute the weight of the cargo first before combining it with the vehicle's tare weight.
ANSWER: 1891.25 kg
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Without calculating the exact product, determine whether the product of $7.89 \times 12$ is greater than or less than $90$, and justify your reasoning using estimation.
COMPETENCY: Estimating and justifying decimal operations
STEPS:
- Step 1: Round the decimal factor $7.89$ to the nearest whole number, which is $8$.
- Step 2: Multiply the rounded whole number by the multiplier: $8 \times 12 = 96$.
- Step 3: Recognize that since $7.89$ is slightly less than $8$, the actual product will be slightly less than $96$, but still greater than $7 \times 12 = 84$.
- Step 4: Conclude whether the product is greater than $90$. Since $96$ is close to 90, verify more closely: $7 \times 12 = 84$ and $0.89 \times 12$ is about $10.68$, so $84 + 10.68 = 94.68$, which is greater than $90$.
ANSWER: The product is greater than 90 because rounding $7.89$ down to 8 gives $8 \times 12 = 96$, and even a tighter lower bound check ($7 \times 12 = 84$ plus a portion of 12) yields approximately $94.68$, which exceeds 90.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A container holds 3.25 litres of juice. A juice vendor prepares 24 such containers for a school function. He spills 1.5 litres during transport. How much juice is finally available for the function?
COMPETENCY: Multi-step real-world problem solving with decimals
STEPS:
- Step 1: Find the total volume prepared by multiplying the number of containers by the volume per container: $24 \times 3.25$.
- Step 2: Perform the multiplication: $24 \times 325 = 7800$, and place two decimal places to get $78.00\text{ litres}$.
- Step 3: Subtract the spilled amount from the total prepared volume: $78.00 - 1.5$.
- Step 4: Align decimal points for subtraction: $78.0 - 1.5 = 76.5\text{ litres}$.
ANSWER: 76.5 litres of juice are finally available after accounting for the spill.
DIFFICULTY: hard
