## ELI5
Where a rectangle's two diagonals cross, each one is cut into two equal pieces — the crossing point is the exact midpoint of both strings. The rectangle's diagonals bisect each other. The reason lives in the twin triangles formed around the crossing: the parallel sides create equal alternate angles, a shared side completes the picture, and the angle-side-angle rule makes the triangles congruent — so the four little segments from the crossing pair off equally. Every rectangle keeps this quiet centre of balance.

## DEFINITION
In a rectangle, the two diagonals intersect at a point that divides each diagonal into two equal segments. This means the intersection point is the midpoint of both diagonals, so each diagonal is bisected by the other.

## MASTERY_CRITERIA

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are designing a rectangular garden bed with corners marked by stakes. You stretch strings from opposite corners to find the exact center for a fountain. How do you know the strings cross exactly at their midpoints? Start by drawing rectangle ABCD with diagonals AC and BD intersecting at O. Recall from c8_math_ch4_2 that opposite sides of a rectangle are parallel and equal, and all angles are 90°. To prove AO = OC and BO = OD, focus on triangles formed by the diagonals and parallel sides. The key insight is that alternate interior angles created by the parallel sides and the transversal diagonals are equal, giving us the angle-side-angle (ASA) congruence needed to prove the triangles formed by the diagonals are congruent.

Takeaway: the diagonals of a rectangle bisect each other; the proof pairs the triangles on opposite sides of the crossing — parallel sides force equal alternate angles, and ASA congruence makes the crossing segments correspond. Exam format: expect the congruence proof and 'find AO or OC given the diagonal' problems; separate the congruence step from the equal-segments conclusion. Verification beat: confirm both diagonals are bisected — the property belongs to the pair, not one string. Why students miss it: they cite the property without the alternate-angle engine; the parallel sides are what feed ASA, and that citation is the proof the examiner reads for.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The proof for a rectangle is essentially the same as for a general parallelogram because a rectangle is a special parallelogram. Both use ASA congruence on triangles formed by the diagonals and parallel sides (alternate interior angles). However, the rectangle proof can also use SAS because adjacent sides are perpendicular, giving right angles. A wrong answer claiming "rectangle proof is harder" reveals the student doesn't recognize that rectangle inherits all parallelogram properties. ## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student proves that diagonals of rectangle ABCD bisect each other by showing ΔAOB ≅ ΔCOD using SSS (AB = CD, AO = CO, BO = DO). Evaluate this proof. Is it valid? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The proof is invalid because it uses the conclusion (AO = CO, BO = DO) as a given in the SSS criterion. This is circular reasoning. To prove bisecting, you must prove AO = CO and BO = DO without assuming them. The correct approach uses ASA or SAS with known equal sides (AB = CD) and alternate interior angles. A wrong answer saying "it's valid" reveals the student doesn't understand logical structure of proofs and confuses given information with what needs to be proven.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often confuse "diagonals bisect each other" with "diagonals bisect the angles of the rectangle." They incorrectly assume that because the diagonals cross at the midpoint, they also split the 90° corner angles into 45° each. This leads to errors like assuming ∠ABD = 45° in every rectangle, which is only true for a square. For example, in a 3 cm × 4 cm rectangle, the diagonal makes an angle of arctan(4/3) ≈ 53° with the base, not 45°.
## ON_FAILURE
Draw a non-square rectangle (e.g., length 8 cm, breadth 4 cm) on grid paper. Draw both diagonals and measure the angles they make with the sides using a protractor. Show that the angles are not 45°. Then prove using triangle congruence: In rectangle ABCD, diagonal AC creates ΔABC and ΔADC. These are congruent by RHS (hypotenuse AC common, AB = CD, ∠B = ∠D = 90°). This gives ∠BAC = ∠DCA (alternate interior), but ∠BAC ≠ ∠CAD unless AB = BC. Emphasize: bisecting each other ≠ bisecting angles.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A rectangular playground has length 40 m and breadth 30 m. Two straight paths are built along the diagonals crossing at the center. A water fountain is placed exactly at the crossing point. Find the distance from the fountain to each corner of the playground.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the rectangle ABCD with AB = 40 m, BC = 30 m. Diagonals AC and BD intersect at O. The fountain is at O. We need AO, BO, CO, DO.
- Step 2: Use Pythagoras theorem in right triangle ABC to find diagonal AC: AC² = AB² + BC² = 40² + 30² = 1600 + 900 = 2500. So AC = √2500 = 50 m.
- Step 3: Since rectangle diagonals are equal, BD = AC = 50 m.
- Step 4: Since diagonals bisect each other, O is the midpoint of both diagonals. Therefore, AO = OC = AC/2 = 25 m, and BO = OD = BD/2 = 25 m.
- Step 5: The fountain is 25 m from each corner. Verify: All four distances are equal, consistent with the property that the intersection point is equidistant from all vertices in a rectangle.

REASONING: The problem combines the Pythagorean theorem (Class 7/8) with the diagonal bisecting property. First we find the diagonal length using the right angle property of rectangles, then apply the bisecting property to halve it. The equidistance from the center to vertices is a direct consequence of equal diagonals bisecting each other.

ANSWER: 25 m from each corner.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: In rectangle PQRS, diagonals PR and QS intersect at O. If ∠POQ = 110°, find ∠OPS. (Hint: Use the fact that diagonals bisect each other and triangle angle sum property.)

STEPS:
- Step 1: Draw rectangle PQRS with diagonals intersecting at O. Given ∠POQ = 110°.
- Step 2: Since diagonals bisect each other, PO = OQ and RO = OS. So triangle POQ is isosceles with PO = OQ.
- Step 3: In isosceles triangle POQ, base angles are equal: ∠OPQ = ∠OQP. Sum of angles in triangle POQ = 180°. So ∠OPQ + ∠OQP + 110° = 180° → 2∠OPQ = 70° → ∠OPQ = 35°.

- Step 5: Better: Look at triangle POS. Since diagonals bisect each other, PO = OS? No, diagonals bisect each other means PO = OR and QO = OS. Since diagonals are also equal, PO = QO = RO = SO. So triangle POS is also isosceles with PO = OS.
- Step 6: ∠POS is supplementary to ∠POQ (linear pair). So ∠POS = 180° - 110° = 70°.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A farmer has a rectangular field ABCD. He wants to divide it into four triangular plots of equal area by building fences along the diagonals. Prove that the four triangles formed (AOB, BOC, COD, DOA) have equal area, where O is the intersection of diagonals.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Draw rectangle ABCD with diagonals AC and BD intersecting at O. We need to prove Area(ΔAOB) = Area(ΔBOC) = Area(ΔCOD) = Area(ΔDOA).
- Step 2: From the property proved in this node, diagonals bisect each other: AO = OC and BO = OD.
- Step 3: Consider triangles AOB and COD. They have AO = OC, BO = OD, and vertically opposite angles ∠AOB = ∠COD. So ΔAOB ≅ ΔCOD (SAS). Therefore, Area(ΔAOB) = Area(ΔCOD). (Congruent triangles have equal area.)
- Step 4: Similarly, ΔBOC ≅ ΔDOA (SAS: BO = OD, CO = OA, ∠BOC = ∠DOA). So Area(ΔBOC) = Area(ΔDOA).
- Step 5: Now compare Area(ΔAOB) and Area(ΔBOC). They share the same altitude from B to line AC (since B is a vertex and base is on AC). Their bases are AO and OC, which are equal (bisected). Area = ½ × base × height. Since bases AO = OC and height is same, Area(ΔAOB) = Area(ΔBOC).
- Step 6: By transitivity, all four triangles have equal area. The farmer's four plots are equal.

REASONING: This problem connects the bisecting property to area equality. The proof uses congruence (from bisecting) to show opposite triangles equal, and then uses the equal-base-equal-height principle (since O is midpoint) to show adjacent triangles equal. It demonstrates how a geometric property (bisecting) has a practical consequence (equal division of land).

ANSWER: The four triangular plots have equal area because the diagonals bisect each other, making the bases of the triangles on each diagonal equal, and they share the same altitudes.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Rahul draws a rectangle and its diagonals. He measures the angle between the diagonals and finds it to be 80°. He claims, "Since the diagonals bisect each other, the four triangles formed are all congruent." Is Rahul correct? Justify your answer with reasoning.

COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction; reasoning about geometric properties.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand the claim: "four triangles formed by diagonals are all congruent."
- Step 2: Recall properties: In a rectangle, diagonals are equal and bisect each other. This gives PO = QO = RO = SO (all four half-diagonals equal).
- Step 3: The four triangles are ΔAOB, ΔBOC, ΔCOD, ΔDOA. They have two sides equal (half-diagonals) and included angles? The included angles are the angles between the diagonals. Vertically opposite angles are equal (e.g., ∠AOB = ∠COD = 80°). Adjacent angles are supplementary (∠BOC = 100°).
- Step 4: So ΔAOB ≅ ΔCOD (SAS: two half-diagonals and 80°). And ΔBOC ≅ ΔDOA (SAS: two half-diagonals and 100°). But ΔAOB is NOT congruent to ΔBOC because their included angles differ (80° vs 100°) unless the angle is 90° (square).
- Step 5: Conclusion: Rahul is incorrect. The four triangles are congruent only if the diagonals are perpendicular (i.e., in a square). In a general rectangle, there are two pairs of congruent triangles.

ANSWER: Rahul is incorrect. The diagonals bisect each other, making all four half-diagonals equal. However, the angles between the diagonals are not necessarily 90°. The vertically opposite angles are equal (80° and 100°), so only opposite triangles are congruent. All four are congruent only in a square (where diagonals are perpendicular).
