## ELI5
Two friends lap the school track every 4 and 6 minutes from a shared start: their shared return times are common multiples, and the first one home to the line is the LCM. Every common multiple later is just the LCM wearing a bigger number - the fourth meeting sits at four times the first. Checking membership is simple division with no remainder; a number that clears only one runner's timetable is an impostor. Multiples race forward; factors dig down - never confuse the two directions.

## DEFINITION
Common multiples are numbers that are exact multiples of two or more given numbers, sharing properties such as the cyclical appearance of intersection points and being exact multiples of the Least Common Multiple (LCM).
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
analyze
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
Can identify, list, and analyze common multiples of two or more numbers, apply properties of common multiples to solve real-world scheduling and synchronization problems, and connect them to LCM.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers the definition, properties (such as all common multiples being multiples of the LCM), and listing methods for two or three whole numbers within Class 7 arithmetic scope. Does not cover advanced modular arithmetic or abstract ring theory.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you and your friend are running on a circular athletic track at your school sports ground. You complete one full round every 4 minutes, while your friend completes one round every 6 minutes. Both of you start running together from the starting line at minute 0. When will both of you cross the starting line together again? To figure this out, we write down the minutes at which you cross (4, 8, 12, 16, 20...) and the minutes at which your friend crosses (6, 12, 18, 24...). We look for numbers that appear in both lists—these are our common multiples! Building on our prior knowledge of factors and multiples from Class 6, we discover that common multiples follow a fascinating pattern: every single common multiple is a multiple of the very first common multiple, which is the Least Common Multiple (LCM).

**Takeaway:** common multiples are the shared timetables; every one of them is the LCM times a counting number. **In the exam:** list forward from the LCM for the k-th meeting; test membership by exact division by both numbers. **Check yourself:** predict the runners' next three shared crossings from the first one alone. **Why students miss it:** they accept a number that clears only one timetable, or add instead of multiply for the k-th.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the definition of a common multiple of two numbers, say 3 and 4?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
A common multiple of 3 and 4 is a number that can be divided evenly by both 3 and 4 (or is found in the multiplication tables of both 3 and 4). If a student says "it is a number that divides both 3 and 4," they are confusing multiples with factors.

**A wrong answer (inverting the tables) reveals the student stood the definitions on their heads** - multiples climb the times tables and live above the numbers; factors burrow inside.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
True or False: Every common multiple of 4 and 6 is also a multiple of their Least Common Multiple (LCM), which is 12. Explain why.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
True. Every common multiple of two numbers is an integer multiple of their LCM because the set of common multiples of $a$ and $b$ is precisely the set of multiples of $\text{LCM}(a, b)$. If a student answers False, they do not understand the fundamental structural property connecting common multiples to the LCM.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Find the first three common multiples of 6 and 8.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: List the multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42, 48, 54, 72...
Step 2: List the multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48, 56, 64, 72...
Step 3: Identify the numbers common to both lists: 24, 48, 72.
Answer: The first three common multiples are 24, 48, and 72. If a student writes 12 or 16, they confused multiples with factors or miscalculated the multiplication tables.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
If the Least Common Multiple (LCM) of two numbers $x$ and $y$ is 15, what can you deduce about their 4th common multiple?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The 4th common multiple is $4 \times \text{LCM} = 4 \times 15 = 60$. Common multiples are generated by multiplying the LCM by 1, 2, 3, 4, etc. If a student answers 15 + 4 = 19 or multiplies 15 by another random number, they failed to recognize that common multiples are successive integer multiples of the LCM.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student claims that the number 50 is a common multiple of 4 and 5 because it is a multiple of 5. Evaluate this claim and correct any error.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The claim is false. While 50 is a multiple of 5 ($5 \times 10 = 50$), it is not a multiple of 4 because $50 \div 4 = 12$ with a remainder of 2. A common multiple must be a multiple of *both* numbers simultaneously. If a student agrees with the claim, they checked only one number instead of verifying divisibility by both.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a real-world scenario involving two repeating events (e.g., blinking lights or bus schedules) where finding the 3rd common multiple is necessary to solve a problem. Write the problem and its solution.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Example response: "Two electronic bells in a temple ring at intervals of 9 minutes and 12 minutes. They rang together at 8:00 AM. At what time will they ring together for the 3rd time after 8:00 AM?"
Solution: LCM of 9 and 12 is 36 minutes. The 1st time they ring together is 36 mins, 2nd time is 72 mins, and the 3rd time is $3 \times 36 = 108$ minutes (or 1 hour 48 minutes) later, which is at 9:48 AM. A poor answer fails to use the LCM or miscalculates the multiple step.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often confuse "common multiples" with "common factors" or assume that common multiples can be found by simply adding the two numbers together repeatedly without checking both sets of multiples. For example, when asked for common multiples of 3 and 5, a student might list factors like 1, or add $3+5=8, 8+5=13$, producing completely incorrect sequences.
## ON_FAILURE
Pause and have the student write out the multiplication tables side by side: for 3 (3, 6, 9, 12, 15...) and for 5 (5, 10, 15, 20...). Remind them that a "multiple" is the result of multiplying a whole number by 1, 2, 3, etc., and a *common* multiple must appear on *both* lists. Show them how circling matching numbers reveals the shared values.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Traffic lights at three different road crossings change after every 15 seconds, 20 seconds, and 30 seconds respectively. If they change simultaneously at 7:00 AM, find the next three times they will change together.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the given intervals for the traffic lights: 15 seconds, 20 seconds, and 30 seconds.
- Step 2: List the multiples for each interval to find the common multiples. Multiples of 15: 15, 30, 45, 60, 75, 90, 105, 120... Multiples of 20: 20, 40, 60, 80, 100, 120... Multiples of 30: 30, 60, 90, 120...
- Step 3: Identify the first common multiple (LCM), which is 60 seconds (1 minute).
- Step 4: Use the property that all common multiples are multiples of the LCM. The first three common multiples are $1 \times 60 = 60$ seconds, $2 \times 60 = 120$ seconds, and $3 \times 60 = 180$ seconds.
- Step 5: Convert the seconds into minutes and add to 7:00 AM. 60 seconds = 1 minute (7:01 AM); 120 seconds = 2 minutes (7:02 AM); 180 seconds = 3 minutes (7:03 AM).
REASONING: Traffic lights sync up at intervals that are common multiples of their individual change times. Finding the LCM gives the first sync time, and subsequent sync times are simply integer multiples of that LCM.
ANSWER: 7:01 AM, 7:02 AM, and 7:03 AM
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Two runner athletes, Priya and Amit, start running around a circular stadium track. Priya takes 12 minutes to complete one round, and Amit takes 18 minutes. If they start together at the starting line at 6:00 AM, after how many minutes will they meet again at the starting line for the second time?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Note Priya's time per round (12 minutes) and Amit's time per round (18 minutes).
- Step 2: List the multiples of 12: 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, 72...
- Step 3: List the multiples of 18: 18, 36, 54, 72, 90...
- Step 4: Find the common multiples in both lists: 36, 72, 108...
- Step 5: Since the question asks for the *second* time they meet, select the second common multiple, which is 72 minutes.
REASONING: Meeting at the starting line means both have completed a whole number of rounds in the exact same total elapsed time, making the time a common multiple of their individual lap times. The second meeting corresponds to the second common multiple.
ANSWER: After 72 minutes (or 1 hour and 12 minutes)
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A school library has books that need to be bundled. If they are bundled in stacks of 12 or stacks of 15, no books are left over. Find the smallest number of books greater than 100 that can be bundled this way.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand that the total number of books must be a common multiple of 12 and 15.
- Step 2: Find the LCM of 12 and 15 by listing multiples. Multiples of 12: 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, 72... Multiples of 15: 15, 30, 45, 60, 75... Thus, $\text{LCM}(12, 15) = 60$.
- Step 3: Generate successive common multiples using the property $M = k \times \text{LCM}$: $1 \times 60 = 60$, $2 \times 60 = 120$, $3 \times 60 = 180$.
- Step 4: Apply the condition that the number of books must be *greater than 100*.
- Step 5: Evaluate the generated common multiples (60, 120, 180...) to find the smallest one greater than 100, which is 120.
REASONING: Since the total must be a multiple of both 12 and 15, it must be a multiple of their LCM (60). We generate the sequence of common multiples (60, 120, 180) and select the first value strictly greater than 100.
ANSWER: 120 books
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Riya says: "Every common multiple of 4 and 6 is a multiple of 24 because $4 \times 6 = 24$." Analyze Riya's statement. Is she correct about all common multiples being multiples of 24? Explain why or why not, and state what the correct property is.
COMPETENCY: Error identification and conceptual justification
STEPS:
- Step 1: Examine Riya's claim that common multiples of 4 and 6 are multiples of 24.
- Step 2: List the first few common multiples of 4 and 6 by finding their LCM. Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24... Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24... Common multiples: 12, 24, 36, 48...
- Step 3: Test whether the first common multiple (12) is a multiple of 24. 12 is not a multiple of 24.
- Step 4: Identify Riya's mistake: She multiplied the two numbers ($4 \times 6 = 24$) instead of finding the Least Common Multiple (LCM), which is 12.
ANSWER: Riya is incorrect. While 24 is *a* common multiple, the *least* common multiple of 4 and 6 is 12. Therefore, all common multiples of 4 and 6 are multiples of 12 (12, 24, 36, 48...), not 24. Simply multiplying two numbers does not always give their LCM.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Three runners run on a circular track of length 400 metres, taking 40 seconds, 50 seconds, and 60 seconds respectively to complete one round. If they all start together from the starting point at 5:00 PM, calculate how many times they will meet at the starting point within the first 1 hour (3600 seconds).
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations and multi-step problem solving with LCM properties
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify individual completion times: 40s, 50s, 60s.
- Step 2: Find the LCM of 40, 50, and 60 to determine when they first meet at the start line. Multiples of 40: ..., 1200. Multiples of 50: ..., 1200. Multiples of 60: ..., 1200. $\text{LCM} = 1200$ seconds.
- Step 3: Understand that they meet at the starting point every 1200 seconds (which is 20 minutes).
- Step 4: Calculate the total time available: 1 hour = 3600 seconds.
- Step 5: Find how many 1200-second intervals fit into 3600 seconds: $3600 \div 1200 = 3$ times (at 1200s, 2400s, and 3600s). Note: If counting the start at 0s, state that explicitly, but standard interval counting yields 3 meetings after start.
ANSWER: They meet at the starting point 3 times within the 1-hour duration (at 20 mins, 40 mins, and 60 mins from start).
DIFFICULTY: hard
