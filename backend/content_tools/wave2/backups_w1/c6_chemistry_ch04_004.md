## ELI5
Why do old copper coins turn green while a gold ring stays shiny for years? Metals are not silent blocks; they react with air and moisture, each at its own speed. Iron rusts and crumbles, but some metals grow armour instead of wounds: copper grows a green skin called patina, aluminium grows an invisible, tough oxide layer — and both skins cling tight, blocking air from the metal underneath, so the damage stops there. Gold plays a different game: it is inert, refusing to react with air at all. That is why the ring needs no protection, while other metals must trust the shields they grow.

## DEFINITION
Metals react with air and moisture at different rates: some form protective layers (like aluminium and copper) that prevent further damage, while others, like gold, are inert and do not react at all.

## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever noticed that old copper coins turn green, or why a gold ring stays shiny even after years of wearing it? To begin, think of metals not just as solid blocks, but as materials that "breathe" when they touch air and water. While iron rusts and crumbles, other metals form a thin, tight "skin" on their surface. For copper, this skin is a green layer called patina; for aluminium, it is an invisible, tough layer of oxide. These layers act like a shield, stopping the air from reaching the metal underneath. Gold, however, is unique—it is "lazy" and refuses to react with the air at all, which is why it keeps its shine forever.
