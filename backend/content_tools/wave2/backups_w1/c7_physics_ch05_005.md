## ELI5
Stand in the afternoon sun on the school playground: your shadow stretches across the concrete - a dark patch made of absence. Three hires are needed: a light source, something opaque or translucent to block the beam, and a surface to receive the darkness. The shadow wears the blocker's outline but nothing of its colour or detail - shade, not portrait. Move the sun, and the shape stretches like taffy.

## DEFINITION
A shadow is a dark patch formed on a surface when an opaque or translucent object blocks the path of light, requiring a source of light, an opaque/translucent object, and a screen. It has no SI unit as it is a geometric region of absence of light, but its linear dimensions are measured in metres (m).

## MASTERY_CRITERIA

## WALKTHROUGH
Standing in the sunlight on your school playground in the afternoon, you notice a dark shape stretching out on the concrete pavement behind you. When you move closer to a wall with a desk lamp in your room, you can make the dark patch on the wall grow larger or smaller. To understand how this happens, we must recall from Class 6 that light travels in a straight line. When an opaque obstacle intercepts these straight light rays, the space behind the object receives no light, creating a silhouette. By observing how changing the distance between a torchlight, a ball, and a white screen alters the shadow, we discover the geometric rules governing shadow formation.

**Takeaway:** a shadow needs three hires - source, blocker, screen - and it records the blocker's outline, never its colours. **In the exam:** in prediction questions, move one of the three and reason the shadow's new size, shape and sharpness. **Check yourself:** stand in the walkthrough's afternoon sun and list the three employees working to paint your darkness. **Why students miss it:** students draw shadows in black with details - the flagged portrait habit. Shadows are silhouettes of blocked light, not paintings of the object. Name the three requirements first, and every shadow question starts on solid ground.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A student places a cricket ball 20 cm away from a small LED torch (acting as a point source) and projects its shadow on a wall that is 100 cm away from the torch. If the ball has a diameter of 7 cm, calculate the approximate magnification factor if the shadow size depends linearly on the ratio of screen distance to source-to-object distance.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Steps: 
1. Identify given values: Distance from source to object ($d_{so} = 20\text{ cm}$), distance from source to screen ($d_{ss} = 100\text{ cm}$), object size ($h_o = 7\text{ cm}$).
2. Identify the formula for linear magnification ($m$) or shadow size ($h_s$): $h_s = h_o \times \left(\frac{d_{ss}}{d_{so}}\right)$.
3. Substitute values with units: $h_s = 7\text{ cm} \times \left(\frac{100\text{ cm}}{20\text{ cm}}\right)$.
4. Compute and track unit cancellation: $h_s = 7\text{ cm} \times 5 = 35\text{ cm}$.
5. Verify physical reasonableness: The shadow size is larger than the object because the screen is further away than the object, which aligns with geometric optics.
Answer: 35 cm. A wrong answer reveals an error in setting up the ratio of distances or dividing by the wrong distance term.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
How does replacing a point source of light (like a small LED bulb) with an extended source of light (like a long tube light) affect the appearance of a shadow cast by an opaque sphere?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
An extended source creates a sharp central dark region called the umbra and a lighter outer fringe called the penumbra, whereas a point source creates only a sharp, uniform umbra. A wrong answer shows a failure to recognize that light rays from different parts of a large source overlap partially to create a faded outer region.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student claims that if you move a screen closer to an opaque object while keeping the light source fixed, the shadow will become larger. Evaluate this statement.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is incorrect because moving the screen closer to the object decreases the spread of the blocked light cone, thereby making the shadow smaller, not larger. A wrong answer indicates confusion between object-to-source distance and screen-to-object distance effects on shadow dimensions.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that the shadow of an object moves on its own or that translucent objects cast completely pitch-black shadows identical to opaque objects. They think that because light passes through translucent materials partially, it forms no shadow at all.
## ON_FAILURE
To fix this, take a translucent plastic scale and an opaque book. Hold each between a torch and a white wall in a darkened room. Observe that the book casts a dark, sharp shadow (umbra only), while the translucent scale casts a very faint, hazy shadow because some light rays pass through it. Conclude that translucent objects reduce the intensity of light rather than blocking it completely, resulting in a faint shadow.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A cylindrical water bottle of height 15 cm is placed 30 cm away from a point source of light on a desk. A vertical screen is placed 90 cm away from the light source. Calculate the height of the shadow formed on the screen.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given quantities: height of object ($h_o = 15\text{ cm}$), distance from source to object ($d_{so} = 30\text{ cm}$), distance from source to screen ($d_{ss} = 90\text{ cm}$).
- Step 2: Identify the geometric proportionality formula for similar triangles formed by light rays: $\frac{h_s}{h_o} = \frac{d_{ss}}{d_{so}}$, where $h_s$ is the shadow height.
- Step 3: Rearrange the formula to solve for shadow height: $h_s = h_o \times \left(\frac{d_{ss}}{d_{so}}\right)$.
- Step 4: Substitute the values with units and compute: $h_s = 15\text{ cm} \times \left(\frac{90\text{ cm}}{30\text{ cm}}\right) = 15\text{ cm} \times 3 = 45\text{ cm}$.
- Step 5: Verify the answer physically: The screen is three times as far from the source as the object, so the shadow height is three times the object height ($45\text{ cm}$), which is physically sound.
REASONING: Since light travels in straight lines, rays passing the top and bottom of the object form similar triangles with the rays hitting the screen, allowing us to use the ratio of distances to find the size of the shadow.
ANSWER: 45 cm
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: In a school science lab experiment, a student moves a screen from a distance of 60 cm to 120 cm away from a fixed point light source. If the initial shadow width of a rectangular block was 10 cm, determine the new width of the shadow, assuming the block's position from the light source remains unchanged at 40 cm.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given initial values: initial screen distance ($d_{ss1} = 60\text{ cm}$), initial shadow width ($w_1 = 10\text{ cm}$), object distance ($d_{so} = 40\text{ cm}$).
- Step 2: Identify new condition: new screen distance ($d_{ss2} = 120\text{ cm}$).
- Step 3: Apply the linear proportionality relation between screen distance and shadow size: $w_2 = w_1 \times \left(\frac{d_{ss2}}{d_{ss1}}\right)$.
- Step 4: Substitute values with units and compute: $w_2 = 10\text{ cm} \times \left(\frac{120\text{ cm}}{60\text{ cm}}\right) = 10\text{ cm} \times 2 = 20\text{ cm}$.
- Step 5: Verify physically: Doubling the distance of the screen from 60 cm to 120 cm doubles the width of the shadow from 10 cm to 20 cm.
REASONING: Shadow dimensions scale directly with the distance of the screen from the light source when the object position is held constant, because the light cone expands linearly.
ANSWER: 20 cm
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A spherical football of diameter 20 cm is placed between a point light source and a wall. The light source is 50 cm from the ball, and the ball is 100 cm from the wall (so the total distance from source to wall is 150 cm). Calculate the diameter of the circular shadow formed on the wall.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given quantities: object diameter ($d_o = 20\text{ cm}$), source-to-object distance ($d_{so} = 50\text{ cm}$), object-to-wall distance ($d_{ow} = 100\text{ cm}$).
- Step 2: Determine total source-to-screen (wall) distance: $d_{sw} = d_{so} + d_{ow} = 50\text{ cm} + 100\text{ cm} = 150\text{ cm}$.
- Step 3: Use the magnification ratio equation: $\text{Shadow Diameter } (D_s) = d_o \times \left(\frac{d_{sw}}{d_{so}}\right)$.
- Step 4: Substitute values with units and compute: $D_s = 20\text{ cm} \times \left(\frac{150\text{ cm}}{50\text{ cm}}\right) = 20\text{ cm} \times 3 = 60\text{ cm}$.
- Step 5: Verify the answer: Total distance is 150 cm and source-to-object is 50 cm, giving a ratio of 3. Multiplying object diameter (20 cm) by 3 yields 60 cm, which is correct and reasonable.
REASONING: To find shadow size with respect to a point source, the total distance from the light source to the screen must be divided by the distance from the source to the object, then multiplied by the object's actual size.
ANSWER: 60 cm
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: During a sunny afternoon in New Delhi, a vertical iron pole casts a shadow on the ground. A student notices that as the sun moves across the sky from morning to noon, the length of the shadow changes significantly. Explain why the shadow is longest in the early morning and shortest at noon.
COMPETENCY: Predicting outcomes from changing variables and interpreting physical situations
STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand the position of the light source (the sun) relative to the object (pole) at different times of day.
- Step 2: Recognize that in the morning, the sun is at a low angle to the horizon, causing light rays to strike the pole at a wide slant.
- Step 3: Recognize that at noon, the sun is directly overhead (high altitude), causing light rays to strike almost vertically.
- Step 4: Apply rectilinear propagation: low-angle rays travel a longer path across the ground before meeting the obstacle's blocked cone, creating a long shadow, whereas vertical rays create a very compact blocked region beneath the pole.
ANSWER: In the morning, the sun's low angle creates slanted rays that stretch the blocked region far across the ground, resulting in a long shadow. At noon, when the sun is overhead, the rays fall more vertically, compressing the blocked area and making the shadow shortest.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: An extended source of light (such as a tubelight) is used instead of a point source to cast a shadow of an opaque sphere onto a screen. A student observes two distinct regions in the shadow: a dark central patch surrounded by a lighter, hazy border. Identify these two regions, explain why they form, and describe what happens to the outer lighter region if the tubelight is moved closer to the sphere.
COMPETENCY: Analyzing complex physical phenomena and multi-variable reasoning
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the two regions: the dark central region is the umbra, and the lighter outer border is the penumbra.
- Step 2: Explain formation using light rays: an extended source has multiple points emitting light; some regions on the screen receive no light from any part of the source (umbra), while other regions receive light from only some parts of the source (penumbra).
- Step 3: Analyze the effect of moving the extended light source closer to the sphere: the angle subtended by the source increases, expanding the overlapping partial light regions.
- Step 4: Conclude the final change: the penumbra (lighter outer region) grows wider and fuzzier, while the umbra may shrink or change shape.
ANSWER: The dark central region is the umbra (where all light is blocked) and the hazy outer border is the penumbra (where light is only partially blocked from parts of the tubelight). Moving the tubelight closer increases the spread of incoming rays, causing the penumbra to become wider and more diffuse.
DIFFICULTY: hard
