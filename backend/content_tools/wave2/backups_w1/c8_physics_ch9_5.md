## ELI5
Earth's gravity is the reason our sky stays put. Every air molecule has weight, and the planet's pull presses the entire atmosphere against the surface like an invisible sea. Weak pull and the gas leaks away — the Moon, with scant gravity, long ago lost whatever air it had. The same pull that drops a mango from a tree holds a hundred kilometres of air in place; the falling fruit and the standing sky obey one law.

## DEFINITION
Gravity on Earth is the attractive force exerted by Earth's mass that pulls objects toward its center, giving them weight (W = mg, where g ≈ 9.8 m/s²) and preventing atmospheric gases from escaping into space by continuously pulling gas molecules downward.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
Student can explain why Earth's gravity holds the atmosphere while the Moon cannot, and can calculate the weight of an object given its mass using W = mg with correct SI units (newtons).
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers gravity as a force causing weight and retaining atmosphere; includes weight calculation W = mg. Does NOT cover Newton's universal law of gravitation (F = G Mm/r²), escape velocity calculations, or variation of g with altitude/latitude.
## WALKTHROUGH
Observe a falling leaf or a dropped pen in the classroom – it always moves downward toward the floor. This downward pull is gravity, a force Earth exerts on all objects with mass. Connect to Class 7 understanding of force and motion: gravity is the force that causes objects to accelerate downward at ~9.8 m/s². The same pull acts on invisible air molecules, giving them weight and keeping them from drifting into space. Introduce the key relationship: Weight (N) = mass (kg) × acceleration due to gravity (9.8 m/s²).

Takeaway: gravity gives every gas molecule weight and holds the atmosphere against the surface — a sufficiently strong pull keeps air for billions of years; a weak one loses it, which is why the airless Moon contrasts with Earth. Exam format: expect the why-Moon-has-no-atmosphere argument and straightforward weight calculations from mass using the gravitational constant. Verification beat: answer weight questions in newtons, never kilograms — the unit is the concept here. Why students miss it: they forget air has weight because it cannot be held — yet the entire column presses on every square metre, and gravity is the only thing keeping it piled up. The atmosphere is a stack a hundred kilometres tall, and gravity is the only thing holding the pile. Lose the pull, and the stack simply walks off the planet, molecule by molecule.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the SI unit of weight, and how does it differ from the SI unit of mass?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Weight is measured in newtons (N), mass in kilograms (kg). A wrong answer (e.g., "weight in kg") reveals the student confuses mass (amount of matter) with weight (force due to gravity), a fundamental misconception that prevents understanding why gravity's pull is a force.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why does Earth have a thick atmosphere while the Moon has almost none, even though both have gravity?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Earth's stronger gravity (due to larger mass) pulls gas molecules down hard enough to prevent most from reaching escape velocity; the Moon's weaker gravity cannot retain them. A wrong answer (e.g., "Moon has no air because it's smaller") shows the student doesn't connect gravitational strength to atmospheric retention – they state a fact without the causal mechanism.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A school bag has a mass of 5 kg. Calculate its weight on Earth. (Take g = 9.8 m/s²)
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Identify given: m = 5 kg, g = 9.8 m/s². Find: W.
Step 2: Formula W = m × g. Units: kg × m/s² = N.
Step 3: Substitute: W = 5 kg × 9.8 m/s².
Step 4: Compute: 5 × 9.8 = 49. Unit: kg·m/s² = N.
Step 5: Weight = 49 N. Reasonable: a 5 kg bag weighs ~5 kgf ≈ 49 N.
A wrong answer (e.g., 5 N or 49 kg) reveals either forgetting to multiply by g or confusing weight units with mass units.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare two planets: Planet A has mass 2× Earth's mass and radius 2× Earth's radius. Planet B has mass 4× Earth's mass and radius 2× Earth's radius. Which planet has stronger surface gravity? Explain without calculating exact g.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Surface gravity g ∝ M/R². Planet A: g_A ∝ 2M/(2R)² = 2M/4R² = 0.5 (M/R²). Planet B: g_B ∝ 4M/(2R)² = 4M/4R² = 1 (M/R²). Planet B has stronger gravity (same as Earth), Planet A has half. A wrong answer (e.g., "Planet A because it's more massive") shows the student ignores the radius squared dependence – they think only mass matters.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student claims: "If gravity pulls everything down, a helium balloon should fall too, but it rises. So gravity doesn't act on it." Evaluate this statement.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is incorrect. Gravity acts on the balloon (it has mass), but buoyant force (upward push from denser surrounding air) exceeds its weight, causing net upward motion. Gravity still pulls it down. A wrong evaluation (agreeing with the student) reveals the misconception that gravity only acts on falling objects, not understanding that multiple forces can act simultaneously.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple experiment using a kitchen scale, a balloon, and a pump to demonstrate that air has weight because of gravity.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Weigh an empty balloon on the scale (tare it). Inflate the balloon with the pump, tie it, and place it on the scale again. The reading increases, showing the added air has weight. A poor answer (e.g., "measure the balloon's size") fails to isolate the weight of air from the balloon's material, missing the key idea that gravity gives air measurable weight.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students believe gravity only acts on solid objects that visibly fall, not on gases. They think the atmosphere stays because "there's a lid on Earth" or "air is weightless." For example, they cannot explain why a helium balloon rises while a carbon-dioxide filled balloon falls, attributing it to "magic" rather than density differences in a gravitational field.
## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Show a digital balance reading zero with an empty balloon (tared). Step 2: Inflate the balloon, place it on the balance – reading increases (e.g., +2 g). Step 3: Explain: the extra mass is air; gravity pulls it down, registering as weight. Step 4: Compare with helium balloon (lighter than air) rising – gravity still pulls it, but buoyancy wins. This directly confronts "air is weightless" with measurable evidence.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A student of mass 40 kg stands on a weighing scale in a school in Delhi. What is the reading on the scale in newtons? (Take g = 9.8 m/s²)
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given quantities: mass m = 40 kg, acceleration due to gravity g = 9.8 m/s². Find weight W in newtons.
- Step 2: Recall the formula for weight: W = m × g. Check units: kg × m/s² = kg·m/s² = N (newton).
- Step 3: Substitute the values with units: W = 40 kg × 9.8 m/s².
- Step 4: Perform multiplication: 40 × 9.8 = 392. Track unit cancellation: kg × m/s² = N.
- Step 5: Verify reasonableness: 40 kg mass corresponds to about 40 kgf, and 1 kgf ≈ 9.8 N, so 40 × 9.8 ≈ 392 N is physically plausible for a student's weight.
REASONING: Weight is the force with which Earth pulls the student downward. The scale measures this force by compressing a spring or strain gauge calibrated in newtons. Using W = mg directly converts mass to the gravitational force acting on it.
ANSWER: 392 N
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A standard LPG cylinder for cooking has a mass of 14.2 kg when empty and 29.0 kg when full. Calculate the weight of the LPG gas alone inside the full cylinder. (g = 9.8 m/s²)
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given: mass of empty cylinder = 14.2 kg, mass of full cylinder = 29.0 kg. Need mass of gas = difference. g = 9.8 m/s².
- Step 2: Find mass of LPG gas: m_gas = 29.0 kg - 14.2 kg = 14.8 kg.
- Step 3: Use weight formula W = m × g. Units: kg × m/s² = N.
- Step 4: Substitute: W_gas = 14.8 kg × 9.8 m/s². Compute: 14.8 × 9.8 = 145.04. Unit: N.
- Step 5: Check reasonableness: LPG gas mass ~14.8 kg, weight ~145 N (about 14.8 kgf). This is the force gravity exerts on the gas, which the cylinder base must support.
REASONING: Gravity acts on the mass of the gas inside the cylinder, giving it weight. The cylinder must withstand this weight plus its own. Subtracting the empty mass isolates the gas mass, showing even invisible gases have weight due to gravity.
ANSWER: 145.04 N (or 145 N to three significant figures)
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A weather balloon carries instruments of total mass 2 kg. At launch, the balloon is filled with helium such that the total upward buoyant force is 30 N. Will the balloon rise or fall initially? Calculate the net force and initial acceleration. (g = 9.8 m/s²)
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given: total mass m = 2 kg, buoyant force F_b = 30 N upward, g = 9.8 m/s² downward. Find net force and acceleration.
- Step 2: Calculate weight of balloon system: W = m × g = 2 kg × 9.8 m/s² = 19.6 N downward.
- Step 3: Determine net force: upward positive. F_net = F_b - W = 30 N - 19.6 N = 10.4 N upward.
- Step 4: Since net force is upward, balloon will rise. Use Newton's second law: F_net = m × a → a = F_net / m.
- Step 5: Compute acceleration: a = 10.4 N / 2 kg = 5.2 m/s² upward. Units: N/kg = (kg·m/s²)/kg = m/s².
- Step 6: Interpret: The balloon accelerates upward at 5.2 m/s² initially. As it rises, buoyancy decreases (air density drops), so acceleration changes.
REASONING: This combines gravity (weight) with buoyancy – a force from pressure differences in the atmosphere (held by gravity). The net force determines motion. Gravity still acts (weight), but buoyancy overcomes it. This connects to Class 7 convection (c7_physics_ch03_004) where density differences cause fluid motion.
ANSWER: Net force = 10.4 N upward; initial acceleration = 5.2 m/s² upward.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: An astronaut measures the weight of a rock on Earth as 98 N. She takes the same rock to the Moon where gravity is 1/6th of Earth's. What will be the rock's mass and weight on the Moon? Explain why mass doesn't change but weight does.
COMPETENCY: Interpreting physical situations using formulas
STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand mass is intrinsic amount of matter (kg), independent of location. Weight is force due to gravity (N), depends on g.
- Step 2: Find mass from Earth weight: m = W_earth / g_earth = 98 N / 9.8 m/s² = 10 kg.
- Step 3: Moon's g = 9.8/6 ≈ 1.63 m/s². Mass remains 10 kg.
- Step 4: Calculate Moon weight: W_moon = m × g_moon = 10 kg × 1.63 m/s² = 16.3 N.
- Step 5: Interpret: Mass unchanged because rock's matter is same. Weight changed because gravitational pull is weaker.
ANSWER: Mass = 10 kg (same on Moon); Weight on Moon = 16.3 N (≈ 1/6th of Earth weight).
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Earth's atmosphere is 78% nitrogen (N₂, molar mass 28 g/mol) and 21% oxygen (O₂, 32 g/mol). Hydrogen (H₂, 2 g/mol) is almost absent. Using the concept that gravity holds gases, explain why light gases escape more easily. If Earth's gravity were halved, predict what would happen to the nitrogen and oxygen over geological time.
COMPETENCY: Predicting outcomes from changing variables
STEPS:
- Step 1: Recall gas molecules move with a range of speeds (kinetic theory). Lighter molecules have higher average speeds at same temperature.
- Step 2: Gravity pulls all molecules down. Escape requires speed > escape velocity. Lighter molecules more likely to exceed escape velocity.
- Step 3: Hydrogen (2 g/mol) moves ~√(28/2) ≈ 3.7 times faster than nitrogen on average, so it escapes easily. Nitrogen and oxygen are heavy enough to be retained.
- Step 4: If gravity halved, escape velocity decreases by √2 ≈ 1.4 times. More nitrogen/oxygen molecules would exceed it.
- Step 5: Predict: Over millions of years, significant fraction of N₂ and O₂ would escape, thinning atmosphere drastically, possibly making Earth uninhabitable.
ANSWER: Light gases have higher thermal speeds, so more molecules reach escape velocity. With half gravity, escape velocity drops, causing even N₂ and O₂ to gradually leak into space, leading to atmospheric loss.
DIFFICULTY: hard
