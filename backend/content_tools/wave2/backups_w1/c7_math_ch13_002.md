## ELI5
A cricket captain stares at ten messy scores - 12, 45, 12, 30, 45, 12, 60, 30, 45, 12 - and needs to know which score repeats most. The frequency table is the tally clerk: each distinct value gets a row, every sighting adds a stroke, strokes bundle in fives, and the bundles convert to numbers. Finish by adding the frequency column - it must equal the total number of observations, or somebody got counted twice or missed entirely.

## DEFINITION
Collecting and organising raw data into a structured frequency table involves systematically counting how many times each distinct data value appears using tally marks, allowing us to summarize large datasets clearly.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
Can construct a complete frequency table from an unstructured list of 20+ data values, accurately tally occurrences, compute numerical frequencies, and verify that the sum of all frequencies equals the total number of data items.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers raw data collection, tally mark creation, individual frequency tables for discrete numerical or categorical data, and checking total frequency sums. It does NOT cover grouped class intervals (which are introduced in higher grades) or cumulative frequencies.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are the captain of your school cricket team, and you recorded the runs scored by players in the last 10 matches: 12, 45, 12, 30, 45, 12, 60, 30, 45, 12. Looking at this messy list, it is hard to tell how many times a score of 12 actually happened without counting carefully. To make sense of this information, we build upon what we learned in basic data handling by systematically organizing items into categories and counting them using tally marks. We list each unique value, draw a vertical stroke (tally) for each appearance—crossing every fifth tally horizontally—and then write the total count, known as the frequency, in a dedicated column.

**Takeaway:** a frequency table is a tally clerk: rows for distinct values, strokes for sightings, numbers for totals. **In the exam:** tally in bundles of five, convert each bundle to a number, and total the frequency column as your audit. **Check yourself:** re-tally the captain's ten cricket scores and confirm the column sums to exactly ten. **Why students miss it:** students tally loosely - a missed sighting here, a double count there - and the table quietly lies. The sum-check is the seatbelt: frequencies that do not add to the total observations announce the error before any conclusion rides on them. Bundles of five keep even long lists countable at a glance, which is why examiners award method marks for neat tallies.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The correct answer is to keep an accurate, organized count of each individual data value as you scan through a messy list without missing or double-counting items. A student who answers that tally marks are used to calculate the final mean or average shows a confusion between data organization and representative values. ## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
When recording tally marks for a count of 7, how should the vertical strokes be grouped according to standard convention?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The correct answer is that the first 5 strokes should be grouped together with the fifth stroke crossing the previous four diagonally (HI), followed by 2 separate vertical strokes (I I), making it HI I I. A student who draws 7 straight vertical lines side-by-side (IIIIIII) fails to use standard grouping, which makes it very difficult to count large frequencies quickly. Misinterpreting tally groupings leads directly to counting errors in the frequency column.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often fail to cross off or check off raw data items as they tally them, leading to missed entries or double-counting. For example, when given a list of 20 numbers, a student might count only 18 tallies because they skipped numbers or lost track mid-way, resulting in a frequency sum that does not match the total data size.
## ON_FAILURE
Remind the student to use a pencil to lightly cross out each number in the raw list the exact moment a corresponding tally mark is drawn. Show them how to write the distinct values in ascending order first before tallying to prevent scattering counts. Emphasize that the final step must always be adding all frequency values together and matching that sum to the total count of items in the original list.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A teacher records the weekly pocket money (in rupees) of 15 students in a Class 7 section: 50, 100, 50, 200, 100, 50, 100, 50, 150, 200, 50, 100, 150, 50, 100. Construct a frequency table to organise this data.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify and list all unique data values in ascending order to create the table's first column: ₹50, ₹100, ₹150, ₹200.
- Step 2: Go through the raw data list item by item, crossing off each number and recording a corresponding tally mark in the Tally column for its respective value.
- Step 3: Count the tally marks for each distinct value to determine the frequency: ₹50 appears 6 times (HI I), ₹100 appears 5 times (HI), ₹150 appears 2 times (II), and ₹200 appears 2 times (II).
- Step 4: Add all the frequencies together to verify the total: $6 + 5 + 2 + 2 = 15$, which matches the total number of students surveyed.
- Step 5: Present the final frequency table clearly with columns for Pocket Money (₹), Tally Marks, and Frequency.

REASONING: Organizing raw numbers into ascending order prevents confusion and ensures no unique values are missed. Tally marks provide an intermediate visual grouping that makes counting large quantities error-free before writing the final numeric frequency.

ANSWER: Frequency table: ₹50 (Frequency: 6), ₹100 (Frequency: 5), ₹150 (Frequency: 2), ₹200 (Frequency: 2); Total = 15.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A library assistant records the favorite book genres of 20 students as: Mystery, Sports, Mystery, Fantasy, Fantasy, Sports, Mystery, Science, Fantasy, Mystery, Sports, Science, Fantasy, Mystery, Sports, Fantasy, Mystery, Science, Fantasy, Sports. Construct a frequency table for this categorical data.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify all unique categories (genres) present in the dataset: Mystery, Sports, Fantasy, Science.
- Step 2: Iterate through each of the 20 entries in the raw data, tallying each vote under its respective genre category.
- Step 3: Count the tally marks for each genre: Mystery has 6 votes (HI I), Sports has 5 votes (HI), Fantasy has 6 votes (HI I), Science has 3 votes (III).
- Step 4: Calculate the sum of all frequencies to check accuracy: $6 + 5 + 6 + 3 = 20$, matching the total number of students.
- Step 5: Construct the final table showing Genre, Tally Marks, and Frequency columns.

REASONING: Categorical data follows the exact same organizational principles as numerical data, where text labels take the place of numbers, and tally marks are accumulated across categories.

ANSWER: Mystery: 6, Sports: 5, Fantasy: 6, Science: 3; Total = 20.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A sports instructor notes the number of goals scored by a football team in 18 matches: 2, 0, 1, 3, 2, 4, 1, 0, 2, 1, 3, 2, 1, 0, 2, 3, 1, 2. Construct the frequency table, find which goal count has the highest frequency (mode), and verify the total number of matches.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify all unique goal counts in ascending order: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4.
- Step 2: Tally each occurrence from the 18 matches: 
  - 0 goals: III (Frequency: 3)
  - 1 goal: HI (Frequency: 5)
  - 2 goals: HI II (Frequency: 7)
  - 3 goals: III (Frequency: 3)
  - 4 goals: I (Frequency: 1)
- Step 3: Compute the sum of frequencies to verify data integrity: $\sum f = 3 + 5 + 7 + 3 + 1 = 19$. Wait! Let us re-verify the raw data count. Let's recount the raw data list: 
  - 2, 0, 1, 3, 2, 4, 1, 0, 2, 1, 3, 2, 1, 0, 2, 3, 1, 2. 
  - Total items in raw list = 18. 
  - Let's check our tallies: 0 appears 3 times; 1 appears 5 times; 2 appears 6 times (let's recount 2s: 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 2 -> 6 times); 3 appears 3 times; 4 appears 1 times. Sum = $3 + 5 + 6 + 3 + 1 = 18$.
- Step 4: Identify the value with the highest frequency: 2 goals has a frequency of 7, which is the maximum.
- Step 5: Format the final response showing the complete table, verification sum of 18, and the identification of 2 goals as the most frequent outcome.

REASONING: Multi-step data problems require rigorous verification at every stage. Checking the sum of frequencies against the exact count of raw data items ensures that tallying errors are caught before drawing conclusions.

ANSWER: Frequency table: 0 goals (freq 3), 1 goal (freq 5), 2 goals (freq 6), 3 goals (freq 3), 4 goals (freq 1); Total = 18; Most frequent score = 2 goals.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A survey of 30 households in a neighborhood recorded the number of children per family: 1, 2, 0, 2, 3, 1, 1, 2, 0, 1, 2, 4, 1, 2, 3, 0, 1, 2, 1, 2, 3, 1, 0, 2, 1, 2, 3, 1, 2, 1. 
Part A: Construct the frequency table for this dataset. 
Part B: A student analyzes your table and states that because the number 1 has the highest frequency, more than half of the households surveyed have 1 child. Is the student's conclusion mathematically sound? Justify your answer using the frequency values.
COMPETENCY: Interpreting frequency tables and logical deduction
STEPS:
- Step 1: List unique values (0, 1, 2, 3, 4) and tally the 30 entries to build the complete frequency table.
- Step 2: Calculate individual frequencies: 0 children (freq 4), 1 child (freq 12), 2 children (freq 10), 3 children (freq 3), 4 children (freq 1). Verify sum: $4 + 12 + 10 + 3 + 1 = 30$.
- Step 3: Evaluate the student's claim: frequency of 1 child is 12. Compare this to half of the total households ($30 / 2 = 15$).
- Step 4: Formulate the justification: since 12 is less than 15, households with 1 child make up less than half of the total surveyed, making the student's conclusion false.
ANSWER: Part A: Frequency table shows 0: 4, 1: 12, 2: 10, 3: 3, 4: 1 (Total = 30). Part B: The student's conclusion is incorrect because while 1 child is the most frequent response (mode = 12), 12 is less than half of 30 (which is 15). Therefore, fewer than half the households have 1 child.
DIFFICULTY: hard
