## ELI5
Water freezes to ice and melts back — same water, so a physical change. Wood burns to ash and refuses to become wood again — new substance, so a chemical change. The sorting question never changes: is the substance still itself? If undoing the process returns the original stuff, it is physical; if something genuinely new has appeared, it is chemical. And chemistry leaves clues at the scene: unexpected bubbles of gas, a colour you never mixed in, heat released though you added none. Spot one of those signals and you can be fairly sure a new material is being born in front of you.

## DEFINITION
A physical change alters the state or appearance of a substance without creating a new material, whereas a chemical change transforms a substance into an entirely new material with different properties.

## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever wondered why water turns into ice but paper turns into ash when burnt? To decide if a change is physical or chemical, first ask: "Is the substance still the same thing?" If you can turn ice back into water, that's physical—no new stuff was made. If you burn wood and get ash, you can't turn that ash back into wood; that’s a chemical change because a new substance appeared. Look for clues like bubbles (gas), unexpected color changes, or release of heat, which are strong signals that a chemical reaction has taken place.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Rusting is chemical because the iron reacts with oxygen and moisture to form a new substance (iron oxide) with properties distinct from iron. A student who thinks it is physical because "it's just a color change" ignores that the substance itself has been fundamentally altered.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often confuse "change in state" with "chemical change." For example, they might think melting wax is chemical because the shape changes. They believe any visible change to an object implies a chemical reaction.
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student: "If you melt this wax, can you freeze it back into a candle?" When they say yes, explain: "Since you can get the original material back, it hasn't turned into a new substance." Then, compare it to burning a wick: "Can you turn that smoke and ash back into a string?" Use this "reversibility" test to solidify the distinction.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: You have a block of butter. You melt it in a pan. Then, you accidentally leave the pan on the heat, and the butter turns black and starts smoking. Identify the changes.
STEPS:
- 1: Identify the first change: Melting butter.
- 2: Check if new substance is formed: The melted butter is still butter.
- 3: Identify the second change: Heating butter until it turns black.
- 4: Check if new substance is formed: Black charred butter is not the same as yellow butter.
- 5: Conclusion: Melting is physical; charring is chemical.
REASONING: The first step only changes the state of matter, while the second step causes a chemical breakdown of the fats.
ANSWER: Melting is a physical change; charring is a chemical change.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: An iron gate is painted. Later, the paint peels off, and you see brown flakes underneath. Identify the change related to the brown flakes.
STEPS:
- 1: Identify the observation: Brown flakes appear on iron.
- 2: Analyze the material: Is the brown flake iron? No, it is a different substance.
- 3: Determine the process: The iron reacted with the environment.
- 4: Classification: Formation of a new substance is a hallmark of a chemical change.
REASONING: Since the iron transformed into a new brittle substance (rust), it fits the definition of a chemical change.
ANSWER: This is a chemical change.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A student mixes 50g of salt into 200ml of water. They then heat the solution until all water evaporates. What changes occurred?
STEPS:
- 1: Analyze step 1 (mixing): Did the salt change its chemical identity? No.
- 2: Analyze step 2 (evaporation): Did the water change its chemical identity? No, it became steam.
- 3: Evaluate reversibility: Can we separate the salt from the water? Yes.
- 4: Conclusion: Since no new substance was formed, both steps are physical.
REASONING: Mixing and separation of mixtures are classic physical changes because the components retain their properties.
ANSWER: Both are physical changes.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You observe a candle burning. Explain why the melting of the wax is a physical change while the burning of the wick is a chemical change.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- 1: Define physical change (no new substance).
- 2: Define chemical change (new substance formed).
- 3: Apply to wax: Wax melts and re-solidifies; it is still wax.
- 4: Apply to wick: Burning creates smoke and ash; it is no longer the original wick material.
ANSWER: Melting is physical because the wax remains wax. Burning is chemical because it creates ash and gases, which are entirely new substances.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A classmate claims that because boiling water creates bubbles, it must be a chemical change. How do you correct this using the "new substance" test?
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction
STEPS:
- 1: Identify the student's assumption: Bubbles = chemical change.
- 2: Explain the physical process: The bubbles are just water vapor (gas state).
- 3: Test for new substance: Is water vapor still water? Yes (H2O).
- 4: Final judgment: Since it is still water, it is physical.
ANSWER: You explain that bubbles don't always mean a chemical reaction; here, they are just water changing from liquid to gas. Because the substance is still water, it is a physical change.
DIFFICULTY: hard
