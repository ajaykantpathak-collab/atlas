## ELI5
Four kilograms of potatoes, three-quarters cooked into evening snacks: the phrase 'fraction of a quantity' is a multiplication order. Translate the sentence into arithmetic, convert any mixed numbers to improper fractions, cross-cancel, and answer with units. Read the question twice - 'how many cows' and 'how many are not cows' are different destinies for the same correct product. And keep your fraction sense: multiplying by an improper fraction grows the amount, by a proper fraction shrinks it.

## DEFINITION
Solving word problems involving fraction multiplication requires translating real-world scenarios into mathematical expressions where a fractional part of a quantity, fraction, or mixed number is computed by multiplying the numerators together and the denominators together.

## MASTERY_CRITERIA

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers single and multi-step word problems involving multiplication of proper fractions, improper fractions, and mixed numbers in real-life contexts. Does not cover fraction division word problems or complex algebraic word problems with fractions.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you went to the local vegetable market with your mother and bought 4 kilograms of potatoes, but you only used $\frac{3}{4}$ of them to make evening snacks. How do you find out the exact weight of the potatoes you used? In Class 6, you learned how to multiply two fractions by multiplying numerators and denominators. Now, in Class 7, we take that skill further by extracting mathematical clues from descriptive sentences to decide *when* and *what* to multiply. The key insight is that the word "of" in mathematics almost always translates to multiplication ($\times$), telling us we need to find a fraction of a given amount. By identifying the total quantity and the fractional part taken, we set up our multiplication expression, simplify before multiplying, and attach the correct real-world unit to our final answer.

**Takeaway:** fraction-of word problems are multiplication orders: part times whole, mixed numbers converted, units kept. **In the exam:** underline the fraction, underline the whole, multiply, then re-read the question before answering. **Check yourself:** solve the potato-snack weight and state the unit in the final line. **Why students miss it:** they answer the calculated quantity instead of the question actually asked. Circle the units in the question, and carry them alive into the final line of the answer.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
A recipe calls for $2\frac{1}{2}$ cups of flour. If Rohan wants to make $\frac{3}{4}$ of the recipe, what operation should he use to find the amount of flour needed, and why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Rohan should use multiplication ($\frac{3}{4} \times 2\frac{1}{2}$). A wrong answer like addition or division shows the student does not understand that taking a fractional part ("$\frac{3}{4}$ of the recipe") means multiplying the fraction by the total quantity required.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Priya says that multiplying a number by $\frac{4}{3}$ will always result in a number smaller than the original number because $\frac{4}{3}$ is a fraction. Is she correct? Explain briefly.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Priya is incorrect. Because $\frac{4}{3}$ is an improper fraction (its numerator is greater than its denominator), its value is greater than $1$. Multiplying any positive number by a fraction greater than $1$ results in a *larger* product, not a smaller one. A wrong answer reveals confusion between proper fractions (which decrease numbers when multiplied) and improper fractions.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A ribbon is $5\frac{1}{4}$ metres long. If Sunita cuts off $\frac{2}{3}$ of the ribbon for a craft project, what is the length of the piece she cut off? Give your answer as a mixed number in simplest form.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
First, convert $5\frac{1}{4}$ to an improper fraction: $\frac{21}{4}$. Next, set up the multiplication: $\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{21}{4}$. Multiply numerators and denominators: $\frac{2 \times 21}{3 \times 4} = \frac{42}{12}$. Simplify by dividing numerator and denominator by $6$: $\frac{7}{2}$, which converts to $3\frac{1}{2}$ metres. A wrong answer often indicates failure to convert mixed numbers to improper fractions before multiplying or forgetting to simplify.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the two word problems: Problem A asks to find $\frac{3}{5}$ of $40$ litres of milk. Problem B asks to find how many $\frac{3}{5}$ litre bottles can be filled from $40$ litres of milk. Explain why they require different operations even though the same numbers are given.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Problem A asks for a fractional part *of* a total quantity, which requires multiplication ($\frac{3}{5} \times 40 = 24$ litres). Problem B asks how many times a smaller portion fits *into* a total quantity, which requires division ($40 \div \frac{3}{5} = \frac{200}{3}$ bottles). A wrong answer reveals a failure to interpret the verbal structure of grouping versus fractional scaling.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate the following student work for this problem: "A farm has $60$ animals. $\frac{2}{3}$ of them are cows. How many cows are not cows?" Student's work: $60 \times \frac{2}{3} = 40$. Answer: $40$ cows. Is the student completely correct? Justify your evaluation.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The student calculated the number of cows correctly ($60 \times \frac{2}{3} = 40$), but they did not answer the actual question asked, which was how many animals are *not* cows. The student needed to perform a final subtraction step: $60 - 40 = 20$ animals that are not cows. A wrong evaluation shows the student stopped reading the problem carefully after finding the first intermediate product.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a word problem about buying fruits at an Indian vegetable market where the solution requires multiplying a mixed number by a proper fraction, and provide its correct answer.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good response should feature a realistic scenario, such as: "Amit bought $3\frac{1}{2}$ kg of apples. If $\frac{3}{7}$ of the apples were red and the rest were green, how many kilograms of red apples did Amit buy?" The correct calculation is $\frac{7}{2} \times \frac{3}{7} = \frac{3}{2} = 1\frac{1}{2}$ kg. A poor answer would use numbers that do not fit real-world contexts or fail to use multiplication.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students frequently multiply mixed numbers directly by multiplying the whole numbers together and the fractions together separately (e.g., $2 \times \frac{1}{3}$ and $3 \times \frac{1}{4}$), rather than first converting mixed numbers into improper fractions. For example, when solving $2\frac{1}{3} \times 1\frac{1}{4}$, a student might incorrectly calculate $(2 \times 1) + (\frac{1}{3} \times \frac{1}{4}) = 2\frac{1}{12}$ instead of converting to $\frac{7}{3} \times \frac{5}{4} = \frac{35}{12} = 2\frac{11}{12}$.
## ON_FAILURE
If a student makes this error, pause and remind them that mixed numbers represent a single combined quantity. Ask them to write the mixed number as an addition sentence first ($2\frac{1}{3} = 2 + \frac{1}{3}$) and explain why distributive property requires multiplying every part, or simply guide them to always convert mixed numbers into improper fractions before performing any multiplication. Show them side-by-side that the direct product method yields a drastically wrong result compared to the improper fraction method.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Rahul has a garden plot of land with an area of $12\frac{1}{2}$ square metres. He plants vegetables in $\frac{3}{5}$ of the plot. What is the area of the land (in square metres) used for growing vegetables?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the total quantity and the fractional part given in the word problem. Total area = $12\frac{1}{2}\text{ m}^2$, Fraction planted with vegetables = $\frac{3}{5}$.
- Step 2: Set up the mathematical expression for finding a fraction of a quantity. Expression = $\frac{3}{5} \times 12\frac{1}{2}$.
- Step 3: Convert the mixed number into an improper fraction. $12\frac{1}{2} = \frac{12 \times 2 + 1}{2} = \frac{25}{2}$.
- Step 4: Substitute the improper fraction into the expression and multiply numerators and denominators: $\frac{3}{5} \times \frac{25}{2} = \frac{3 \times 25}{5 \times 2} = \frac{75}{10}$. Alternatively, cross-cancel the $5$ and $25$ to get $\frac{3 \times 5}{1 \times 2} = \frac{15}{2}$.
- Step 5: Convert the resulting improper fraction back to a mixed number with units: $\frac{15}{2} = 7\frac{1}{2}\text{ m}^2$.
REASONING: The word "of" in the problem statement indicates that we need to find a fractional part of the total area, which requires multiplication. Converting mixed numbers to improper fractions ensures we can apply the standard numerator-times-numerator rule without calculation errors.
ANSWER: 7\frac{1}{2}\text{ m}^2
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A milk cooperative collects $45$ litres of milk in a morning shift. If $\frac{4}{9}$ of the total milk is supplied to local tea stalls and the rest is processed into paneer, how many litres of milk are processed into paneer?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify what is given and what is asked. Total milk = $45\text{ litres}$, Fraction supplied to tea stalls = $\frac{4}{9}$. We need to find the milk *processed into paneer* (which is the remaining fraction).
- Step 2: Determine the fraction of milk left for paneer. Whole is $1$, so the remaining fraction is $1 - \frac{4}{9} = \frac{9}{9} - \frac{4}{9} = \frac{5}{9}$.
- Step 3: Set up the multiplication expression to find $\frac{5}{9}$ of $45\text{ litres}$. Expression = $\frac{5}{9} \times 45$.
- Step 4: Write the whole number as a fraction with denominator $1$ and multiply: $\frac{5}{9} \times \frac{45}{1} = \frac{5 \times 45}{9 \times 1} = \frac{225}{9}$. Simplify by dividing $45$ by $9$ to get $5$, giving $5 \times 5 = 25$.
- Step 5: State the final answer clearly with appropriate units: $25\text{ litres}$.
REASONING: By first determining that the remaining fraction for paneer is $\frac{5}{9}$, we avoid doing an extra subtraction step at the end. Multiplying this fraction directly by the total volume gives the exact quantity of milk processed into paneer.
ANSWER: 25\text{ litres}
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Sneha bought $3\frac{1}{3}$ kg of sweets for a family gathering. Her younger brother ate $\frac{1}{4}$ of the sweets before the party started. Later, during the party, the guests ate $\frac{3}{5}$ of the *remaining* sweets. How many kilograms of sweets are left over after the party?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify initial quantity of sweets = $3\frac{1}{3}\text{ kg} = \frac{10}{3}\text{ kg}$.
- Step 2: Calculate the fraction and amount of sweets remaining after her brother ate $\frac{1}{4}$. Fraction left = $1 - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{4}$. Amount left = $\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{10}{3} = \frac{30}{12} = \frac{5}{2}\text{ kg}$ ($2\frac{1}{2}\text{ kg}$).
- Step 3: Calculate the amount of sweets eaten by guests, which is $\frac{3}{5}$ of the *remaining* sweets ($\frac{5}{2}\text{ kg}$). Expression = $\frac{3}{5} \times \frac{5}{2}$.
- Step 4: Multiply and simplify: $\frac{3 \times 5}{5 \times 2} = \frac{15}{10} = \frac{3}{2}\text{ kg}$ ($1\frac{1}{2}\text{ kg}$) eaten by guests.
- Step 5: Subtract the sweets eaten by guests from the amount remaining after the brother ate to find the final leftover sweets: $\frac{5}{2} - \frac{3}{2} = \frac{2}{2} = 1\text{ kg}$.
REASONING: This multi-step problem requires sequential fraction multiplication where the base quantity changes after the first event. We must calculate the new remaining base before applying the second fraction multiplier.
ANSWER: 1\text{ kg}
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Two friends, Anita and Akbar, are solving the problem: "Find the product of $\frac{3}{4}$ and $8\frac{1}{3}$." 
Anita converts $8\frac{1}{3}$ to $\frac{25}{3}$ and calculates $\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{25}{3} = \frac{25}{4} = 6\frac{1}{4}$. 
Akbar uses the distributive property: $\frac{3}{4} \times (8 + \frac{1}{3}) = (\frac{3}{4} \times 8) + (\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{1}{3}) = 6 + \frac{1}{4} = 6\frac{1}{4}$. 
Are both methods mathematically valid? Justify your reasoning by explaining why Akbar's method works.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying alternative mathematical methods
STEPS:
- Step 1: Analyze Anita's method (converting mixed number to improper fraction and multiplying).
- Step 2: Analyze Akbar's method (splitting the mixed number into whole and fractional parts and applying the distributive property of multiplication over addition).
- Step 3: Compute Akbar's steps: $\frac{3}{4} \times 8 = 6$, and $\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{1}{3} = \frac{3}{12} = \frac{1}{4}$, then sum them to get $6\frac{1}{4}$.
- Step 4: Compare results and conclude that both are correct because multiplication distributes over addition.
ANSWER: Yes, both methods are completely valid. Anita's method converts the mixed number to an improper fraction first, while Akbar's method uses the distributive property of multiplication over addition: $a \times (b + c) = (a \times b) + (a \times c)$. Both arrive at the same correct result of $6\frac{1}{4}$.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A school sports committee has a budget of ₹$12,000$ for buying athletic equipment. They spend $\frac{3}{8}$ of the total budget on footballs and cricket bats. Out of the *remaining* budget, they spend $\frac{2}{5}$ on badminton kits. How much money (in rupees) is left in the budget after both purchases?
COMPETENCY: Translating multi-step real-world financial situations into fraction multiplication sequences
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify total budget = ₹$12,000$.
- Step 2: Calculate money spent on footballs and cricket bats ($\frac{3}{8}$ of ₹$12,000$) and determine the remaining budget fraction ($1 - \frac{3}{8} = \frac{5}{8}$).
- Step 3: Calculate the remaining budget amount: $\frac{5}{8} \times 12000 = \frac{60000}{8} = \text{₹}7,500$.
- Step 4: Calculate the money spent on badminton kits, which is $\frac{2}{5}$ of the *remaining* budget ($\text{₹}7,500$): $\frac{2}{5} \times 7500 = \frac{15000}{5} = \text{₹}3,000$.
- Step 5: Subtract this expenditure from the remaining budget to find the final balance: $\text{₹}7,500 - \text{₹}3,000 = \text{₹}4,500$.
ANSWER: The amount of money left in the budget after both purchases is ₹$4,500$.
DIFFICULTY: hard
