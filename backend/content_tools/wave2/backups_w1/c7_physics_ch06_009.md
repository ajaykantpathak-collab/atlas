## ELI5
Watch the moon for a few weeks and it changes costume: thin crescent, half, full, and back to nothing. The phases are the sunlit half of the moon presented at changing angles as it circles Earth - one complete costume cycle, the lunar month, taking about 29.5 days. The moon never changes shape; only our view of its lit side does. Same actor, rotating stage, endless new silhouettes.

## DEFINITION
The phases of the moon are the different shapes of the illuminated portion of the Moon as seen from Earth, measured in the repeating cycle of the lunar month (approximately 29.5 days), caused by changing angles between the Earth, Moon, and Sun.

## WALKTHROUGH
Look up at the night sky over a few weeks, and you will notice that the Moon changes its visible shape from a thin crescent to a bright full circle and back again, just like a glowing streetlamp shifting its brightness from different angles. To understand why this happens, we must remember that the Moon does not produce its own light; it only reflects sunlight like a giant mirror in space. As the Moon revolves around the Earth, the half facing the Sun is always lit, but our viewpoint from Earth changes. By tracking how much of that lit half we can see each night, we map out the lunar cycle.

**Takeaway:** moon phases are viewpoints, not shape-shifts: the lit half is constant; Earth's changing angle to it writes the crescent-to-full calendar. **In the exam:** in diagram answers, place sun, moon and Earth for each phase, then shade the half we actually see. **Check yourself:** watch the walkthrough's thin crescent grow and say which side of the moon is lit and where the sun must be sitting. **Why students miss it:** students blame shadows for the phases - the flagged confusion with eclipses. Phases are geometry of illumination, not Earth's shadow at work. Separate the two phenomena once, and the moon's calendar becomes simple, predictable arithmetic.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that the phases of the Moon are caused by the shadow of the Earth falling on the Moon throughout the month. They confuse the monthly cycle of phases with a lunar eclipse, failing to realize that the Earth's shadow is far too narrow and pointed away from the Moon during most of its orbit to create the crescent and quarter phases.
## ON_FAILURE
To correct this, take a small globe or ball (representing the Moon) and a torch (representing the Sun) in a dark room. Have the student stand in place holding the ball while you shine the torch from one side. Walk the ball in a circle around the student's head, and have the student observe how different amounts of the lit surface become visible from their viewpoint *without* the student's head ever casting a shadow on the ball during the crescent or quarter positions.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A satellite orbiting the Moon takes roughly 7 days to travel from the New Moon position to the First Quarter position. If the total lunar cycle takes 29.5 days, what fraction of the total cycle has been completed, expressed in days and as a percentage?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify given time elapsed ($t = 7\text{ days}$) and total cycle duration ($T = 29.5\text{ days}$).
- step 2: Set up the ratio of elapsed time to total cycle time: $\text{Fraction} = \frac{t}{T}$.
- step 3: Substitute the numerical values with units: $\text{Fraction} = \frac{7\text{ days}}{29.5\text{ days}}$.
- step 4: Compute the decimal value and track unit cancellation (days cancel out): $\frac{7}{29.5} \approx 0.2373$.
- step 5: Convert to percentage by multiplying by 100%: $0.2373 \times 100\% = 23.73\%$, which is physically reasonable for roughly one-quarter of a lunar month.
REASONING: The lunar cycle is periodic and spans 29.5 days. Finding the fractional completion requires dividing the elapsed time by the total period, which uses basic proportion to track orbital progress.
ANSWER: 7 days is approximately 23.73% of the 29.5-day lunar cycle.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: If a student starts observing a Waxing Crescent moon on Day 3 of the lunar cycle, on approximately which day of the cycle should they expect to observe the Full Moon, assuming uniform orbital speed?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the starting day ($d_1 = 3\text{ days}$) and the target phase (Full Moon).
- step 2: Recall that the Full Moon occurs halfway through the 29.5-day cycle, at approximately day $\frac{29.5}{2} = 14.75\text{ days}$.
- step 3: Calculate the time difference needed from the start day to reach the target phase: $\Delta t = 14.75\text{ days} - 3\text{ days}$.
- step 4: Perform subtraction with unit tracking: $\Delta t = 11.75\text{ days}$.
- step 5: Verify physical reasonableness: About 12 days past a crescent moon brings the Moon to its fully illuminated phase, aligning with the periodic nature of lunar phases.
REASONING: Since the lunar cycle progresses at a relatively uniform rate, knowing the standard calendar day for each major phase allows us to predict when subsequent phases will appear by finding time differences.
ANSWER: Approximately Day 11.75 (or Day 12) of the cycle.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: An astronomer in an observatory in Bengaluru tracks the angular position of the Moon relative to the Sun. If the angle between the Sun and the Moon as viewed from Earth is 90 degrees during a quarter phase, how many radians or degrees does the Moon traverse in its 360-degree orbit during one full 29.5-day cycle? Assuming uniform speed, what is its average angular speed in degrees per day?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify total angular displacement for one complete revolution ($\theta = 360^\circ$) and total time ($T = 29.5\text{ days}$).
- step 2: State the formula for average angular speed: $\omega_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\theta}{T}$.
- step 3: Substitute values with units: $\omega_{\text{avg}} = \frac{360^\circ}{29.5\text{ days}}$.
- step 4: Perform division and track units: $\frac{360}{29.5} \approx 12.203\text{ degrees/day}$.
- step 5: Verify physical reasonableness: The Moon moves roughly 12 to 13 degrees eastward against background stars each day, which matches astronomical observations.
REASONING: The Moon completes one full circular orbit ($360^\circ$) around Earth in one lunar month (29.5 days). Dividing total degrees by total days gives the average daily angular displacement.
ANSWER: Approximately $12.20^\circ$ per day.
