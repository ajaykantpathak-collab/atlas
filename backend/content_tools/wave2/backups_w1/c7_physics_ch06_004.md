## ELI5
The night sky is a rotating wardrobe: from the school rooftop at Diwali you see one set of constellations; from the summer vacation sky, a different rack. Earth's revolution carries our viewing window around the sun, so the sun sits in front of different star-backdrops through the year - and the night side faces a shifting cast. The stars did not move; our viewing platform did. A year in orbit, a year in constellations.

## DEFINITION
The changing view of the night sky due to revolution is the apparent shift in visible star constellations over the course of a year, measured in degrees of angular shift per month, caused by Earth's orbital movement around the Sun.

## WALKTHROUGH
When you look at the night sky from your school rooftop in Diwali compared to when you are on summer vacation, you notice completely different star patterns like Orion or the Big Dipper. To understand why this happens, recall how Earth moves in an orbit around the Sun over a full year. As Earth travels along this orbital path, the dark night-side of our planet faces a different direction of deep space every few months. Connecting this yearly orbital motion to the changing background stars explains why certain constellations disappear and reappear seasonally.

**Takeaway:** constellations change with the seasons because revolution moves Earth's night side to face different slices of the sky. **In the exam:** sketch the orbit with the sun at centre and mark which stars the midnight side faces at two different points. **Check yourself:** compare the walkthrough's Diwali sky with the summer-vacation sky and attribute the change to the lap, not the spin. **Why students miss it:** students blame the stars for wandering - the flagged inversion of mover and viewed. Draw two orbital positions with their midnight directions marked, and the seasonal sky explains itself in one clean diagram, no star left behind.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Because during June, Earth is on the side of the Sun where the night-side points toward a different region of space, while Orion is behind the Sun from our perspective and hidden by daytime glare. A wrong answer assumes stars disappear permanently rather than being blocked by daytime orientation.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If Earth completes one revolution ($360^\circ$) around the Sun in $365$ days, calculate the average angular shift of our view of the night sky per day, rounded to one decimal place.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Steps: 
1. Total angle of revolution = $360^\circ$. 
2. Total time taken = $365$ days. 
3. Divide angle by days: $\frac{360^\circ}{365\text{ days}} \approx 0.986^\circ/\text{day}$. 
4. Round to one decimal place: $1.0^\circ/\text{day}$. 
A wrong answer usually divides by $24$ hours instead of $365$ days, confusing rotation with revolution.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think that stars change position in the night sky because the stars themselves are physically moving or orbiting around the Earth. They fail to realize that Earth's revolution changes our perspective point from deep space, making fixed background stars appear to shift seasonally.
## ON_FAILURE

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: An astronomy club in Delhi observes a particular constellation directly overhead at midnight on October 1st. If Earth's orbital revolution rate is approximately $0.986^\circ/\text{day}$, calculate the total angular shift in the night sky view after $90$ days.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given values: rate $R = 0.986^\circ/\text{day}$, time $t = 90\text{ days}$. Identify what to find: total angular shift $\theta$.
- Step 2: Select the formula relating angular shift, rate, and time: $\theta = R \times t$. Check unit consistency ($\text{degrees/day} \times \text{days} = \text{degrees}$).
- Step 3: Substitute the given values into the formula: $\theta = 0.986^\circ/\text{day} \times 90\text{ days}$.
- Step 4: Perform the multiplication: $0.986 \times 90 = 88.74$. Track unit cancellation: days cancel out, leaving degrees ($\circ$).
- Step 5: Verify the answer is physically reasonable ($88.74^\circ$ is roughly a quarter of a full $360^\circ$ circle, which makes sense for 90 days out of 365).
REASONING: Multiplying the daily angular rate of change by the number of elapsed orbital days gives the total accumulated shift in our line of sight toward background space.
ANSWER: $88.74^\circ$
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A student tracks a star cluster that shifts by a total of $180^\circ$ in the night sky. Given that Earth's average shift rate is $0.986^\circ/\text{day}$, calculate how many days have passed during this observation.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given values: total shift $\theta = 180^\circ$, rate $R = 0.986^\circ/\text{day}$. Identify what to find: time $t$ in days.
- Step 2: Rearrange the formula $\theta = R \times t$ to solve for time: $t = \frac{\theta}{R}$. Check units ($\text{degrees} / (\text{degrees/day}) = \text{days}$).
- Step 3: Substitute the given values into the rearranged formula: $t = \frac{180^\circ}{0.986^\circ/\text{day}}$.
- Step 4: Perform the division: $\frac{180}{0.986} \approx 182.55\text{ days}$. Track unit cancellation: degrees cancel, leaving days.
- Step 5: Verify the answer is physically reasonable ($182.55$ days is approximately half a year, which matches a $180^\circ$ opposite shift in orbital viewing position).
REASONING: Dividing the total observed angular displacement by the daily shift rate yields the exact duration of Earth's orbital travel between those two viewing positions.
ANSWER: $182.55\text{ days}$
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: An observational satellite records that Earth has moved through $45^\circ$ of its annual orbital arc around the Sun. If the total orbital circumference is approximately $940,000,000\text{ km}$ completed in $365.25\text{ days}$, calculate both the arc distance traveled in kilometers and the number of days elapsed.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given values: orbital arc angle $\theta = 45^\circ$, total orbit angle $= 360^\circ$, total distance $= 940,000,000\text{ km}$, total time $= 365.25\text{ days}$.
- Step 2: Calculate the fraction of the orbit completed: $\text{Fraction} = \frac{45^\circ}{360^\circ} = \frac{1}{8}$.
- Step 3: Calculate the arc distance: $\text{Distance} = \frac{1}{8} \times 940,000,000\text{ km} = 117,500,000\text{ km}$. Check units ($\text{km}$).
- Step 4: Calculate the days elapsed: $\text{Time} = \frac{1}{8} \times 365.25\text{ days} = 45.66\text{ days}$. Check units ($\text{days}$).
- Step 5: Verify physical reasonableness ($45^\circ$ is one-eighth of a circle, so distance and time should both equal one-eighth of their respective yearly totals).
REASONING: By finding the angular fraction of the full $360^\circ$ orbit, we can proportionally determine both the physical orbital distance traveled and the time elapsed.
ANSWER: $117,500,000\text{ km}$ and $45.66\text{ days}$
