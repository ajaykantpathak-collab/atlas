## ELI5
Distribute sweets fairly: one packet meant for a group of two friends is opened and shared — each friend gets their share from you directly. Multiplication obeys the same fairness: multiplying a number by a bracketed sum equals multiplying it by each resident separately and adding the results. The multiplier outside owes a visit to every term inside; skipping one term is the classic blunder. Area pictures show it as one big field split into two smaller ones.

## DEFINITION
The distributive property of multiplication over addition states that for any three numbers \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) (integers, fractions, decimals, or algebraic terms), multiplying \(a\) by the sum of \(b\) and \(c\) gives the same result as multiplying \(a\) by \(b\) and \(a\) by \(c\) separately, then adding the products: \(a \times (b + c) = a \times b + a \times c\).
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can correctly apply the distributive property to expand expressions of the form \(a(b + c)\) where \(a, b, c\) are integers (including negatives), fractions, decimals, or algebraic terms, and can explain why the property holds using a numerical example or an area model.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the distributive property in the form \(a(b + c) = ab + ac\) for all real number types studied so far (integers, fractions, decimals, variables). It does NOT cover distribution over subtraction (though it follows directly), distribution with more than two terms inside the bracket (e.g., \(a(b+c+d)\)), or the expansion of binomials like \((a+b)(c+d)\) which are covered in later nodes.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are buying supplies for your classroom: each student needs 1 notebook (₹12) and 1 pen (₹8). If there are 5 students, you can calculate the total cost in two ways. First, find the cost per student (₹12 + ₹8 = ₹20) and multiply by 5 (5 × 20 = ₹100). Second, multiply the quantity of each item by 5 separately (5 × 12 = 60, 5 × 8 = 40) and add (60 + 40 = 100). Both give ₹100. This is the distributive property: \(5 \times (12 + 8) = 5 \times 12 + 5 \times 8\). In Class 7, you used this with whole numbers and simple arithmetic expressions (c7_math_ch02_006). Now we extend it to negative integers, fractions, decimals, and algebraic terms like \(3(x + 4)\). The key insight is that the multiplier outside the bracket must reach *every* term inside the bracket.

Takeaway: a times bracket-b-plus-c equals a-b plus a-c — the outside multiplier must reach every term inside, including negative and fractional ones. Exam format: expect expansions with negatives, fractions, decimals and algebraic terms; each missed term is a lost mark. Verification beat: substitute a small number for the variable and compare both sides once. Why students miss it: they multiply the first term only and drop the rest — treat the bracket as a queue every multiplier must serve completely.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
State the distributive property of multiplication over addition using the variables \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\).
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The property is \(a(b + c) = ab + ac\). A wrong answer like \(a(b + c) = ab + c\) reveals the student thinks the multiplier only applies to the first term inside the bracket, a classic "incomplete distribution" error. Writing \(a(b + c) = a + b + c\) shows confusion between multiplication and addition.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why \(4 \times (3 + 5)\) and \(4 \times 3 + 4 \times 5\) give the same answer. Use the meaning of multiplication as repeated addition in your explanation.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
\(4 \times (3 + 5)\) means adding \((3+5)\) four times: \((3+5)+(3+5)+(3+5)+(3+5)\). By reordering (commutative/associative property of addition), this becomes \((3+3+3+3) + (5+5+5+5)\) which is \(4 \times 3 + 4 \times 5\). A student who only computes both sides (getting 32) but cannot explain the *reason* shows procedural fluency without conceptual understanding. They may fail when variables replace numbers.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Simplify \(7 \times \left(\frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{6}\right)\) using the distributive property. Show all steps.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Distribute 7 to both fractions: \(7 \times \frac{2}{3} + 7 \times \frac{1}{6}\).  
Step 2: Multiply: \(\frac{14}{3} + \frac{7}{6}\).  
Step 3: Common denominator 6: \(\frac{28}{6} + \frac{7}{6} = \frac{35}{6}\).  
Step 4: Convert to mixed number: \(5 \frac{5}{6}\).  
A wrong answer like \(\frac{14}{3} + \frac{1}{6}\) (missing the 7 on the second term) shows the "incomplete distribution" error. An answer of \(\frac{21}{9}\) (adding numerators and denominators) shows a fraction addition misconception.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the two methods for evaluating \(12 \times 19\). Method A: \(12 \times (20 - 1)\). Method B: \(12 \times (10 + 9)\). Which uses the distributive property over addition? Can Method A be rewritten to use distributive property over addition? Explain.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Method B uses distributive property over addition: \(12 \times (10 + 9) = 12 \times 10 + 12 \times 9\). Method A uses subtraction. It can be rewritten as \(12 \times (20 + (-1)) = 12 \times 20 + 12 \times (-1)\), which is distributive property over addition with a negative integer. A student who says "Method A doesn't use distributive property" fails to see that subtraction is adding a negative, a key Class 8 extension (c8_math_ch6_6).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student wrote: \(5(x + 3) = 5x + 3\). Is this correct? If not, identify the error and give the correct expansion.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Incorrect. The error is incomplete distribution: the student multiplied 5 by \(x\) but forgot to multiply 5 by 3. The correct expansion is \(5x + 15\). A student who says "it's correct because 5 times x is 5x and you just bring down the 3" reveals a fundamental misunderstanding that the bracket implies multiplication of the outside term with *each* inside term, not just the first.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a real-life word problem (Indian context: rupees, metres, litres) that requires using the distributive property \(a(b + c) = ab + ac\) to solve efficiently. The problem must involve a decimal or fraction multiplier.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Example: "Riya buys 2.5 kg of apples at ₹120/kg and 2.5 kg of oranges at ₹80/kg. Use the distributive property to find the total cost."  
Solution: \(2.5 \times (120 + 80) = 2.5 \times 200 = 500\) or \(2.5 \times 120 + 2.5 \times 80 = 300 + 200 = 500\).  
A poor problem would use only whole numbers (already mastered in Class 7) or would not naturally suggest grouping the sum first (e.g., asking for cost of each separately). A good problem shows the student recognizes when distributing *or* summing first is easier.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students frequently write \(a(b + c) = ab + c\) (forgetting to multiply the outside term by the second inside term) or \(a(b + c) = ab + ac\) but then make sign errors with negatives like \(-2(x + 3) = -2x + 6\) (forgetting \(-2 \times 3 = -6\)). With fractions/decimals, they might distribute only the whole number part: \(2.5(4 + 2) = 2.5 \times 4 + 2\) (treating 2.5 as 2 and 0.5 separately but incorrectly). The core error is treating the bracket as a loose group rather than a single quantity that must be multiplied in its entirety.
## ON_FAILURE
1. Return to the area model: draw a rectangle with sides \(a\) and \((b+c)\). Divide it into two rectangles \(a \times b\) and \(a \times c\). Show total area = sum of parts.  
2. Use a numerical example with coloured counters: 3 groups of (2 red + 4 blue) counters. Physically separate into 3 groups of 2 red and 3 groups of 4 blue. Count totals.  
3. For algebraic errors, substitute a number for the variable (e.g., \(x=2\)) in both the original \(5(x+3)\) and the student's answer \(5x+3\). Show they give different results (25 vs 13), proving the expansion is wrong.  
4. Practice "distribute to every term" chant: "Multiply the outside by the first, multiply the outside by the second, then add."

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A school orders 15 boxes of pencils. Each box contains 12 red pencils and 8 blue pencils. Use the distributive property to find the total number of pencils in two different ways.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the multiplier (number of boxes = 15) and the addends inside the bracket (red pencils per box = 12, blue pencils per box = 8).
- Step 2: Write the expression for total pencils using the sum first: \(15 \times (12 + 8)\).
- Step 3: Apply distributive property: \(15 \times 12 + 15 \times 8\).
- Step 4: Compute each product: \(15 \times 12 = 180\) (since \(10 \times 12 = 120\) and \(5 \times 12 = 60\), sum = 180). \(15 \times 8 = 120\) (since \(10 \times 8 = 80\) and \(5 \times 8 = 40\), sum = 120).
- Step 5: Add the partial products: \(180 + 120 = 300\). Verify with sum-first method: \(12+8=20\), \(15 \times 20 = 300\). Both match.

REASONING: The distributive property allows us to break a multiplication into smaller, easier parts. Here, multiplying by 12 and 8 separately might be easier than multiplying by 20 mentally for some students, or vice versa. The property guarantees equivalence.

ANSWER: 300 pencils
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Simplify the expression: \(-4 \times \left( \frac{3}{5} + \left(-\frac{2}{5}\right) \right)\) using the distributive property. Show all steps.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the multiplier \(a = -4\) and the addends \(b = \frac{3}{5}\), \(c = -\frac{2}{5}\).
- Step 2: Write the distributive form: \(-4 \times \frac{3}{5} + (-4) \times \left(-\frac{2}{5}\right)\).
- Step 3: Multiply the first term: \(-4 \times \frac{3}{5} = -\frac{12}{5}\). (Negative × positive = negative).
- Step 4: Multiply the second term: \((-4) \times \left(-\frac{2}{5}\right) = +\frac{8}{5}\). (Negative × negative = positive).
- Step 5: Add the results: \(-\frac{12}{5} + \frac{8}{5} = \frac{-12+8}{5} = -\frac{4}{5}\).
- Step 6: Verify by summing inside bracket first: \(\frac{3}{5} + (-\frac{2}{5}) = \frac{1}{5}\). Then \(-4 \times \frac{1}{5} = -\frac{4}{5}\). Matches.

REASONING: This example combines three Class 8 extensions: negative multiplier, fraction addends, and a negative addend. The distributive property holds for all rational numbers. The sign rules for integer multiplication (c7_math_ch10_004) are critical in steps 3 and 4.

ANSWER: \(-\frac{4}{5}\)
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A rectangular garden has length \((x + 5)\) metres and width 3 metres. The length is increased by 2 metres. Write an expression for the new area using the distributive property, then expand it. If \(x = 4\), find the numerical increase in area.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Original area = width × length = \(3 \times (x + 5)\). Expanded: \(3x + 15\) sq m.
- Step 2: New length = \((x + 5) + 2 = x + 7\) metres.
- Step 3: New area = \(3 \times (x + 7)\). Apply distributive property: \(3x + 21\) sq m.
- Step 4: Increase in area = New area - Original area = \((3x + 21) - (3x + 15)\).
- Step 5: Simplify: \(3x + 21 - 3x - 15 = 6\) sq m. (The increase is constant, independent of x).
- Step 6: Verify with \(x = 4\): Original length = 9 m, area = 27 sq m. New length = 11 m, area = 33 sq m. Increase = 6 sq m. Matches algebraic result.

REASONING: This combines distributive property with algebraic expression simplification and a real-world geometry context. It shows the power of algebra: the increase (6 sq m) is the same for any \(x\) because the width (3) times the length increase (2) = 6. The distributive property allowed us to expand both areas before subtracting.

ANSWER: Expanded new area = \(3x + 21\) sq m; Increase in area = 6 sq m (for any x, including x=4).
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Ravi calculated \(19 \times 24\) by writing \(19 \times 24 = 19 \times (20 + 4) = 19 \times 20 + 19 \times 4 = 380 + 76 = 456\). Priya did \(19 \times 24 = (20 - 1) \times 24 = 20 \times 24 - 1 \times 24 = 480 - 24 = 456\). Both used the distributive property. Explain why both methods are valid and which one you find mentally easier. Justify your choice.

COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying methods using mathematical properties

STEPS:
- Step 1: Recognize that both methods rewrite one factor as a sum or difference to use distributive property.
- Step 2: Ravi used distributive property over addition: \(a(b+c) = ab+ac\) with \(a=19, b=20, c=4\).
- Step 3: Priya used distributive property over subtraction, which is derived from addition: \(a(b-c) = a(b+(-c)) = ab + a(-c) = ab - ac\).
- Step 4: Compare mental load: Ravi computes \(19 \times 20\) (380) and \(19 \times 4\) (76), adds. Priya computes \(20 \times 24\) (480) and subtracts 24.
- Step 5: Choose and justify: Priya's method is often easier because multiplying by 20 and 1 is simpler than multiplying by 19 and 4.

ANSWER: Both are valid because subtraction is adding a negative, so distributive property over addition covers both. Priya's method is generally easier mentally: \(20 \times 24 = 480\) is a known fact (double 24 is 48, add zero), and subtracting 24 is simpler than computing \(19 \times 4 = 76\) and adding to 380.

DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A student claims that the distributive property works for division over addition too, i.e., \((a + b) \div c = a \div c + b \div c\). Test this claim with \(a=10, b=5, c=2\) and \(a=10, b=5, c=3\). Is the claim always true? Explain why or why not using the relationship between division and multiplication.

COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions / Error identification and correction

STEPS:
- Step 1: Test first set: \((10+5) \div 2 = 15 \div 2 = 7.5\). Right side: \(10 \div 2 + 5 \div 2 = 5 + 2.5 = 7.5\). Works.
- Step 2: Test second set: \((10+5) \div 3 = 15 \div 3 = 5\). Right side: \(10 \div 3 + 5 \div 3 = \frac{10}{3} + \frac{5}{3} = \frac{15}{3} = 5\). Works.
- Step 3: Generalize: Division by \(c\) is multiplication by \(\frac{1}{c}\). So \((a+b) \div c = (a+b) \times \frac{1}{c}\). By distributive property of multiplication: \(= a \times \frac{1}{c} + b \times \frac{1}{c} = a \div c + b \div c\).
- Step 4: Conclude the claim IS always true (for \(c \neq 0\)) because division is multiplication by the reciprocal, and multiplication distributes over addition.
- Step 5: Note the reverse \((a \div (b+c))\) does NOT distribute.

ANSWER: Yes, the claim is always true (for non-zero divisor). \((a+b) \div c = a \div c + b \div c\) holds because dividing by \(c\) is multiplying by \(1/c\), and multiplication distributes over addition: \((a+b) \times \frac{1}{c} = a \times \frac{1}{c} + b \times \frac{1}{c}\). The student's claim is correct for this direction.

DIFFICULTY: hard
