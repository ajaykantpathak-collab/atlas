## ELI5
Share three and a half litres of paint into half-litre containers and division gives the count - but multiplication is the auditor: quotient times divisor must replay the dividend. That inverse dance works both ways, so a missing factor surrenders to a division, and any division can be rewritten as multiplying by the flipped fraction. Check answers by multiplying back, and the paint always lands exactly in its containers - no spilt arithmetic.

## DEFINITION
The relationship between multiplication and division of fractions states that dividing a fraction by another fraction is equivalent to multiplying the first fraction by the reciprocal (multiplicative inverse) of the divisor, meaning that if $\frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d} = e$, then $\frac{a}{b} = e \times \frac{c}{d}$. This inverse relationship allows us to convert any fraction division problem into an equivalent multiplication problem.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you bought $3\frac{1}{2}$ litres of paint and want to divide it equally into small containers that hold $\frac{1}{2}$ litre each to share with your friends in your apartment building. To find out how many containers you need, you perform a division: $3\frac{1}{2} \div \frac{1}{2}$. But how can you check if your answer is correct using what you already know about multiplication? Just like whole number division ($12 \div 3 = 4$ because $4 \times 3 = 12$), fraction division is completely undone by fraction multiplication. We begin by converting mixed fractions to improper fractions and then observing how multiplying our quotient by the divisor brings us right back to our original dividend. This connection shows that division is simply multiplication by the reciprocal!

**Takeaway:** division and multiplication of fractions are inverse operations; each verifies the other. **In the exam:** after solving, multiply quotient by divisor to confirm the dividend; for missing factors, divide. **Check yourself:** verify the paint-container count by multiplying the count back by the container size. **Why students miss it:** they treat the flip as a trick to remember, not an inverse relationship they can audit with. The audit habit turns every answer into its own proof, one multiplication back.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Which fraction operation is mathematically equivalent to dividing any number by a fraction $\frac{c}{d}$?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Multiplying by the reciprocal $\frac{d}{c}$. A wrong answer (such as "dividing by the reciprocal" or "multiplying by the same fraction") indicates the student confuses the definition of the reciprocal with regular operations and does not understand that division by a fraction transforms into multiplication by its flipped version.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If a student calculates $\frac{3}{4} \div \frac{2}{5} = \frac{15}{8}$, how can they use multiplication to verify if their answer is correct?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
They must multiply the quotient ($\frac{15}{8}$) by the divisor ($\frac{2}{5}$) to see if it equals the original dividend ($\frac{3}{4}$): $\frac{15}{8} \times \frac{2}{5} = \frac{30}{40} = \frac{3}{4}$. A wrong answer shows the student does not grasp that multiplication checks division for fractions just as it does for whole numbers.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Find the missing fraction $x$ if $x \times \frac{4}{7} = \frac{12}{35}$.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The correct answer is $\frac{3}{5}$. Working: Since multiplication and division are inverse operations, $x = \frac{12}{35} \div \frac{4}{7}$. Convert division to multiplication by the reciprocal: $x = \frac{12}{35} \times \frac{7}{4}$. Simplify cross-terms: $\frac{12 \div 4}{35 \div 7} \times \frac{7 \div 7}{4 \div 4} = \frac{3}{5} \times \frac{1}{1} = \frac{3}{5}$. A wrong answer (like $\frac{48}{245}$) indicates the student multiplied instead of using the inverse operation of division to isolate $x$.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often invert the *dividend* (the first fraction) instead of the *divisor* (the second fraction) when converting a division problem into multiplication, or they apply the reciprocal to both fractions. For example, when evaluating $\frac{2}{3} \div \frac{4}{5}$, a student might incorrectly compute $\frac{3}{2} \times \frac{5}{4}$ instead of keeping the first fraction as $\frac{2}{3}$ and multiplying by $\frac{5}{4}$.
## ON_FAILURE
Remind the student using the whole number analogy: in $12 \div 3 = 4$, the number 12 stays untouched, and we multiply by the reciprocal of 3 (which is $\frac{1}{3}$). Say to the student: "Keep the first fraction safe and unchanged, flip only the second fraction after the division sign, and then change the sign to multiplication." Have them practice writing 'Keep, Change, Flip' before touching any numbers.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A recipe for making traditional Mysore Pak requires $\frac{3}{4}$ kg of sugar for a single batch. If a sweet stall owner has $6\frac{3}{4}$ kg of sugar available, how many batches can be made? Use the relationship between multiplication and division to solve and verify your answer.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the given total quantity ($6\frac{3}{4}$ kg) and the quantity per batch ($\frac{3}{4}$ kg). Write the division expression: $6\frac{3}{4} \div \frac{3}{4}$.
- Step 2: Convert the mixed fraction into an improper fraction: $6\frac{3}{4} = \frac{(6 \times 4) + 3}{4} = \frac{27}{4}$.
- Step 3: Apply the relationship between multiplication and division by rewriting the division as multiplication by the reciprocal of the divisor: $\frac{27}{4} \div \frac{3}{4} = \frac{27}{4} \times \frac{4}{3}$.
- Step 4: Multiply and simplify algebraically: $\frac{27 \times 4}{4 \times 3} = \frac{27}{3} = 9$.
- Step 5: Verify the answer using multiplication (quotient $\times$ divisor = dividend): $9 \times \frac{3}{4} = \frac{27}{4} = 6\frac{3}{4}$ kg. The calculation is correct.
REASONING: By converting the division of fractions into multiplication by the reciprocal ($\frac{4}{3}$), we eliminate complex fraction division. Verifying via multiplication confirms that 9 batches multiplied by the sugar per batch yields our exact starting stock.
ANSWER: 9 batches
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Find the unknown fractional multiplier $y$ in the equation $y \div \frac{2}{5} = 3\frac{3}{4}$. Verify your solution by substituting $y$ back into the original equation.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Translate the division equation into its multiplication equivalent using the inverse relationship. Since $y \div \frac{2}{5} = 3\frac{3}{4}$, this means $y \times \frac{5}{2} = 3\frac{3}{4}$.
- Step 2: Convert the mixed number $3\frac{3}{4}$ to an improper fraction: $\frac{(3 \times 4) + 3}{4} = \frac{15}{4}$.
- Step 3: Isolate $y$ by applying the inverse operation of multiplication on both sides (dividing by $\frac{5}{2}$, which is multiplying by $\frac{2}{5}$): $y = \frac{15}{4} \times \frac{2}{5}$.
- Step 4: Compute the product with cross-simplification: $y = \frac{15 \div 5}{4 \div 2} \times \frac{2 \div 2}{5 \div 5} = \frac{3}{2} \times \frac{1}{1} = \frac{3}{2} = 1\frac{1}{2}$.
- Step 5: Verify by substituting $y = \frac{3}{2}$ into $y \div \frac{2}{5}$: $\frac{3}{2} \times \frac{5}{2} = \frac{15}{4} = 3\frac{3}{4}$, which matches the right side.
REASONING: Isolating a variable in a fraction equation requires applying inverse operations step by step, transitioning between division and multiplication forms as demonstrated.
ANSWER: $y = 1\frac{1}{2}$ (or $\frac{3}{2}$)
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A long-distance runner covers a track of $12\frac{1}{2}$ kilometres in $\frac{5}{4}$ hours. Calculate her average speed in kilometres per hour. Then, use the relationship between multiplication and division to find how far she would run in $\frac{3}{2}$ hours at that same speed.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Recall the speed formula: $\text{Speed} = \text{Distance} \div \text{Time}$. Substitute the values: $\text{Speed} = 12\frac{1}{2} \div \frac{5}{4}$.
- Step 2: Convert $12\frac{1}{2}$ to an improper fraction: $\frac{25}{2}$.
- Step 3: Rewrite division as multiplication by the reciprocal: $\text{Speed} = \frac{25}{2} \times \frac{4}{5}$.
- Step 4: Simplify and compute speed: $\frac{25 \div 5}{2 \div 2} \times \frac{4 \div 2}{5 \div 5} = \frac{5}{1} \times \frac{2}{1} = 10\text{ km/h}$.
- Step 5: To find the distance covered in $\frac{3}{2}$ hours, use the inverse relationship (Distance = Speed $\times$ Time): $\text{Distance} = 10 \times \frac{3}{2} = \frac{30}{2} = 15\text{ km}$.
REASONING: This problem connects division of fractions (finding unit rate/speed) with multiplication of fractions (calculating distance from speed and time), illustrating how both operations complement each other in real-world kinematics.
ANSWER: Speed is $10\text{ km/h}$; Distance is $15\text{ km}$
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Rahul solves the problem $\frac{7}{9} \div \frac{14}{15}$ by writing it as $\frac{7}{9} \times \frac{15}{14}$. He then states that because multiplication is commutative, he can also rewrite it as $\frac{15}{14} \times \frac{7}{9}$ and get the exact same answer. Is Rahul correct? Explain the mathematical reasoning behind why swapping the order works or fails in this context.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying algebraic properties of fractions
STEPS:
- Step 1: Analyze Rahul's first step: converting $\frac{7}{9} \div \frac{14}{15}$ to $\frac{7}{9} \times \frac{15}{14}$ using the definition of fraction division (multiplication by the reciprocal).
- Step 2: Examine his second claim regarding commutativity: $\frac{7}{9} \times \frac{15}{14} = \frac{15}{14} \times \frac{7}{9}$ because multiplication of rational numbers is commutative ($a \times b = b \times a$).
- Step 3: Compute the product: $\frac{7 \times 15}{9 \times 14} = \frac{1 \times 5}{3 \times 2} = \frac{5}{6}$.
- Step 4: Conclude whether his reasoning holds true.
ANSWER: Rahul is completely correct. While division itself is NOT commutative (since $a \div b \neq b \div a$), once the division expression is transformed into a multiplication expression ($\frac{7}{9} \times \frac{15}{14}$), the commutative property of multiplication fully applies to the factors, yielding the same correct result of $\frac{5}{6}$.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A container holds $18$ litres of cooking oil. A hotel uses $\frac{3}{4}$ of a litre per day. 
(a) Set up a division expression to find how many days the oil will last. 
(b) Without performing the full division, write a multiplication equation that can be used to check your answer. 
(c) If the hotel increases its daily usage by $\frac{1}{4}$ of a litre, recalculate the number of days the 18 litres will last using the relationship between multiplication and division, and explain whether the number of days increases or decreases.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions and analyzing inverse relationships
STEPS:
- Step 1: Part (a) - Set up division: $18 \div \frac{3}{4}$.
- Step 2: Part (b) - Write multiplication check: Let $N$ be the number of days; check equation is $N \times \frac{3}{4} = 18$.
- Step 3: Part (c) - Compute new daily usage: $\frac{3}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{4}{4} = 1$ litre per day.
- Step 4: Compute new number of days using division/multiplication relationship: $18 \div 1 = 18$ days.
- Step 5: Compare and explain: The number of days decreases from 24 days ($18 \times \frac{4}{3} = 24$) to 18 days because the divisor (daily usage) increased, and dividing by a larger number yields a smaller quotient.
ANSWER: (a) $18 \div \frac{3}{4}$; (b) $N \times \frac{3}{4} = 18$; (c) The oil lasts 18 days. The days decrease because consuming more oil per day reduces the total duration it can last, demonstrating inverse proportionality between rate and time.
DIFFICULTY: hard
