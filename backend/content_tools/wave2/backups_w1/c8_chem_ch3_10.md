## ELI5
Make nimbu pani and you have a mixture: sugar, salt, lemon and water tossed together physically, each keeping its own taste, each separable by a physical trick. Leave an iron nail out in the monsoon and you get rust — a compound: iron and oxygen chemically married in a fixed ratio by mass, producing something brand new, flaky and reddish-brown, nothing like the strong grey metal you started with. The test is simple. Can you still recognise the old ingredients, and can physical methods pull them apart? Yes means mixture; no means compound.

## DEFINITION
A mixture is a substance formed by physically mixing two or more substances together such that they retain their individual chemical properties and can be separated by physical means. A compound is a pure substance formed when two or more elements chemically combine in a fixed ratio by mass, resulting in a completely new substance with unique properties that can only be separated by chemical or electrochemical means.

## MASTERY_CRITERIA

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the fundamental differences between mixtures (both homogeneous and heterogeneous) and compounds. It focuses on differences in composition, properties, separation techniques, and energy changes during formation. It does not cover ionic vs. covalent bond structures, detailed oxidation states, or thermodynamic calculations.
## WALKTHROUGH
Think about what happens when you make a glass of *Nimbu Pani* (lemonade) in your kitchen versus what happens when an iron nail rusts in the damp monsoon air. When making lemonade, you physically mix sugar, salt, lemon juice, and water. You can still taste the sweetness of the sugar and the sourness of the lemon because they are physically combined and retain their properties. 

However, when iron reacts with oxygen and moisture to form rust (iron oxide), a brand-new chemical compound is born. The flaky, reddish-brown rust has completely different properties from the strong, grey, magnetic iron metal we started with. 

To determine whether a substance is a mixture or a compound, we first observe if the starting materials have kept their individual properties or if they have transformed into something entirely new. We then test if we can separate them using physical methods like filtration, evaporation, or magnetic separation. If physical methods fail and a chemical reaction (indicated by heat, light, or gas evolution) was required to create it, we are dealing with a compound.

Takeaway: the mixture-versus-compound court accepts three exhibits — were properties kept or replaced, can physical methods separate it, did making it release or absorb telltale energy — and the verdict follows the evidence. Exam format: expect observation-based verdicts (magnet test, acid test, solubility) with reasons. Verification beat: argue from one decisive exhibit, not from the name. Why students miss it: they decide by familiarity — iron-sulphur looks like iron-plus-sulphur until the magnet fails and the acid bubbles, and the tests are the proof.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
State two key differences between a physical mixture of iron filings and sulphur powder, and the chemical compound iron sulphide ($\text{FeS}$), in terms of their magnetic property and appearance under a magnifying glass.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
In a mixture of iron and sulphur, you can see distinct yellow sulphur particles and grey iron particles separately under a magnifying glass, and the iron filings can be pulled out using a magnet. In the compound iron sulphide ($\text{FeS}$), the substance appears as a uniform, black, homogeneous solid, and it is completely non-magnetic.

*What a wrong answer reveals:* If a student thinks the compound iron sulphide is still magnetic because "it contains iron," they do not understand that elements completely lose their individual physical properties (like magnetism) when they chemically react to form a compound.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why does dissolving carbon dioxide gas ($\text{CO}_2$) in water to make club soda represent a physical mixture, while burning charcoal (carbon) in oxygen to produce carbon dioxide gas represents the formation of a compound?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Dissolving $\text{CO}_2$ in water is a physical process where no new chemical bonds are formed; the carbon dioxide molecules simply slip between water molecules and can easily escape as gas when the bottle is opened (reversible by physical pressure release). Burning charcoal ($C + O_2 \rightarrow CO_2$) is a chemical reaction where carbon and oxygen atoms form strong chemical bonds to create a new substance ($\text{CO}_2$) with entirely different properties, accompanied by the release of heat and light energy.

*What a wrong answer reveals:* If a student thinks both are chemical changes because "bubbles or gases are involved," they are confusing the physical process of gas dissolution (solubility) with a chemical synthesis reaction.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
You are given two jars. Jar A contains a mixture of Hydrogen gas and Oxygen gas in a $2:1$ volume ratio. Jar B contains water vapour ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$). Predict what will happen when a burning wooden splinter is introduced into each jar, and use this to classify them.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
- **Step 1:** Analyze Jar A (mixture). Hydrogen is highly flammable and oxygen supports combustion. Because they are in a mixture, they retain their individual properties.
- **Step 2:** Predict Jar A's reaction. The burning splinter will ignite the free hydrogen gas, causing a loud, explosive "pop" sound.
- **Step 3:** Analyze Jar B (compound). Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) is a compound where hydrogen and oxygen have chemically combined. The compound has entirely new properties: it neither burns nor supports burning.
- **Step 4:** Predict Jar B's reaction. The burning splinter will immediately be extinguished.
- **Step 5:** Conclusion. Jar A is a mixture because the gases behave independently; Jar B is a compound because the elements have lost their original properties to form a fire-extinguishing substance.

*What a wrong answer reveals:* If a student predicts that Jar B will also explode because "it contains hydrogen," they fail to understand that the chemical combination of elements in a compound alters their reactivity entirely.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Two samples of water are analyzed. Sample 1 is muddy water collected from a pond in West Bengal, containing dissolved minerals and suspended clay. Sample 2 is pure distilled water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) prepared in a school lab. Explain how the ratio of Hydrogen to Oxygen by mass behaves in both samples.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
In Sample 2 (pure distilled water), which is a pure compound, the ratio of Hydrogen to Oxygen is strictly fixed at $1:8$ by mass, according to the Law of Constant Proportions. In Sample 1 (muddy water), which is a mixture of water, clay, and minerals, the overall composition is variable depending on how much mud is mixed in. However, the water molecules *within* that mud still maintain the exact $1:8$ mass ratio of H to O.

*What a wrong answer reveals:* If a student thinks the H:O ratio of the water molecules changes because of the mud, they do not realize that adding impurities to a compound forms a mixture, which does not alter the internal chemical bonds of the compound's molecules.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student claims: "Since brass (an alloy of copper and zinc) has a uniform golden colour throughout and cannot be separated into copper and zinc by simple filtration, it must be a chemical compound." Evaluate this claim and provide two reasons to support or refute it.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The student's claim is incorrect. Brass is a homogeneous mixture (an alloy), not a compound, because:
1. **Variable Composition:** The ratio of copper to zinc in brass can be varied continuously (e.g., $60\%$ copper and $40\%$ zinc, or $70\%$ copper and $30\%$ zinc) to change its properties, whereas a compound must have a strictly fixed chemical formula.
2. **Retention of Properties:** Copper and zinc in brass retain their individual properties, such as high electrical and thermal conductivity. No chemical reaction (heat or light exchange) occurs when they are melted together to form brass.

*What a wrong answer reveals:* The student is over-relying on "uniform appearance" (homogeneity) and "difficulty of mechanical separation" as proof of a compound, failing to recognize that alloys are homogeneous mixtures.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple experimental procedure using common laboratory chemicals to prove that a black powder is a physical mixture of Charcoal (Carbon) and Copper(II) Oxide ($\text{CuO}$), and not a single chemical compound containing carbon and copper.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
- **Test 1 (Physical Separation):** Add the black powder to water. Light charcoal particles will float on the surface, while dense copper oxide particles will sink to the bottom. This physical separation proves it is a mixture.
- **Test 2 (Chemical Reaction):** Add dilute hydrochloric acid ($\text{HCl}$) to the black powder. The copper oxide ($\text{CuO}$) will react with the acid to form a blue-green solution of copper chloride ($\text{CuCl}_2$), leaving the black charcoal completely unreacted and floating in the liquid. 
$$\text{CuO} (s) + 2\text{HCl} (aq) \rightarrow \text{CuCl}_2 (aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O} (l)$$
If it were a single compound, the entire powder would react uniformly, and we would not see carbon separating out unaffected.

*What a poor answer reveals:* A poor answer might suggest heating it to melt it, which is physically difficult in a middle school lab and does not exploit the differences in the chemical solubilities or reactivities of the individual components.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often assume that because a mixture is uniform and clear (like a sugar solution or salt solution), a chemical reaction must have occurred to form a new compound. For example, when making sugar syrup (*Chashni*) for Indian sweets, they believe that since the white sugar crystals "disappear" into the water and cannot be filtered out with a tea strainer, a new chemical compound has been formed.
## ON_FAILURE
To correct this misconception, guide the student through a simple physical reclamation experiment:
1. Have the student dissolve $5\text{ g}$ of common salt ($\text{NaCl}$) in $50\text{ mL}$ of water. Ask: "Can you see the salt?" (No, it is homogeneous).
2. Ask: "Does it still taste like salt?" (Yes, the chemical property of saltiness is retained).
3. Have them boil the solution in a steel pan until all the water evaporates. 
4. Observe the white residue of salt left behind. Ask: "Did we get our salt back without using chemical reactions?" (Yes).
5. Explain: "Since the salt retained its taste and was easily separated by a simple physical process (evaporation), dissolving salt in water is a physical change that forms a mixture, not a compound."

QUESTION: A student calls salt water a 'new substance' because it tastes different from both salt and water. Correct them.
COMPETENCY: Explaining why a mixture is not a new substance.
STEPS:
- 1: Salt water keeps each part chemically unchanged — evaporate it and you get the salt back.
- 2: A mixture is a PHYSICAL blend — proportions can vary, parts can be separated.
- 3: A new substance (compound) needs chemical bonding and a fixed ratio — not the case here.
ANSWER: Salt water is a mixture — the salt and water stay chemically themselves, keep variable proportions, and separate again. No new substance forms.
