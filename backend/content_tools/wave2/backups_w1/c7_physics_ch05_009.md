## ELI5
Standing behind a tall compound wall, you can still watch the street: the periscope bends light around obstacles using two plane mirrors set parallel at 45-degree angles inside a tube. Light from the scene strikes the top mirror, drops down the tube, strikes the second mirror, and enters your eye level with the view. Two reflections, one straight escape - the submarine's window and the cricket fan's cheat code.

## DEFINITION
A periscope is an optical instrument that uses two plane mirrors placed parallel to each other at a 45-degree angle inside a tube to allow an observer to see objects that are out of their direct line of sight. It measures visual paths around obstacles using the law of reflection, and has no standard SI unit as it is a device, though the angles of reflection are measured in degrees (°).

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine standing behind a tall brick compound wall in your colony, unable to see the street traffic or a cricket match happening on the other side. To see over the wall without climbing it, you need to bend light twice—first bringing it down, then directing it forward into your eyes. You can begin by taking a long cardboard tube or box and placing two plane mirrors at the top and bottom ends, facing each other at exact 45-degree angles. This connects directly to what you learned about how light travels in a straight line and bounces off surfaces through reflection. By applying the laws of reflection where the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, light from the hidden object hits the top mirror, bounces down vertically, hits the bottom mirror, and bounces horizontally into the observer's eye.

**Takeaway:** a periscope is two 45-degree mirrors in a tube: light turns once at the top, once at the bottom, and the hidden viewer sees out. **In the exam:** draw the ray path through both mirrors - the parallelism keeps the final image upright, not inverted. **Check yourself:** trace the walkthrough's wall problem: which mirror collects the street, and which delivers it to your eye? **Why students miss it:** students fear double reflection - the flagged intimidation. Each mirror obeys the same reflection laws from the earlier chapter; the periscope is just geometry applied twice. Draw both normals at 45 degrees, and the ray path draws itself.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why must the two plane mirrors inside a periscope be placed at an angle of 45 degrees to the frame of the tube?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The mirrors must be at 45 degrees so that incoming horizontal light rays from an object strike the top mirror and are turned vertically downward by 90 degrees, and then strike the bottom mirror to be turned horizontally outward by another 90 degrees into the eye. A student who answers "so that the light can speed up" or "to make the image bigger" confuses reflection with refraction or lenses, failing to understand how 90-degree directional turns require 45-degree mirror alignment.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If light strikes the top mirror of a periscope at an angle of incidence of 30 degrees measured from the normal, what is the angle of reflection at that mirror?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The angle of reflection is 30 degrees. 
Steps:
1. Identify the given value: angle of incidence ($i$) = 30°.
2. Recall the law of reflection: The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection ($\angle i = \angle r$).
3. Set up the relation: $\angle r = 30°$.
4. State the final answer with units: 30 degrees.
A student who answers "60 degrees" likely subtracted the angle of incidence from 90 degrees and incorrectly put that as the angle of reflection, forgetting that the law states incidence equals reflection directly.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
How does the final image seen through a simple two-mirror periscope compare to the actual object in terms of orientation (upright or inverted)?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The final image is upright (erect). 
Steps:
1. Analyze the first reflection: The top mirror inverts the object vertically and laterally (upside down and reversed left-to-right).
2. Analyze the second reflection: The bottom mirror reflects the image a second time.
3. Combine the effects: Two successive reflections cancel out each other's inversion, making the final image appear right-side up to the observer.
A student who answers "inverted" only thought about the first mirror and forgot that the second mirror performs a second inversion that restores the upright orientation.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student builds a periscope where the top and bottom mirrors are accidentally glued parallel to each other, but at an angle of 30 degrees to the tube instead of 45 degrees. Will the periscope successfully show an object located straight ahead over a barrier? Explain why or why not.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, it will not successfully show the object. 
Justification: For a periscope to successfully transmit light from a horizontal source over a vertical obstacle into a horizontal eye, the total directional change must be 180 degrees (two 90-degree turns). This requires the mirrors to be at 45 degrees to the main axis. If they are at 30 degrees, the light rays will bounce off at incorrect angles and miss the second mirror entirely or exit downward/upward away from the observer's eye. A student who thinks "any angle works as long as they are parallel" does not understand how the geometry of 45-degree angles is strictly required for 90-degree directional changes.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that a periscope magnifies distant objects like a telescope or binoculars do. They confuse the function of a periscope (which is to bypass an obstacle using reflection to maintain normal size and perspective) with optical instruments that use lenses for magnification.
## ON_FAILURE
To fix this, have the student construct a simple periscope using a shoe box and two small pocket mirrors. Ask them to look at a pencil placed outside a window through the periscope and measure the size of the pencil compared to looking at it directly. They will observe that the image is the exact same size, proving that mirrors reflect light without magnifying it.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A student constructs a periscope of height 50 cm using a cardboard tube. A light ray from a flower enters the top mirror at an angle of incidence of 42°. What is the angle of reflection at the top mirror, and what is the total deviation of the light ray after completing both reflections in the periscope?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given values and what to find. Angle of incidence at top mirror ($i_1$) = 42°. Find angle of reflection ($r_1$) and total directional deviation of the light ray.
- Step 2: Apply the first law of reflection for the top mirror. $\angle r_1 = \angle i_1 = 42^\circ$.
- Step 3: Determine the directional change at the top mirror. Light turns downward by 90° because the mirror is at 45°.
- Step 4: Determine the directional change at the bottom mirror. The bottom mirror is parallel to the top mirror, so it turns the vertical ray horizontally outward by another 90°.
- Step 5: Compute total deviation and verify units. Total deviation = 90° + 90° = 180°. The ray reverses its horizontal direction of travel, coming out parallel to its original path.
REASONING: The periscope uses two successive reflections. Each reflection follows the law of reflection ($\angle i = \angle r$), and the 45-degree placement ensures exact 90-degree turns, resulting in a total directional reversal of 180 degrees.
ANSWER: Angle of reflection = 42°; Total deviation = 180°
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A light ray travels from air into a glass slab at an angle of 30 degrees. The refractive index of glass is 1.5. Calculate the angle of refraction.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify given values - angle of incidence (i) = 30 degrees, refractive index (n) = 1.5
- step 2: Apply Snell's Law: n1 * sin(i) = n2 * sin(r), where n1 = 1 (air)
- step 3: Calculate sin(r) = sin(30) / 1.5 = 0.5 / 1.5 = 0.333
- step 4: Find r = arcsin(0.333) = 19.47 degrees
- step 5: Verify: the refracted angle is less than the incident angle, which is expected when light enters a denser medium
REASONING: Snell's Law relates the angles of incidence and refraction to the refractive indices of the two media. When light enters a denser medium (higher n), it bends toward the normal.
ANSWER: The angle of refraction is approximately 19.47 degrees.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: In a periscope used by students in a school science lab, the top mirror is tilted so that the normal makes an angle of 45° with the horizontal tube axis. If a horizontal light ray strikes the top mirror, calculate the angle between the incident ray and the reflected ray at that mirror.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given values. The mirror is at 45° to the tube, meaning the angle of incidence ($\angle i$) for a horizontal ray is 45°.
- Step 2: Apply the law of reflection. The angle of reflection ($\angle r$) equals the angle of incidence, so $\angle r = 45^\circ$.
- Step 3: Set up the calculation for the angle between the incident ray and reflected ray. The total angle between incident and reflected rays is $\angle i + \angle r$.
- Step 4: Substitute the values with units. Total angle = $45^\circ + 45^\circ = 90^\circ$.
- Step 5: Verify physical reasonableness. A horizontal ray hitting a 45° mirror turns straight down (90° deviation), confirming the angle between incoming and outgoing paths at that single mirror is 90°.
REASONING: The angle between the incident ray and the reflected ray is the sum of the angle of incidence and the angle of reflection. Since the mirror is tilted at 45°, both angles are 45°, adding up to 90°.
ANSWER: 90°
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A submarine periscope uses two identical square plane mirrors of side length 10 cm. Light enters the top mirror horizontally, travels down a vertical shaft of length 1.5 m, and exits through the bottom mirror. If the speed of light in air is approximately $3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}$, calculate the time taken by a pulse of light to travel from the top mirror to the bottom mirror inside the shaft.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given values. Distance ($d$) = 1.5 m, Speed of light ($v$) = $3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}$. Find time ($t$).
- Step 2: Recall the formula relating distance, speed, and time: $t = \frac{d}{v}$.
- Step 3: Substitute the values with units into the formula: $t = \frac{1.5 \text{ m}}{3 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}}$.
- Step 4: Compute the division and track units: $t = 0.5 \times 10^{-8} \text{ s} = 5 \times 10^{-9} \text{ s}$. Meter units cancel out, leaving seconds in the numerator.
- Step 5: Verify that the time is physically reasonable. Light travels extremely fast, so 5 nanoseconds for a 1.5-meter shaft is correct.
REASONING: Light travels at a constant high speed through the medium inside the periscope tube. By using the kinematic relation between distance, speed, and time, we can calculate the exact transit time of light across the vertical shaft.
ANSWER: $5 \times 10^{-9} \text{ s}$ (or 5 nanoseconds)

- Step 2: Analyze Student B's setup: 30° mirrors create non-perpendicular turns, causing the light ray to reflect at 60° angles instead of 90°.
