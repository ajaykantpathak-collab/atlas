## ELI5
A ladder leaning on a wall, a pole's shadow meeting its tip, a room's diagonal — everyday scenes secretly hold right triangles, with the ground and the wall as the two legs and the ladder as the hypotenuse. Spot the triangle, name the sides, and the theorem converts knowns into the unknown distance. Two habits save marks: sketch first, and attach units to the final answer — metres to heights, centimetres to crafts.

## DEFINITION
The Pythagoras theorem states that in a right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse (the side opposite the right angle) is equal to the sum of the squares of the other two sides. In real-life problems, we identify right triangles formed by practical situations (like ladders against walls, shadows of poles, or diagonals of rectangles) and apply the relationship \(a^2 + b^2 = c^2\) to find unknown distances, heights, or lengths.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you need to rescue a cat stuck on a 3.6 metre high wall using a 5 metre long ladder. How far from the wall should you place the ladder's base? This is a real-life right triangle where the wall and ground form the perpendicular sides, and the ladder is the hypotenuse. Start by drawing a quick sketch and labelling the known sides: wall height = 3.6 m (one leg), ladder = 5 m (hypotenuse). Recall the theorem from c8_math_ch9_5: \(a^2 + b^2 = c^2\). Substitute the known values, solve for the unknown leg, and remember to take the square root at the end. The key insight is that many real-world problems hide right triangles in vertical-horizontal arrangements.

Takeaway: word problems are right triangles in disguise — identify the perpendicular pair (wall-ground, pole-shadow, length-width), assign the hypotenuse to the slanted member, and solve for the missing side, square root and all. Exam format: expect ladders, shadows, diagonals and distances with a diagram demanded and the answer closed with units. Verification beat: sanity-check the size — a computed ladder longer than the building's wall height should raise suspicion, not applause. Why students miss it: they assign the slanted side as a leg — the tilt is always the hypotenuse, and a rough sketch exposes the mislabelling instantly.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
State the Pythagoras theorem formula and identify which side is the hypotenuse in a right-angled triangle.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The formula is \(a^2 + b^2 = c^2\) where \(c\) is the hypotenuse (the longest side, opposite the right angle), and \(a, b\) are the other two sides. A wrong answer like "\(a^2 + b^2 = c\)" (missing square on c) or identifying the hypotenuse as "the vertical side" reveals the student has memorised the formula without understanding the geometric meaning of each term.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
A student solves a ladder problem: "Ladder = 10 m, wall height = 6 m, find distance from wall." They write \(10^2 + 6^2 = x^2\) and get \(x = \sqrt{136} \approx 11.66\) m. Explain why this setup is incorrect.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The ladder is the hypotenuse (longest side), so it must be \(c\) in the formula. The correct setup is \(6^2 + x^2 = 10^2\). The student's error shows they don't recognise that the hypotenuse is always the side opposite the right angle (the ladder), not just "the slanted side." They added squares of both known sides instead of subtracting.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A rectangular park is 80 m long and 60 m wide. A person walks diagonally from one corner to the opposite corner. How much distance do they save compared to walking along the two sides? Show all steps.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Identify the right triangle - the diagonal is the hypotenuse, length and width are legs. Step 2: Apply theorem: \(d^2 = 80^2 + 60^2 = 6400 + 3600 = 10000\). Step 3: \(d = \sqrt{10000} = 100\) m. Step 4: Distance along sides = 80 + 60 = 140 m. Step 5: Distance saved = 140 - 100 = 40 m. A wrong answer of 100 m (just the diagonal) reveals the student solved for the diagonal but didn't answer the actual question asked (distance saved).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Two problems: (A) A 13 m ladder reaches a 12 m high window. Find base distance. (B) A 13 m rope is tied from the top of a 12 m pole to the ground. Find distance from pole base. Compare the mathematical structure. Are they the same or different? Justify.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Both are identical mathematically: hypotenuse = 13, one leg = 12, find other leg. Both give \(x^2 = 13^2 - 12^2 = 169 - 144 = 25\), so \(x = 5\) m. The contexts differ (ladder vs rope) but the right triangle structure is the same: vertical pole/wall, horizontal ground, slanted hypotenuse. A student saying "different because one is a ladder" cannot abstract the mathematical model from the context.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Ravi solves: "A tree casts a 15 m shadow. The distance from tree top to shadow tip is 17 m. Find tree height." He writes: \(h^2 = 17^2 + 15^2 = 289 + 225 = 514\), so \(h = \sqrt{514} \approx 22.67\) m. Evaluate his solution and correct it.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Ravi incorrectly added the squares. The 17 m (tree top to shadow tip) is the hypotenuse. Correct: \(h^2 + 15^2 = 17^2\) → \(h^2 = 289 - 225 = 64\) → \(h = 8\) m. His error reveals he doesn't identify the hypotenuse as the longest side (17 > 15) and defaults to adding squares. He also didn't check reasonableness: a 15 m shadow with a 22.67 m tree implies an extremely steep sun angle.

Example: "A 20 m high mobile tower is supported by a wire tied from its top to a point on the ground 15 m from its base. Find the wire length." Diagram: vertical tower (20 m), horizontal ground (15 m), wire (hypotenuse). Solution: \(w^2 = 20^2 + 15^2 = 400 + 225 = 625\) → \(w = 25\) m. A poor answer uses a non-right triangle scenario (e.g., "distance between two cities") or forgets the square root, showing inability to recognise right triangles in new contexts.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students consistently misidentify the hypotenuse in word problems. They see two given numbers and automatically plug them as \(a\) and \(b\) in \(a^2 + b^2 = c^2\), then solve for \(c\) — even when one given number is actually the hypotenuse. For example, given "ladder = 10 m, height = 6 m", they compute \(\sqrt{10^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{136}\) instead of \(\sqrt{10^2 - 6^2} = 8\). They also frequently forget to take the square root, leaving answers as \(c^2 = 64\) instead of \(c = 8\).
## ON_FAILURE
First, have the student physically act out or draw the scenario: stand a ruler (ladder) against a book (wall) on a table (ground). Ask: "Which side is the slanted one? Which is the longest?" Label the triangle: right angle at book-table corner, hypotenuse = ruler. Second, give a fixed drill: "For each problem, circle the hypotenuse FIRST, then write the formula with hypotenuse as \(c\)." Third, practise 5 problems where the hypotenuse is given and one leg is unknown, forcing subtraction: \(a^2 = c^2 - b^2\). Emphasise: "Big number squared minus small number squared."

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A farmer has a rectangular field 120 metres long and 50 metres wide. He wants to install a water pipe diagonally from one corner to the opposite corner. If the pipe costs ₹85 per metre, what is the total cost of the pipe?

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the right triangle - the diagonal of the rectangle forms the hypotenuse, with length (120 m) and width (50 m) as the two legs.
- Step 2: Apply Pythagoras theorem: \(d^2 = 120^2 + 50^2\) where \(d\) is the diagonal length.
- Step 3: Compute squares: \(120^2 = 14400\), \(50^2 = 2500\). Sum = \(14400 + 2500 = 16900\).
- Step 4: Take square root: \(d = \sqrt{16900} = 130\) m (since \(130^2 = 16900\)).
- Step 5: Calculate cost: \(130 \times 85 = ₹11,050\). Verify: 130 m is reasonable (less than sum of sides 170 m, more than longer side 120 m).

REASONING: The rectangle's diagonal creates two congruent right triangles. The theorem applies because all rectangle corners are 90°. We first find the geometric quantity (diagonal length) then apply the real-world rate (cost per metre). The 5-12-13 triple scaled by 10 (50-120-130) provides a mental check.

ANSWER: ₹11,050
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A 25 metre long guy wire is attached to the top of an electric pole and fixed to the ground 15 metres from the base of the pole. During a storm, the wire snaps at a point 10 metres from the top. How far from the pole base does the broken end touch the ground? (Assume the pole remains vertical and the ground is horizontal.)

STEPS:
- Step 1: Draw the initial setup - right triangle with hypotenuse = 25 m (wire), base = 15 m (ground distance). Find pole height \(h\): \(h^2 + 15^2 = 25^2\).
- Step 2: Solve for pole height: \(h^2 = 625 - 225 = 400\) → \(h = 20\) m.
- Step 3: After snapping, the remaining wire from break point to ground is \(25 - 10 = 15\) m. The break point is \(20 - 10 = 10\) m above ground (vertical distance).
- Step 4: New right triangle: vertical leg = 10 m, hypotenuse = 15 m. Find new ground distance \(x\): \(x^2 + 10^2 = 15^2\).
- Step 5: Compute: \(x^2 = 225 - 100 = 125\) → \(x = \sqrt{125} = 5\sqrt{5} \approx 11.18\) m.

REASONING: This multi-step problem requires applying the theorem twice - first to find the pole height from the original configuration, then to find the new ground distance after the wire shortens. The vertical pole and horizontal ground maintain the right angle throughout. Subtracting the snapped length from both the wire and the pole height is the critical insight.

ANSWER: Approximately 11.18 metres from the pole base
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Anju's house is 3 km east and 4 km north of her school. Her friend Binny's house is 5 km east and 12 km north of the same school. If Anju walks directly to Binny's house (shortest path), how far does she walk? Give answer in metres.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Set up coordinate system with school at origin (0,0). Anju's house at (3,4), Binny's at (5,12). All distances in km.
- Step 2: Find horizontal (east-west) separation: \(5 - 3 = 2\) km. Find vertical (north-south) separation: \(12 - 4 = 8\) km.
- Step 3: These separations form the legs of a right triangle (east-west ⟂ north-south). Direct distance is the hypotenuse.
- Step 4: Apply theorem: \(d^2 = 2^2 + 8^2 = 4 + 64 = 68\). So \(d = \sqrt{68} = \sqrt{4 \times 17} = 2\sqrt{17}\) km.
- Step 5: Convert to metres: \(2\sqrt{17} \times 1000 = 2000\sqrt{17} \approx 2000 \times 4.123 = 8246\) m.
- Step 6: Verify reasonableness: Direct distance (8.2 km) is less than sum of legs (10 km) and greater than longer leg (8 km). Correct.

REASONING: This problem combines coordinate geometry (Class 7/8 prior knowledge) with Pythagoras. The key is recognising that east-west and north-south directions are perpendicular, creating a right triangle. The solution requires algebraic simplification of surds (\(\sqrt{68} = 2\sqrt{17}\)) and unit conversion (km to m), integrating multiple Class 8 skills.

ANSWER: \(2000\sqrt{17}\) metres (approximately 8246 metres)
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A 15 m long bamboo pole is leaning against a vertical wall. The foot of the pole is 9 m from the wall. The pole starts sliding down such that its top moves down the wall at a constant rate. When the top is 8 m above the ground, how far is the foot from the wall? Explain why the Pythagoras theorem still applies at every moment during the sliding.

COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions and justifying the model's validity.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand the problem - initially a right triangle (wall, ground, pole). As it slides, the pole remains straight, wall vertical, ground horizontal.
- Step 2: Identify that at every instant, the pole, wall, and ground form a right triangle (pole = hypotenuse = 15 m constant).
- Step 3: Apply theorem at the specific moment: height = 8 m (one leg), pole = 15 m (hypotenuse), find base distance \(x\).
- Step 4: Compute: \(x^2 + 8^2 = 15^2\) → \(x^2 = 225 - 64 = 161\) → \(x = \sqrt{161} \approx 12.69\) m.
- Step 5: Justify: The wall-ground angle stays 90°, the pole length is fixed, so the right triangle condition holds throughout the motion.

ANSWER: The foot is \(\sqrt{161}\) m (≈ 12.69 m) from the wall. The theorem applies because the three segments always form a right triangle: vertical wall ⟂ horizontal ground, with the rigid pole as hypotenuse.

DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A rectangular swimming pool is 50 m long and 30 m wide. A rope is stretched diagonally across the pool from one corner to the opposite corner, 2 m above the water surface. A swimmer wants to swim from the same starting corner to the opposite corner, but must stay underwater (diagonally across the bottom). How much longer is the rope path than the underwater swim path? The pool depth is uniformly 2 m. Explain your 3D reasoning.

COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying methods in multi-dimensional contexts.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand the two paths: Rope is diagonal of a rectangle 50 m × 30 m at height 2 m. Underwater path is diagonal of the pool bottom (same rectangle 50 m × 30 m).
- Step 2: Compute rope length (2D diagonal): \(r^2 = 50^2 + 30^2 = 2500 + 900 = 3400\) → \(r = \sqrt{3400} = 10\sqrt{34}\) m.
- Step 3: Compute underwater diagonal (same 2D diagonal on bottom): \(u^2 = 50^2 + 30^2 = 3400\) → \(u = 10\sqrt{34}\) m.
- Step 4: Wait - the rope is 2 m above water, but horizontally it spans the same rectangle. The rope length equals the bottom diagonal length because both are hypotenuses of the SAME 50×30 right triangle.
- Step 5: Difference = \(10\sqrt{34} - 10\sqrt{34} = 0\) m. The rope path is NOT longer; both are exactly the same length.

REASONING: This problem tests whether students mistakenly think "3D = longer" and try to use a 3D diagonal formula. The rope and the underwater path are both diagonals of the identical 50 m × 30 m rectangle - one at the surface, one at the bottom. The vertical separation (2 m depth) doesn't affect the horizontal diagonal length. The right triangle for each path lies in a horizontal plane.

ANSWER: 0 metres - both paths are exactly the same length (\(10\sqrt{34}\) m ≈ 58.31 m). The rope is directly above the underwater diagonal.

DIFFICULTY: hard
