## ELI5
Point a torch at a ball in a dark room and walk around it: the lit side always faces the torch, but your eyes see changing shapes — edge-on slivers, half-moons, full discs. That walk is the Moon's orbit, the torch is the Sun, and the changing view is the answer to why the Moon changes shape. No shadow falls in this story; only the angle between Sun, Moon and Earth rewrites the visible lit fraction nightly.

## DEFINITION
The changing shape of the Moon (phases) is caused by the changing relative positions of the Sun, Earth, and Moon as the Moon orbits Earth, which alters the fraction of the Moon's sunlit hemisphere that is visible from Earth.

## WALKTHROUGH
Observe the Moon on two different nights a week apart — you will see a distinctly different shape, like a thin crescent versus a half-circle. Recall from Class 6 (c6_physics_ch11_036) that light travels in straight lines and the Moon reflects sunlight (c6_physics_ch12_051). To begin, place a white ball (Moon) on a table, shine a torch (Sun) from one side, and view the ball from different positions around it (Earth). Notice how the lit portion visible to you changes as you move. Connect this to the Moon's revolution around Earth (c7_physics_ch06_003): as the Moon moves along its orbit, the angle between the Sun-Earth-Moon lines changes, causing the visible lit fraction to change. The key principle is: **The Moon's phase depends entirely on the angle between the Sun-Earth line and the Earth-Moon line.**

Takeaway: phases arise because the Moon orbits Earth, so the angle between the Sun-Earth line and the Earth-Moon line keeps changing — Earth sees shifting fractions of the same sunlit hemisphere; position to phase and phase to position are both testable mappings. Exam format: expect two-way questions: identify the phase from a positions diagram, or place the three bodies for a named phase. Verification beat: mark the sunlit half toward the Sun in every sketch before judging the visible part. Why students miss it: they confuse phases with eclipses — eclipses need alignments and shadows; phases need only an orbit and a changing viewing angle.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
A student draws the Sun, Earth, and Moon in a straight line with Earth in the middle. What phase of the Moon would be seen from Earth, and why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The phase would be **Full Moon**. With Earth in the middle, the Sun and Moon are on opposite sides of Earth. Sunlight hits the entire hemisphere of the Moon facing Earth, so we see the fully illuminated circle. A wrong answer (e.g., "New Moon" or "Lunar Eclipse") reveals the student confuses the alignment for phases with the alignment for eclipses, or does not realize that Earth's shadow only causes an eclipse when the Moon passes exactly through it (which is rare due to orbital tilt).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why does the Moon show phases at all, instead of always looking like a half-lit circle? Explain using the fact that the Moon is a sphere and sunlight comes from one direction.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The Moon is a sphere, so sunlight always illuminates exactly half of it (the dayside). However, from Earth we see different fractions of that lit hemisphere depending on the viewing angle. As the Moon orbits Earth, our viewing angle changes, so we see a varying mix of the lit and unlit sides. A wrong answer (e.g., "Earth's shadow covers part of the Moon" or "The Moon rotates to show different sides") reveals the common misconception that phases are caused by Earth's shadow or by the Moon's rotation, rather than by the changing viewing geometry of a half-lit sphere.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
The Moon is at a 90° angle from the Sun as seen from Earth (i.e., the Sun-Earth-Moon angle is 90°). What phase is observed, and what fraction of the Moon's visible disk is illuminated?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The phase is **First Quarter (or Last Quarter, depending on orbital direction)**. At a 90° angle, we see exactly half of the Moon's sunlit hemisphere, so **50% of the visible disk is illuminated**. A wrong answer (e.g., "Crescent" or "Gibbous") reveals the student cannot map the geometric angle to the resulting phase. A wrong fraction (e.g., 25% or 75%) reveals confusion between the angle at Earth and the fraction of the disk lit.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the Moon's phase when the Sun-Earth-Moon angle is 45° versus 135°. How does the illuminated fraction change, and are both phases "waxing" or "waning"?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
At 45°, the Moon is a **waxing crescent** (if moving from New to Full) with **<50% illumination**. At 135°, the Moon is a **waxing gibbous** with **>50% illumination**. Both are waxing (increasing illumination) if the Moon is moving from New toward Full. A wrong answer that says one is waning reveals the student does not connect the orbital motion direction to the waxing/waning label. A wrong illumination comparison reveals inability to visualize the geometry.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student claims: "When it is Full Moon in India, it must be New Moon in America because they are on opposite sides of Earth." Evaluate this statement.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is **false**. The Moon's phase is determined by the **global** Sun-Earth-Moon geometry, not the observer's local position on Earth. All observers on Earth see essentially the same phase at the same time (within a few hours' difference due to parallax, but the phase name is the same). The student incorrectly applies the day/night logic (which depends on local rotation) to the Moon's phase (which depends on the Moon's orbital position). A wrong evaluation reveals confusion between Earth's rotation (causing day/night) and Moon's revolution (causing phases).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple tabletop model using a lamp, a ball, and your head to demonstrate why the Moon has phases. Describe the setup and what you would observe at three key positions.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
**Setup:** Dark room. Lamp (Sun) at one end. Hold a white ball (Moon) at arm's length. Your head is Earth. **Position 1:** Ball between you and lamp → ball's lit side faces away → you see dark ball (New Moon). **Position 2:** Ball to your left, lamp in front → you see right half lit (First Quarter). **Position 3:** You between lamp and ball → ball fully lit facing you (Full Moon). A poor answer (e.g., moving the lamp instead of the ball, or casting a shadow of the head on the ball) reveals the student still thinks Earth's shadow causes phases or cannot adopt the Earth-centered perspective.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
**The Earth's shadow causes the Moon's phases.** Students often believe that the dark part of the Moon is Earth's shadow falling on it. This confusion arises because they know Earth casts a shadow (learned in eclipses) and see a dark portion on the Moon. In reality, the dark portion is the Moon's own night side — the hemisphere facing away from the Sun. Earth's shadow only hits the Moon during a lunar eclipse (rare, specific alignment), not every month.
## ON_FAILURE
1. In a dark room, hold a ball (Moon) near a single lamp (Sun). 2. Observe the ball from arm's length (Earth position) — you see phases. 3. Now deliberately cast **your head's shadow** on the ball — this is a lunar eclipse, notice it only happens when your head is exactly between lamp and ball. 4. Move the ball slightly above/below the shadow — phases return without any shadow. This proves phases are viewing-angle effects, not shadow effects.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: On a particular night, the Moon is observed high in the sky at sunset, appearing as a half-circle with the right side illuminated (in the northern hemisphere). Determine the approximate Sun-Earth-Moon angle and name the phase. Explain the reasoning using the geometry of the three bodies.

STEPS:
- step 1: Identify given information: Time = sunset (Sun on western horizon). Moon high in sky = roughly overhead. Illuminated side = right side (northern hemisphere view).
- step 2: Recall geometry: At sunset, the Sun is on the western horizon. The Moon is overhead, so the Sun-Earth-Moon angle is approximately 90° (Sun–Earth–Moon form a right angle at Earth).
- step 3: Determine which half is lit: Sunlight comes from the west (right side in northern hemisphere sky). The Moon's right side faces the Sun, so it is illuminated. The left side is dark.
- step 4: Match to phase: A half-illuminated Moon with the right side lit (in northern hemisphere) is the **First Quarter** phase (waxing).
- step 5: Verify consistency: First Quarter Moon rises at noon, is high at sunset, sets at midnight. This matches the observation.

REASONING: The phase is determined by the angle at Earth between the directions to the Sun and Moon. At sunset, the Sun is on the horizon; a Moon overhead makes a 90° angle. The illuminated side always points toward the Sun. In the northern hemisphere, when facing south, east is on the left and west on the right, so a right-side-lit Moon means the Sun is to the right (west), consistent with sunset.

ANSWER: Sun-Earth-Moon angle ≈ 90°; Phase = First Quarter (waxing half-moon).
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A student sees a thin crescent Moon just after sunset, low in the western sky. The crescent's illuminated edge faces the horizon. Draw the relative positions of Sun, Earth, and Moon at this moment and explain why the crescent is thin and oriented that way.

STEPS:
- step 1: Identify observation: Time = just after sunset. Moon low in western sky. Thin crescent, illuminated edge facing downward (toward horizon/Sun).
- step 2: Place Earth at center. Sun has just set in the west, so Sun direction is just below the western horizon.
- step 3: Moon is low in the west, so the Moon is also in the western sky, near the Sun. The angle between Sun and Moon as seen from Earth is small (< 45°).
- step 4: Determine illumination: Sunlight comes from the direction of the Sun (below the horizon). The Moon's hemisphere facing the Sun is lit. Since the Moon is near the Sun in the sky, we see mostly the dark side of the Moon, with only a thin sliver of the lit side visible — a crescent.
- step 5: Orientation: The lit edge faces the Sun (downward toward the horizon), matching the observation.

REASONING: When the Moon is close to the Sun in the sky (small elongation angle), we see mostly its night side. The bright crescent is the thin edge of the dayside that peeks around. The orientation of the crescent always points toward the Sun.

ANSWER: Sun, Earth, Moon nearly aligned with Moon slightly away from Sun (small angle). Phase = Waxing Crescent. The thin crescent appears because we see only a small fraction of the Moon's sunlit hemisphere; the lit edge faces the Sun (downward at sunset).
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Combine concepts: It is the night of Full Moon. At midnight, an observer in Delhi sees the Moon high in the sky. At the same moment, an observer in New York (approx. 10.5 hours behind Delhi) looks for the Moon. Where in the sky will the New York observer see the Moon, and what phase will they see? Explain using Earth's rotation and the Moon's orbital motion.

STEPS:
- step 1: Establish the global geometry: Full Moon means Sun–Earth–Moon alignment with Earth in middle. The Moon is opposite the Sun in the sky.
- step 2: Delhi at midnight: Midnight means Delhi is on the night side of Earth, facing away from the Sun. The Moon is opposite the Sun, so it is high in the sky (near zenith) for Delhi.
- step 3: Determine New York's position: New York is ~10.5 hours behind Delhi. When it is midnight in Delhi, it is 1:30 PM in New York (daytime).
- step 4: Earth's rotation: In 10.5 hours, Earth rotates ~157.5°. New York has rotated into daylight (facing the Sun).
- step 5: Moon's position for New York: Since the Moon is opposite the Sun, and New York faces the Sun, the Moon is on the opposite side of Earth — below the horizon for New York. It will rise around sunset in New York (~6 PM local time).
- step 6: Phase: The phase is the same globally — **Full Moon**. The Moon's orbital motion in 10.5 hours is negligible (~0.5°), so the phase does not change.

REASONING: Moon phases are global (same for all Earth observers at a given time) because they depend on the Sun-Earth-Moon angle, which is essentially the same from anywhere on Earth. Local sky position (altitude/azimuth) depends on the observer's latitude/longitude and time of day (Earth's rotation). At Full Moon, the Moon is opposite the Sun, so it is up at night and down during day.

ANSWER: New York observer will **not see the Moon** at that moment (it is below the horizon, daytime). When they see it later (at their sunset), it will still be **Full Moon**.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A crescent Moon is observed in the western sky just after sunset. Without using a diagram, explain why the illuminated crescent always faces the Sun (i.e., the horns point away from the Sun). Use the concept of straight-line light travel and the geometry of a sphere.

COMPETENCY: Interpreting physical situations using geometric optics principles

STEPS:
- step 1: Recognize that sunlight travels in straight lines from the Sun to the Moon.
- step 2: The Moon is a sphere; sunlight illuminates the hemisphere facing the Sun. The terminator (day-night line) is a great circle.
- step 3: From Earth, we see the Moon at an angle. The visible lit portion is the projection of the sunlit hemisphere onto our viewing direction.
- step 4: The boundary between lit and dark on the Moon's disk as we see it is the terminator. The lit side of that boundary faces the Sun because that is the direction the light comes from.
- step 5: Therefore, the crescent's convex (illuminated) edge always faces the Sun; the horns point away.

ANSWER: The illuminated crescent faces the Sun because sunlight travels in straight lines and strikes the Moon's near hemisphere. The line dividing light and dark (terminator) is perpendicular to the Sun's rays. From Earth, we see the lit portion as a crescent whose bright limb curves toward the Sun. This is a direct consequence of the Moon being a sphere lit by a distant source.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: An astronaut on the Moon looks at Earth during a Full Moon on Earth. What phase of Earth would the astronaut see? Explain using the relative positions of Sun, Earth, and Moon.

COMPETENCY: Predicting outcomes from changing reference frames (Earth-view vs Moon-view)

STEPS:
- step 1: Recall Full Moon geometry: Sun–Earth–Moon in line, Earth in middle. Sun and Moon on opposite sides of Earth.
- step 2: From the Moon's perspective, the Sun and Earth are in nearly the same direction (since Earth is between Sun and Moon? Wait: Full Moon means Earth is between Sun and Moon. So from Moon, Earth is in the direction of the Sun? No: Sun–Earth–Moon line. If you stand on Moon, Earth is between you and Sun? Actually, Sun–Earth–Moon: Sun shines on Earth, then Earth, then Moon. So from Moon, Earth is in the direction of the Sun. The Sun is behind Earth.)
- step 3: Therefore, the astronaut on the Moon sees the Earth with the Sun behind it (or nearly so). The Earth's hemisphere facing the Moon is fully illuminated by the Sun.
- step 4: The astronaut sees a **Full Earth** (fully lit disk). This is the complement: Full Moon on Earth ↔ Full Earth on Moon.
- step 5: Note: If it's a lunar eclipse (perfect alignment), the astronaut would see Earth blocking the Sun (a solar eclipse from Moon), with Earth's night side faintly lit by the solar corona. But for a normal Full Moon, the alignment is slightly off, so Earth appears fully lit.

ANSWER: The astronaut would see a **Full Earth** (fully illuminated disk). During Full Moon, the Sun, Earth, and Moon are aligned with Earth in the middle. From the Moon, the Earth lies in the direction of the Sun, so the Sun fully illuminates the Earth's hemisphere facing the Moon.
