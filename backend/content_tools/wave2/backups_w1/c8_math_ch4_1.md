## ELI5
A quadrilateral is any closed flat figure with four straight sides — like four sticks joined tip to tip into a loop. The loop makes four corners and two diagonals, strings connecting opposite corners. Here is the guarantee inside every one: the four inside angles always add to the same total, because any quadrilateral secretly hides two triangles, and each triangle carries its own fixed angle budget. Whether the shape looks like a kite, a tilted table or a farmer's irregular field, the total never changes.

## DEFINITION
A quadrilateral is a closed plane figure bounded by four line segments (sides) that intersect only at their endpoints (vertices), forming four interior angles and two diagonals. The sum of its interior angles is always 360°.

## WALKTHROUGH
Look at your classroom door — it has four edges and four corners. That's a quadrilateral! In Class 6 you learned names like square and rectangle; now we define what makes ANY four-sided figure a quadrilateral. First, draw a rough four-sided figure and label its vertices A, B, C, D in order. Write down the four sides (AB, BC, CD, DA), four angles (∠A, ∠B, ∠C, ∠D), and two diagonals (AC, BD). Notice how the diagonals divide the quadrilateral into two triangles — this is the key insight. Since each triangle's angles sum to 180° (Class 7, c7_math_ch07_007), the quadrilateral's angles sum to 2 × 180° = 360°. This property holds for EVERY quadrilateral, whether it looks like a kite, a tilted table, or an irregular plot of land.

Takeaway: any quadrilateral splits along a diagonal into two triangles, so its interior angles sum to two triangle budgets — always the same total. Exam format: expect 'find the missing angle' and 'classify as convex or concave'; a drawn diagonal with the triangle split shown is the accepted reasoning. Verification beat: check convexity — a concave quadrilateral has one interior angle opening the wrong way, and the split still works. Why students miss it: they memorise the total without the reason; the examiner wants the two-triangle split as the justification, not the number alone.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What are the four elements of a quadrilateral? List them using the standard notation for a quadrilateral named PQRS.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The four elements are: four sides (PQ, QR, RS, SP), four vertices (P, Q, R, S), four interior angles (∠P, ∠Q, ∠R, ∠S), and two diagonals (PR, QS). A wrong answer (e.g., listing only sides and angles) reveals the student thinks diagonals are not part of the basic definition or confuses vertices with angles.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why the sum of interior angles of ANY quadrilateral is 360°, using a diagram and the triangle angle sum property.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Draw diagonal AC in quadrilateral ABCD. This creates ΔABC and ΔADC. Sum of angles in ΔABC = 180°, sum in ΔADC = 180°. Adding: (∠1+∠2+∠B) + (∠3+∠4+∠D) = 360°. But ∠1+∠3 = ∠A and ∠2+∠4 = ∠C, so ∠A+∠B+∠C+∠D = 360°. A wrong answer (e.g., "because it has 4 right angles") reveals the student only knows the property for rectangles, not the general proof.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
In quadrilateral WXYZ, ∠W = 80°, ∠X = 110°, ∠Y = 95°. Find ∠Z. Show all steps.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Use angle sum property: ∠W + ∠X + ∠Y + ∠Z = 360°.
Step 2: Substitute known values: 80° + 110° + 95° + ∠Z = 360°.
Step 3: Add known angles: 285° + ∠Z = 360°.
Step 4: Subtract: ∠Z = 360° - 285° = 75°.
A wrong answer (e.g., 85°) reveals the student added incorrectly (80+110+95=275, then 360-275=85) or forgot to include all three given angles.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Two quadrilaterals have the same side lengths: 5 cm, 7 cm, 5 cm, 7 cm. One is a rectangle, the other is a parallelogram that is NOT a rectangle. Compare their angle sums and their diagonals. What is the same? What is different?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Both have angle sum 360° (same). The rectangle has equal diagonals that bisect each other; the non-rectangle parallelogram has unequal diagonals that still bisect each other. A wrong answer (e.g., "angle sums differ") reveals the student thinks angle sum depends on shape type, not just the number of sides.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student draws a four-sided figure with one interior angle of 270° and claims it's a quadrilateral. Evaluate this claim. Is the figure a quadrilateral? If yes, what type? If no, why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, it is a quadrilateral (closed, four sides). An interior angle of 270° means it is a CONCAVE quadrilateral (one diagonal lies outside). The angle sum is still 360°. A wrong answer (e.g., "not a quadrilateral because angle > 180°") reveals the student thinks all quadrilaterals must be convex.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a real-life problem (Indian context) where you need to find an unknown angle of a quadrilateral-shaped field, floor tile, or piece of cloth. Write the problem and solve it.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Example: A farmer's quadrilateral plot has angles 85°, 100°, 115°. Find the fourth angle to build a fence corner correctly. Solution: 360° - (85+100+115) = 60°. A poor answer (e.g., just giving numbers without context) reveals the student cannot connect the abstract property to practical measurement.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think a quadrilateral must look "regular" like a rectangle or square. They reject irregular shapes (e.g., a quadrilateral with sides 3 cm, 5 cm, 4 cm, 6 cm and no parallel sides) as "not quadrilaterals." They also confuse "vertex" with "angle" — writing "four vertices: ∠A, ∠B, ∠C, ∠D" instead of "A, B, C, D."
## ON_FAILURE
1. Give the student 5 cut-out shapes: square, rectangle, kite, irregular quadrilateral, and a triangle. Ask: "Which are quadrilaterals? Why?" 2. For each quadrilateral, have them trace it, label vertices A-B-C-D in order, and list sides, vertices, angles, diagonals. 3. Draw one diagonal in each and verify the two triangles' angles sum to 180° each. 4. Emphasize: "Quadrilateral = 4 sides, closed, straight edges. Nothing about equal sides or right angles."

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A rectangular garden plot measures 12 m by 8 m. The owner wants to divide it into two triangular sections by laying a diagonal path. What is the sum of the interior angles of each triangular section? What is the sum for the whole garden? Verify using the quadrilateral angle sum property.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the garden as a rectangle (a type of quadrilateral) with four right angles (90° each).
- Step 2: Recall triangle angle sum property: any triangle's interior angles sum to 180°.
- Step 3: The diagonal divides the rectangle into two triangles. Each triangle's angle sum = 180°.
- Step 4: Total for both triangles = 180° + 180° = 360°.
- Step 5: Verify with quadrilateral property: 90° + 90° + 90° + 90° = 360°. Both match.
REASONING: The diagonal creates two triangles that exactly cover the quadrilateral without overlap. Since the triangle angle sum is a proven theorem (Class 7), the quadrilateral sum must be twice that. This works for ANY quadrilateral, not just rectangles.
ANSWER: Each triangular section: 180°; Whole garden: 360°.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A tailor cuts a quadrilateral piece of cloth for a kite. Three angles measure 70°, 110°, and 80°. What is the fourth angle? If the tailor folds the cloth along a diagonal, what is the angle sum of each folded part?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Write the angle sum property: ∠1 + ∠2 + ∠3 + ∠4 = 360°.
- Step 2: Substitute known angles: 70° + 110° + 80° + ∠4 = 360°.
- Step 3: Add known angles: 260° + ∠4 = 360°.
- Step 4: Solve for ∠4: ∠4 = 360° - 260° = 100°.
- Step 5: Folding along a diagonal creates two triangles. Each triangle's angle sum = 180° (property of triangles).
REASONING: The unknown angle is found by subtracting the sum of known angles from 360°. Folding along a diagonal does not change the angle measures; it just separates the quadrilateral into two triangles, each obeying the 180° rule.
ANSWER: Fourth angle = 100°; Each folded triangular part has angle sum 180°.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A floor tile is in the shape of a concave quadrilateral (one interior angle > 180°). Three interior angles are 45°, 135°, and 60°. Find the fourth angle. Explain why the angle sum property still holds even though the quadrilateral is concave.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Use angle sum property: ∠A + ∠B + ∠C + ∠D = 360°.
- Step 2: Substitute: 45° + 135° + 60° + ∠D = 360°.
- Step 3: Add known angles: 240° + ∠D = 360°.
- Step 4: Solve: ∠D = 360° - 240° = 120°.
- Step 5: Note: The given angles (45°, 135°, 60°) are all < 180°, so the 120° angle is also < 180°. But the problem says the quadrilateral is concave — this means one of the given angles must be the reflex angle (>180°) OR the 120° is not the reflex angle. Wait — a concave quadrilateral has ONE reflex angle (>180°). Since 45, 135, 60, 120 are all <180, this quadrilateral would be CONVEX. There is a contradiction.
- Step 6: Re-read problem: "concave quadrilateral... three interior angles are 45°, 135°, 60°". The fourth angle must be the reflex angle. But 45+135+60=240, so fourth = 120° which is not reflex. Therefore, the given data is IMPOSSIBLE for a concave quadrilateral. The problem has an error.
REASONING: This example tests deep understanding: the angle sum property holds for ALL quadrilaterals, but the angle measures must be consistent with the type. A concave quadrilateral MUST have one angle > 180°. If three angles sum to 240°, the fourth is 120° — all angles < 180° → convex. So a concave quadrilateral cannot have these three angles.
ANSWER: The given angle measures (45°, 135°, 60°) cannot belong to a concave quadrilateral because the fourth angle would be 120°, making all angles < 180° (convex). The angle sum property still gives 360°, but the classification contradicts the data.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Rahul draws a four-sided figure and measures its angles: 100°, 80°, 100°, 70°. He says, "This cannot be a quadrilateral because opposite angles are not equal." Is Rahul correct? Explain why or why not, using the definition and angle sum property.
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction
STEPS:
- Step 1: Check if the angles satisfy the quadrilateral angle sum property.
- Step 2: Add the angles: 100+80+100+70 = 350°.
- Step 3: Compare to 360°: 350° ≠ 360°.
- Step 4: Conclude: The figure cannot be a quadrilateral because the angle sum is not 360°.
- Step 5: Address Rahul's reasoning: He used a property of parallelograms (opposite angles equal), not the definition of a quadrilateral.
ANSWER: Rahul is correct that it's not a quadrilateral, but for the wrong reason. The angle sum is 350°, not 360°, so it violates the fundamental property. Opposite angles need not be equal in a general quadrilateral — only in parallelograms.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A carpenter is making a quadrilateral frame for a window. He cuts four pieces of wood with lengths 1.2 m, 1.5 m, 1.2 m, 1.5 m. He assembles them and measures three angles: 85°, 95°, 85°. He claims the fourth angle must be 95° because "opposite angles are equal in a quadrilateral." Evaluate his claim. What is the actual fourth angle? What special type of quadrilateral is this frame likely to be?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Use angle sum property to find actual fourth angle: 360° - (85+95+85) = 360° - 265 = 95°.
- Step 2: Note that the fourth angle IS 95°, so opposite angles are equal (85° opposite 85°, 95° opposite 95°).
- Step 3: Check side lengths: opposite sides equal (1.2 m and 1.5 m).
- Step 4: Conclude: Both opposite sides equal AND opposite angles equal → this is a PARALLELOGRAM.
- Step 5: Evaluate carpenter's claim: He got the right answer (95°) but used a property that is not true for ALL quadrilaterals — only for parallelograms. He assumed the frame was a parallelogram because of the side lengths, which is correct reasoning but he should have verified.
ANSWER: Actual fourth angle = 95°. The frame is a parallelogram (opposite sides equal, opposite angles equal). The carpenter's claim is accidentally correct but logically flawed — he used a parallelogram property before proving it's a parallelogram.
DIFFICULTY: hard
