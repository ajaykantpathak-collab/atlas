## ELI5
Multiply a sum by the matching difference and the cross terms fight to a draw — plus-the-product meets minus-the-product and both vanish. What survives is pure contrast: the first term squared minus the second term squared. This identity is a two-way tool — run forward to expand such a product instantly, run backward to factorise a difference of squares into a tidy bracket pair. Mental arithmetic borrows it too: products near a round number collapse in one thought.

## DEFINITION
The algebraic identity \((a + b)(a - b) = a^2 - b^2\) states that the product of the sum and difference of the same two terms equals the difference of their squares. It holds for any real numbers or algebraic expressions \(a\) and \(b\).
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can recognise the \((a+b)(a-b)\) pattern in multiplication problems, correctly apply the identity to expand or simplify expressions, and use it to factorise differences of squares. They can also evaluate numerical products mentally using this identity.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the derivation, application, and factorisation use of the identity \((a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2\) for binomials with real coefficients. It does not cover higher-degree polynomial identities, complex numbers, or trigonometric substitutions.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine a rectangular garden where the length is 5 metres more than a side \(x\) and the breadth is 5 metres less than the same side \(x\). The area is \((x+5)(x-5)\). Instead of multiplying term-by-term, notice that the middle terms \(+5x\) and \(-5x\) cancel out, leaving only \(x^2 - 25\). This pattern occurs whenever we multiply a sum by a difference of the same two terms. We first write the two binomials, identify \(a\) and \(b\), then directly write \(a^2 - b^2\). This connects to the distributive property (c8_math_ch6_1) where we saw cross terms cancel when signs are opposite.

Takeaway: sum times matching difference equals first squared minus second squared — the cross terms cancel because the signs oppose — and the identity works equally as a factoriser of differences of squares. Exam format: expect forward expansions, reverse factorisations, and mental multiplication of numbers straddling a round value, each with the identity quoted first. Verification beat: confirm the two brackets share the same first term and opposite second terms before applying; a mismatched pair is not this identity. Why students miss it: they add the squares instead of subtracting, trusting a plus out of habit — expand one tiny case and watch the middle terms cancel to a minus. The pattern's power is the cancellation: signs in opposition empty the middle entirely.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
State the algebraic identity for the product of \((a + b)\) and \((a - b)\).
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The identity is \((a + b)(a - b) = a^2 - b^2\). A wrong answer like \((a+b)(a-b)=a^2+b^2\) reveals the student confuses this with the square of a sum and does not understand the cancellation of the middle terms. **A wrong answer (sum of squares) reveals the student has misremembered the sign of the payoff — the product of a sum and difference always lands on a difference of squares, because the opposing cross terms annihilate each other.** Demonstrate with a market bargain in Varanasi: buying at one price and selling at a matching discount leaves you the gap between the two squared amounts, never their total — the middle money simply cancels in the exchange.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why the middle terms cancel out when expanding \((a + b)(a - b)\) using the distributive property.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Expanding gives \(a(a-b) + b(a-b) = a^2 - ab + ab - b^2\). The terms \(-ab\) and \(+ab\) are additive inverses, so they sum to zero. If a student says "they cancel because one is plus and one is minus" without showing the distributive steps, they lack a structural understanding of why the cancellation occurs.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Use the identity to find the product \((2x + 3)(2x - 3)\) and then evaluate it for \(x = 4\).
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Here \(a = 2x\) and \(b = 3\). Using the identity: \((2x)^2 - 3^2 = 4x^2 - 9\). For \(x=4\): \(4(4)^2 - 9 = 4 \times 16 - 9 = 64 - 9 = 55\). A wrong answer like \(2x^2 - 9\) shows the student forgot to square the coefficient of \(x\) (treating \(a\) as \(x\) instead of \(2x\)).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the expansions of \((a + b)^2\) and \((a + b)(a - b)\). What is the same and what is different? How does the sign between the terms affect the result?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Both give \(a^2\) and \(b^2\) terms. \((a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2\) has a middle term \(2ab\) and a plus sign before \(b^2\). \((a+b)(a-b) = a^2 - b^2\) has no middle term and a minus sign before \(b^2\). The sign in the second binomial changes the cross terms from additive to subtractive, causing cancellation. A student who says "both are the same except the sign" without mentioning the missing middle term misses the structural difference.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student wrote: \((3y + 4)(3y - 4) = 9y - 16\). Identify the error and correct it.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The error is that the student wrote \(9y\) instead of \(9y^2\). They squared the coefficient 3 to get 9 but forgot to square the variable \(y\). The correct expansion is \((3y)^2 - 4^2 = 9y^2 - 16\). This reveals a common mistake: squaring only the numerical coefficient and not the entire term \(a = 3y\).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a real-life problem (using metres, rupees, or kilograms) that can be solved using the identity \((a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2\). Write the problem and show the solution.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Example: A square cloth of side \((x+2)\) metres has a smaller square of side \((x-2)\) metres cut from its centre. Find the area of the remaining frame. Solution: Area = \((x+2)^2 - (x-2)^2\). This is not directly the identity. A better problem: A rectangular plot has length \((50+3)\) m and breadth \((50-3)\) m. Find its area using the identity. Area = \(50^2 - 3^2 = 2500 - 9 = 2491\) m². A poor answer would give a problem that does not fit the sum-difference pattern, showing the student cannot recognise the structure in context.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often treat \(a\) as only the variable part, ignoring the coefficient. For example, in \((3x+5)(3x-5)\), they write \(a = x\) and get \(x^2 - 25\) instead of correctly identifying \(a = 3x\) to get \(9x^2 - 25\). Another frequent error is writing \((a+b)(a-b) = a^2 + b^2\) by confusing it with \((a+b)^2\) and forgetting the minus sign on \(b^2\).
## ON_FAILURE
If a student makes the coefficient error, have them first expand \((3x+5)(3x-5)\) fully using the distributive property: \(3x(3x-5) + 5(3x-5) = 9x^2 -15x +15x -25 = 9x^2 -25\). Then ask: "What is \(a\) here? What is \(a^2\)?" Guide them to see \(a = 3x\), so \(a^2 = (3x)^2 = 9x^2\). Practice with \((2y+7)(2y-7)\), \((5m+2)(5m-2)\) until they consistently square the entire term.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A rectangular garden has length \((x + 4)\) metres and breadth \((x - 4)\) metres. Find the area of the garden in terms of \(x\). If \(x = 10\) m, what is the numerical area?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the formula for area of a rectangle: Area = length × breadth = \((x + 4)(x - 4)\).
- Step 2: Recognise the pattern \((a + b)(a - b)\) with \(a = x\) and \(b = 4\).
- Step 3: Apply the identity: \((x + 4)(x - 4) = x^2 - 4^2 = x^2 - 16\).
- Step 4: Substitute \(x = 10\) into the simplified expression: Area = \(10^2 - 16 = 100 - 16 = 84\).
- Step 5: State the answer with units: The area is \(x^2 - 16\) square metres; when \(x = 10\) m, the area is 84 m².
REASONING: The identity avoids the longer distributive expansion and directly gives the simplified form. The cancellation of the middle terms (\(+4x\) and \(-4x\)) is why the area depends only on \(x^2\) and the constant.
ANSWER: Area = \(x^2 - 16\) m²; for \(x = 10\) m, Area = 84 m².
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Simplify \((3y + 5)(3y - 5)\) using the identity. Then evaluate the expression for \(y = 2\).
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify \(a\) and \(b\) in the given product. Here the first term in both binomials is \(3y\), so \(a = 3y\). The second term is \(5\), so \(b = 5\).
- Step 2: Write the identity: \((a + b)(a - b) = a^2 - b^2\).
- Step 3: Substitute \(a = 3y\) and \(b = 5\): \((3y)^2 - 5^2\).
- Step 4: Compute the squares: \((3y)^2 = 9y^2\) and \(5^2 = 25\). So the simplified expression is \(9y^2 - 25\).
- Step 5: Evaluate for \(y = 2\): \(9(2)^2 - 25 = 9 \times 4 - 25 = 36 - 25 = 11\).
REASONING: It is crucial to square the entire term \(a = 3y\), not just the variable \(y\). The identity reduces the multiplication to a simple subtraction of squares.
ANSWER: Simplified expression: \(9y^2 - 25\); Value at \(y = 2\): 11.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Factorise \(49x^2 - 64y^2\) using the identity. Hence, find the value of \(49 \times 13^2 - 64 \times 5^2\) without direct multiplication of large numbers.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Recognise that \(49x^2 = (7x)^2\) and \(64y^2 = (8y)^2\). So the expression is a difference of squares: \((7x)^2 - (8y)^2\).
- Step 2: Apply the reverse of the identity: \(a^2 - b^2 = (a + b)(a - b)\). Here \(a = 7x\), \(b = 8y\).
- Step 3: Write the factorised form: \((7x + 8y)(7x - 8y)\).
- Step 4: For the numerical evaluation, let \(x = 13\) and \(y = 5\). Then \(49 \times 13^2 - 64 \times 5^2 = (7 \times 13)^2 - (8 \times 5)^2\).
- Step 5: Use the factorised form with these values: \((7 \times 13 + 8 \times 5)(7 \times 13 - 8 \times 5) = (91 + 40)(91 - 40) = 131 \times 51\).
- Step 6: Compute \(131 \times 51 = 131 \times (50 + 1) = 6550 + 131 = 6681\).
REASONING: The identity works both ways: expanding a product and factorising a difference of squares. Using the factorised form simplifies the numerical calculation by avoiding large squares.
ANSWER: Factorised form: \((7x + 8y)(7x - 8y)\); Numerical value: 6681.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Riya says \((2a + 3b)(2a - 3b) = 4a^2 - 9b^2\). Rahul says it is \(2a^2 - 3b^2\). Who is correct? Explain the mistake made by the incorrect student using the concept of "squaring the entire term".
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify \(a\) and \(b\) in the identity for the given expression.
- Step 2: Apply the identity correctly, squaring the entire terms.
- Step 3: Compare with both students' answers.
- Step 4: Explain the specific error.
ANSWER: Riya is correct. In the expression \((2a + 3b)(2a - 3b)\), the first term is \(2a\) (so \(a = 2a\) in the identity) and the second term is \(3b\) (so \(b = 3b\)). Applying the identity: \((2a)^2 - (3b)^2 = 4a^2 - 9b^2\). Rahul incorrectly used \(a = a\) and \(b = b\), squaring only the variables and not the coefficients. He treated the terms as \(a\) and \(b\) instead of \(2a\) and \(3b\).
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A square sheet of side \((5m + 2n)\) cm has a square hole of side \((5m - 2n)\) cm cut out from its centre. Find the area of the remaining frame in terms of \(m\) and \(n\). If \(m = 3\) cm and \(n = 1\) cm, calculate the numerical area.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions
STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand the problem: Area of frame = Area of outer square - Area of inner square.
- Step 2: Write expressions for both areas: Outer area = \((5m + 2n)^2\), Inner area = \((5m - 2n)^2\).
- Step 3: Recognise that the difference of squares can be written using the identity \((a+b)^2 - (a-b)^2 = 4ab\). Alternatively, use the identity directly on the product form: The frame area is also \(( (5m+2n) + (5m-2n) ) \times ( (5m+2n) - (5m-2n) ) = (10m)(4n) = 40mn\).
- Step 4: Substitute \(m = 3\), \(n = 1\): Area = \(40 \times 3 \times 1 = 120\) cm².
- Step 5: Verify by computing squares: \((17)^2 - (13)^2 = 289 - 169 = 120\).
ANSWER: Area of frame = \(40mn\) cm²; for \(m=3, n=1\), Area = 120 cm².
DIFFICULTY: hard
