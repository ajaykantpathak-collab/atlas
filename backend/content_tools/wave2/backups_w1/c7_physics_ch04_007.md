## ELI5
Ride from home to the neighbourhood shop and the clock on the wall takes attendance: that is the whole triangle - speed equals distance divided by time. Know any two, and algebra hands you the third: distance is speed times time; time is distance over speed. The formula is a see-saw with three seats. Cyclists, trains and cricket throws all obey it; only the numbers change jerseys.

## DEFINITION
The mathematical relationship between speed ($v$), distance ($d$), and time ($t$) states that speed is equal to distance divided by time ($v = \frac{d}{t}$), allowing us to calculate any one of these three quantities if the other two are known. In the International System of Units (SI), distance is measured in metres (m), time in seconds (s), and speed in metres per second (m/s).

## WALKTHROUGH
When you ride your bicycle from your home to a nearby shop in your neighbourhood, you notice that it takes a certain duration of clock time to cover that length of the road. To begin understanding this mathematically, we measure the total path length (distance) and the duration of clock time elapsed during the journey. Building on our prior understanding of how objects are classified as slow or fast, we now connect these observations into a precise algebraic relationship: speed equals distance divided by time. By rearranging this core formula, if we know how fast we are moving and for how long, we can predict the exact distance we will travel.

**Takeaway:** one formula, three doors: v equals d over t - rearrange to fetch whichever quantity is hiding. **In the exam:** write the base formula first, then rearrange on paper - substituting into the wrong rearrangement is the classic slip. **Check yourself:** time the walkthrough's ride to the shop and note the duration - which two seats of the see-saw do you already hold? **Why students miss it:** students memorise three formulas and confuse the rearrangements - the flagged clutter. One base equation, rearranged in front of the examiner, is safer than recall. Show the algebra, and the units check your arithmetic for free.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The SI unit of speed is metres per second (m/s), which is derived from the fundamental SI units of length (metre, m) and time (second, s). A wrong answer often confuses the unit of speed with speed limits in kilometres per hour (km/h) or confuses distance with time, showing a failure to recall standard SI definitions.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If a school bus travels twice as far in the exact same amount of time as it did yesterday, what happens to its average speed? Explain why using the relationship between speed and distance.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Its average speed doubles. According to the formula $v = \frac{d}{t}$, speed is directly proportional to distance when time remains constant ($t$ is unchanged), meaning doubling the distance ($2d$) while keeping time constant results in twice the speed ($\frac{2d}{t} = 2v$). A wrong answer usually assumes speed stays constant regardless of distance, indicating a lack of comprehension of how numerator changes affect quotients.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A student cycles from their house to a park, covering a distance of 600 metres in 120 seconds. Calculate the average speed of the student in m/s.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The speed is 5 m/s. 
Step 1: Identify given values: Distance ($d$) = 600 m, Time ($t$) = 120 s. 
Step 2: State the formula: $v = \frac{d}{t}$. 
Step 3: Substitute values with units: $v = \frac{600 \text{ m}}{120 \text{ s}}$. 
Step 4: Compute and cancel units: $v = 5 \text{ m/s}$. 
Step 5: Verify the answer is physically reasonable for a bicycle: 5 m/s is about 18 km/h, which is a normal cycling speed. A wrong answer like 72000 or 0.2 indicates the student multiplied or divided in the wrong order (e.g., $d \times t$ or $t / d$).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
An auto-rickshaw travels at a constant speed of 10 m/s for 3 minutes. Determine the total distance it covers in metres.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The distance is 1800 metres. 
Step 1: Identify given values: Speed ($v$) = 10 m/s, Time ($t$) = 3 minutes. 
Step 2: Convert time into SI units (seconds): $3 \text{ min} \times 60 \text{ s/min} = 180 \text{ s}$. 
Step 3: Rearrange the formula to solve for distance: $d = v \times t$. 
Step 4: Substitute values: $d = 10 \text{ m/s} \times 180 \text{ s} = 1800 \text{ m}$. 
A wrong answer of 30 metres occurs if the student forgets to convert minutes into seconds, directly multiplying 10 by 3 instead of 180.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Two runners participate in a 100-metre race. Runner A finishes in 10 seconds, and Runner B finishes in 12.5 seconds. A classmate claims Runner B ran faster because they spent more time on the track. Evaluate this claim.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The classmate's claim is false. Runner A's speed is $\frac{100 \text{ m}}{10 \text{ s}} = 10 \text{ m/s}$, while Runner B's speed is $\frac{100 \text{ m}}{12.5 \text{ s}} = 8 \text{ m/s}$. Since time is in the denominator of the speed formula ($v = \frac{d}{t}$), a smaller time taken to cover the same distance means a higher speed. A wrong answer agrees with the classmate, showing a failure to understand that spending more time to cover a fixed distance means moving slower, not faster.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple, low-cost experiment using a rolling marble, a metre scale, and a digital stopwatch to verify that doubling the time travelled at a constant speed doubles the distance covered.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
To perform this, release a rolling marble along a smooth floor and use a stopwatch to record how far it travels in 2 seconds, marking the stopping point with chalk and measuring the distance. Then, allow the marble to roll for 4 seconds (double the time) while maintaining a uniform rolling speed, and measure the new distance. You will find that the distance at 4 seconds is twice the distance measured at 2 seconds, verifying direct proportionality ($d = v \times t$). A poor answer fails to keep speed constant or lacks a method to measure time accurately.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often mix up the arrangement of the formula variables, writing $d = \frac{v}{t}$ or $t = v \times d$ because they memorise letters rather than understanding the physical meaning. For instance, if asked to find distance given speed and time, a struggling student might divide speed by time instead of multiplying, producing physically impossible results like a bicycle travelling 0.05 metres in 2 hours at 10 m/s.
## ON_FAILURE
To fix this misconception, guide the student to use unit analysis (dimensional reasoning) as a sanity check. Remind them that speed has units of distance over time (m/s). Therefore, to find distance (metres), we must multiply speed (metres per second) by time (seconds) so that the 'seconds' unit cancels out: $\text{m/s} \times \text{s} = \text{m}$. Have them practice checking units in every calculation before plugging in numbers.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A delivery scooter travels through a local market street at a constant speed of 8 m/s for 5 minutes. Calculate the total distance covered by the scooter in metres.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given values and what needs to be calculated. Speed ($v$) = 8 m/s, Time ($t$) = 5 minutes. Find distance ($d$).
- Step 2: Check units for consistency. Time is given in minutes, but speed uses seconds. Convert time to seconds: $5 \text{ min} \times 60 \text{ s/min} = 300 \text{ s}$.
- Step 3: Select the appropriate rearranged formula based on $v = \frac{d}{t}$: $d = v \times t$.
- Step 4: Substitute the values with their units into the formula: $d = 8 \text{ m/s} \times 300 \text{ s}$.
- Step 5: Compute the numerical value and cancel the units ($\text{m/s} \times \text{s} = \text{m}$): $d = 2400 \text{ m}$. Verify that 2400 metres (2.4 km) is a reasonable distance for a scooter in 5 minutes at 8 m/s.
REASONING: Multiplying speed by time cancels out the time unit in the denominator, leaving only the distance unit (metres). Unit cancellation guarantees that the algebraic rearrangement was performed correctly.
ANSWER: 2400 m
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: An express train covers a distance of 150 kilometres between two stations in 2 hours. Calculate its average speed in kilometres per hour (km/h) and then convert it to metres per second (m/s).
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given values: Distance ($d$) = 150 km, Time ($t$) = 2 hours. Find speed ($v$).
- Step 2: Apply the speed formula: $v = \frac{d}{t}$.
- Step 3: Substitute values in km and hours: $v = \frac{150 \text{ km}}{2 \text{ h}} = 75 \text{ km/h}$.
- Step 4: Convert km/h to m/s by multiplying by $\frac{5}{18}$ (since $1 \text{ km} = 1000 \text{ m}$ and $1 \text{ h} = 3600 \text{ s}$, $\frac{1000}{3600} = \frac{5}{18}$): $v = 75 \times \frac{5}{18} \text{ m/s}$.
- Step 5: Compute the final value: $v = \frac{375}{18} \approx 20.83 \text{ m/s}$. Verify that a train speed of 75 km/h (20.83 m/s) is realistic.
REASONING: Converting units step-by-step ensures that compound units like km/h are correctly translated into standard SI units (m/s) without arithmetic errors.
ANSWER: 75 km/h (or 20.83 m/s)
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A cyclist starts a journey and travels for 30 minutes at a speed of 6 m/s, and then stops for 10 minutes to rest, and finally travels another 1800 metres in 300 seconds. Calculate the total distance covered for the entire journey and the overall average speed in m/s.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Break the journey into parts. Part 1: $v_1 = 6 \text{ m/s}$, $t_1 = 30 \text{ min} = 1800 \text{ s}$. Part 2 (Rest): $v_2 = 0 \text{ m/s}$, $t_2 = 10 \text{ min} = 600 \text{ s}$. Part 3: $d_3 = 1800 \text{ m}$, $t_3 = 300 \text{ s}$.
- Step 2: Calculate distance for Part 1 using $d = v \times t$: $d_1 = 6 \text{ m/s} \times 1800 \text{ s} = 10800 \text{ m}$.
- Step 3: Calculate distance for Part 2: $d_2 = 0 \text{ m/s} \times 600 \text{ s} = 0 \text{ m}$ (resting distance is zero).
- Step 4: Sum all distances to find total distance ($d_{\text{total}}$): $d_{\text{total}} = 10800 \text{ m} + 0 \text{ m} + 1800 \text{ m} = 12600 \text{ m}$.
- Step 5: Sum all time intervals to find total time ($t_{\text{total}}$): $t_{\text{total}} = 1800 \text{ s} + 600 \text{ s} + 300 \text{ s} = 2700 \text{ s}$.
- Step 6: Calculate overall average speed using total distance divided by total time: $v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{12600 \text{ m}}{2700 \text{ s}} = 4.67 \text{ m/s}$. Verify reasonableness: 4.67 m/s is roughly 16.8 km/h, which is a sensible average cycling speed including rest stops.
REASONING: Multi-part motion problems require calculating each segment's distance and time separately before combining them, because average speed is total distance divided by total time, not the simple average of individual speeds.
ANSWER: 12600 m total distance; 4.67 m/s average speed
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Two cars, Car X and Car Y, travel along a straight highway. Car X travels 150 km in 2 hours. Car Y travels 300 km in 4 hours. A student states that Car Y must be moving faster because it covered a greater total distance. Do you agree with the student? Justify your answer with calculations.
COMPETENCY: Interpreting physical situations using formulas
STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand that speed depends on both distance and time, not distance alone.
- Step 2: Calculate the speed of Car X: $v_X = \frac{150 \text{ km}}{2 \text{ h}} = 75 \text{ km/h}$.
- Step 3: Calculate the speed of Car Y: $v_Y = \frac{300 \text{ km}}{4 \text{ h}} = 75 \text{ km/h}$.
- Step 4: Compare the two speeds and conclude that both cars are moving at the exact same speed.
ANSWER: The student is incorrect. Although Car Y covered a greater distance, it also took proportionally more time (4 hours instead of 2). Calculating the speed for both reveals that both Car X and Car Y travel at exactly 75 km/h, meaning their speeds are equal.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A school runner is training for a 400-metre race. On a windy day, the runner's coach notices that the runner covers the first 200 metres in 25 seconds, but the second 200 metres takes 35 seconds due to a strong headwind. Calculate the average speed for the first half, the second half, and the entire 400-metre run. Explain why the overall average speed is not simply the arithmetic mean of the two speeds.
COMPETENCY: Unit analysis and dimensional reasoning
STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate speed for the first half: $v_1 = \frac{200 \text{ m}}{25 \text{ s}} = 8 \text{ m/s}$.
- Step 2: Calculate speed for the second half: $v_2 = \frac{200 \text{ m}}{35 \text{ s}} \approx 5.71 \text{ m/s}$.
- Step 3: Calculate the arithmetic mean of the two speeds: $\frac{8 + 5.71}{2} = 6.86 \text{ m/s}$.
- Step 4: Calculate the true overall average speed using total distance and total time: $d_{\text{total}} = 400 \text{ m}$, $t_{\text{total}} = 25 + 35 = 60 \text{ s}$. $v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{400 \text{ m}}{60 \text{ s}} = 6.67 \text{ m/s}$.
- Step 5: Explain the discrepancy: The runner spent more time travelling at the slower speed, so the time-weighted average is pulled closer to the slower speed than the simple arithmetic mean.
ANSWER: The average speed for the first half is 8 m/s, the second half is 5.71 m/s, and the true overall average speed is 6.67 m/s. The arithmetic mean (6.86 m/s) is incorrect because the runner spent more time running slowly in the second half, giving that slower speed a greater weight in the overall motion.
DIFFICULTY: hard
