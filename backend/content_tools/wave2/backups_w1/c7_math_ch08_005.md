## ELI5
Three-quarters of a water drum shared between two classrooms: dividing a fraction by a whole number splits it into that many equal parts, which is the same as multiplying by the reciprocal - one over the whole number. So each classroom gets three-eighths of the drum. The common error multiplies instead of sharing, swelling water the camp never had. Division by a whole number shrinks; multiplication by one swells. Ask 'how much per part?' and the operation chooses itself.

## DEFINITION
Dividing a fraction by a whole number means partitioning a fractional quantity into an equal number of whole parts, which is mathematically equivalent to multiplying the fraction by the multiplicative inverse (reciprocal) of that whole number.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
Can compute the division of any proper, improper, or mixed fraction by a non-zero whole number using the reciprocal method, simplify the resulting fraction to its lowest terms, and solve multi-step real-world word problems involving shared quantities.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers the division of fractions (proper, improper, mixed) by non-zero whole numbers and simple contextual word problems. It does NOT cover dividing by zero or complex fractions involving algebraic variables in denominators within this specific node.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you have $\frac{3}{4}$ of a large drum of drinking water left after a school camp, and you want to share it equally among 2 classrooms for watering plants. How much of the total drum does each classroom get? To solve this, we need to divide a fraction ($\frac{3}{4}$) by a whole number ($2$). First, recall from Class 6 that a whole number like $2$ can be written as a fraction with a denominator of $1$, which is $\frac{2}{1}$. Next, apply the rule learned in fraction division: to divide by a fraction, multiply by its reciprocal. The reciprocal of $\frac{2}{1}$ is $\frac{1}{2}$. Finally, multiply the numerators together and the denominators together to find that each classroom gets $\frac{3}{8}$ of the drum.

**Takeaway:** dividing a fraction by a whole number means equal sharing: multiply by the reciprocal, one over the number. **In the exam:** write the whole number as a fraction over one, flip it, multiply, and simplify with units intact. **Check yourself:** share the drum's remainder between the two classrooms on paper and sanity-check the shrinkage. **Why students miss it:** they multiply by the whole number instead of its reciprocal and the water supply grows magically. Imagine the actual drum pouring into two buckets, and the shrinkage becomes impossible to forget.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The reciprocal is $\frac{1}{n}$. A wrong answer like writing $n$ itself reveals a failure to understand that the inverse of a whole number requires swapping its numerator and denominator (since $n = \frac{n}{1}$).

**A wrong answer (writing n itself) reveals the student flipped nothing** - the reciprocal turns the fraction upside down, and a whole number written over 1 is no exception.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Dividing by a number means finding how much one part is when the total is shared equally into $c$ parts, which is mathematically identical to taking a fraction $\frac{1}{c}$ of that amount. A wrong answer shows confusion between multiplication and division operations or treating whole numbers as fractions incorrectly.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Calculate $\frac{5}{8} \div 4$ and write your answer in its simplest form.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
First, express the whole number $4$ as $\frac{4}{1}$. Next, change the division sign to multiplication and use the reciprocal of $\frac{4}{1}$, which is $\frac{1}{4}$. Now multiply: $\frac{5}{8} \times \frac{1}{4} = \frac{5 \times 1}{8 \times 4} = \frac{5}{32}$. A wrong answer like $\frac{20}{8}$ or $\frac{5}{2}$ indicates the student multiplied by the whole number directly or inverted the fraction instead of the whole number.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the result of $\frac{4}{5} \div 2$ with $\frac{4}{5} \times 2$. What pattern do you notice about dividing a fraction by a whole number versus multiplying it by the same number?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
$\frac{4}{5} \div 2 = \frac{2}{5}$, whereas $\frac{4}{5} \times 2 = \frac{8}{5}$. Dividing by a whole number makes the fraction smaller (reducing each part), while multiplying by a whole number makes it larger. A wrong answer shows a lack of conceptual understanding of scale changes under operations.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5

The correct method is $\frac{6}{7} \times \frac{1}{3} = \frac{6 \div 3}{7} = \frac{2}{7}$ (after cross-simplifying). ## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often divide the numerator by the whole number while mistakenly leaving the denominator unchanged, or they invert the fraction instead of the whole number. For example, when calculating $\frac{4}{9} \div 2$, a student might compute $\frac{4 \div 2}{9} = \frac{2}{9}$ (which happens to work here because 4 is divisible by 2), but when calculating $\frac{3}{5} \div 2$, they might write $\frac{3 \div 2}{5}$ which results in a complex fraction, or they might flip the fraction to get $\frac{5}{3} \times 2 = \frac{10}{3}$.
## ON_FAILURE
Remind the student that a whole number $c$ is always written as $\frac{c}{1}$. Tell them: "Stop and write out the two steps clearly: Step 1, turn the whole number into a fraction over 1. Step 2, flip that second fraction upside down and change division to multiplication." Practice with a non-divisible numerator first, such as $\frac{3}{4} \div 5$, showing that $\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{1}{5} = \frac{3}{20}$.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Priya has $\frac{3}{5}$ of a litre of milk in a container. She pours it equally into 4 small glasses. What fraction of a litre of milk is in each glass?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the given quantity of milk ($\frac{3}{5}$ litre) and the number of equal parts to divide into ($4$ glasses). Write the division expression as $\frac{3}{5} \div 4$.
- Step 2: Convert the whole number divisor $4$ into a fraction with a denominator of $1$, giving $\frac{4}{1}$.
- Step 3: Rewrite the division problem as multiplication by the reciprocal of the divisor: $\frac{3}{5} \times \frac{1}{4}$.
- Step 4: Multiply the numerators together ($3 \times 1 = 3$) and the denominators together ($5 \times 4 = 20$) to get the product $\frac{3}{20}$.
- Step 5: Verify that the fraction is in its lowest terms and state the final answer with units: $\frac{3}{20}$ of a litre.
REASONING: Dividing a fractional amount among equal groups is equivalent to finding a fraction of that amount, which is achieved by multiplying by the reciprocal of the divisor.
ANSWER: $\frac{3}{20}$ litres
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: An athlete runs a total distance of $\frac{7}{3}$ kilometres during a training session divided equally across 5 laps around the school running track. What is the length of one lap?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the total distance ($\frac{7}{3}$ km) and the number of laps ($5$). Set up the equation: $\frac{7}{3} \div 5$.
- Step 2: Express the whole number $5$ as a fraction: $\frac{5}{1}$.
- Step 3: Apply the reciprocal rule by changing division to multiplication and inverting $\frac{5}{1}$ to $\frac{1}{5}$: $\frac{7}{3} \times \frac{1}{5}$.
- Step 4: Perform the multiplication of numerators and denominators: $\frac{7 \times 1}{3 \times 5} = \frac{7}{15}$.
- Step 5: Check if simplification is possible (7 and 15 share no common factors other than 1). Conclude with units: $\frac{7}{15}$ kilometres per lap.
REASONING: Converting the improper fraction division into multiplication by the reciprocal ensures accurate calculation of unit measurement per share.
ANSWER: $\frac{7}{15}$ km
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A recipe calls for $\frac{5}{6}$ kg of flour to bake a batch of biscuits. If the recipe is split evenly to be shared among 3 baking groups in a cooking club, and each group uses their flour equally for 2 days, what fraction of a kg of flour does each group use per day? (Hint: This is a multi-step problem involving division by a whole number twice, or division by the product of whole numbers).
STEPS:
- Step 1: Find the amount of flour each baking group gets by dividing the total flour ($\frac{5}{6}$ kg) by the number of groups ($3$). Expression: $\frac{5}{6} \div 3$.
- Step 2: Convert $3$ to $\frac{3}{1}$ and multiply by its reciprocal: $\frac{5}{6} \times \frac{1}{3} = \frac{5}{18}$ kg per group.
- Step 3: Now, divide this amount ($\frac{5}{18}$ kg) by the number of days ($2$) to find the daily usage per group. Expression: $\frac{5}{18} \div 2$.
- Step 4: Convert $2$ to $\frac{2}{1}$, write as multiplication by its reciprocal: $\frac{5}{18} \times \frac{1}{2}$.
- Step 5: Compute the final product: $\frac{5 \times 1}{18 \times 2} = \frac{5}{36}$.
- Step 6: Verify the solution in context and append units: $\frac{5}{36}$ kg of flour per day per group.
REASONING: Sequential sharing requires performing division operations step-by-step, where each division by a whole number scales down the fraction using its respective reciprocal.
ANSWER: $\frac{5}{36}$ kg
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Amit claims that dividing a fraction $\frac{4}{7}$ by a whole number $2$ gives the exact same result as multiplying $\frac{4}{7}$ by the fraction $\frac{2}{1}$. Is Amit correct? Justify your reasoning with calculations.
COMPETENCY: Error identification, reasoning, and justification of mathematical operations
STEPS:
- Step 1: Evaluate Amit's claim by performing his operation: $\frac{4}{7} \times \frac{2}{1} = \frac{8}{7}$.
- Step 2: Perform the correct division operation: $\frac{4}{7} \div 2 = \frac{4}{7} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{4}{14} = \frac{2}{7}$.
- Step 3: Compare both results ($\frac{8}{7}$ vs $\frac{2}{7}$).
- Step 4: Conclude whether Amit is correct and explain why (he confused the whole number with its reciprocal).
ANSWER: Amit is incorrect. Multiplying by $\frac{2}{1}$ doubles the fraction to $\frac{8}{7}$, whereas dividing by $2$ requires multiplying by the reciprocal $\frac{1}{2}$, yielding $\frac{2}{7}$.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A container holds $\frac{11}{4}$ litres of paint. A painter uses $\frac{1}{4}$ of a litre per panel, but before starting, decides to divide the total paint equally among 3 different work sites. What is the amount of paint allocated to each work site, and how many panels can be painted at one site?
COMPETENCY: Multi-step problem solving combining fraction division and application in real-world contexts
STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate the paint allocated to each work site by dividing the total paint ($\frac{11}{4}$ litres) by $3$: $\frac{11}{4} \div 3 = \frac{11}{4} \times \frac{1}{3} = \frac{11}{12}$ litres per site.
- Step 2: Determine how many panels can be painted at one site by dividing the site's paint allocation ($\frac{11}{12}$ litres) by the paint required per panel ($\frac{1}{4}$ litre).
- Step 3: Apply the fraction division rule for two fractions: $\frac{11}{12} \div \frac{1}{4} = \frac{11}{12} \times \frac{4}{1}$.
- Step 4: Simplify and compute: $\frac{11 \times 4}{12 \times 1} = \frac{44}{12} = \frac{11}{3} = 3\frac{2}{3}$ panels.
- Step 5: Interpret the physical meaning: exactly 3 full panels can be painted, with $\frac{2}{3}$ of a panel remaining.
ANSWER: Each site gets $\frac{11}{12}$ litres of paint, which allows them to paint 3 full panels (with a fraction remaining).
DIFFICULTY: hard
