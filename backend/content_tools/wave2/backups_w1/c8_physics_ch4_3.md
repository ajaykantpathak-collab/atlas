## ELI5
Pressure is a tug-of-war between force and area, and either rope can win. Sharpen a knife and the area shrinks — the same hand-pressure becomes a cutting edge. Widen a tractor's tyres and the area grows — the same weight stops sinking into the field. When both change at once, compute, don't guess: double the force with triple the area, and the ratio — not the drama — decides the answer.

## DEFINITION
Pressure is the thrust (force acting perpendicular to a surface) per unit area; it increases when the same force acts on a smaller area or when the force increases while the area stays constant. The SI unit of pressure is the pascal (Pa), where 1 Pa = 1 N/m².
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can explain why a sharp knife cuts better than a blunt one using the pressure formula, and can predict whether pressure will increase or decrease when both force and area change simultaneously (e.g., doubling force while tripling area).
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the qualitative and quantitative relationship between pressure, force, and area (P = F/A) for solid objects pressing on surfaces. It does NOT cover fluid pressure, atmospheric pressure variations with altitude, or pressure in gases/liquids (those are in later nodes c8_physics_ch4_5 onward).
## WALKTHROUGH
Observe a student wearing a school bag with thin straps versus broad straps: the thin straps dig into the shoulders more painfully even though the bag’s weight (force) is the same. Ask students to press a blunt pencil tip and a sharp needle tip against their palm with the same effort — the needle hurts more because the force is concentrated on a tiny area. Connect this to the formula P = F/A learned in c8_physics_ch4_1 and c8_physics_ch4_2: pressure is directly proportional to force and inversely proportional to area. Emphasize that changing either factor changes pressure, and that real-world designs (knife edges, tractor tires, building foundations) exploit this relationship. Conclude by stating the principle: “For a given force, pressure increases as contact area decreases.”

Takeaway: pressure rises when force grows or area shrinks; falls with the opposite moves — and when both change together, only the calculation settles it. Exam format: expect 'why does a sharp knife cut better?' via the ratio, and simultaneous-change problems where the factor sizes decide the direction of change. Verification beat: rewrite the change as a fraction — numerator's growth and denominator's growth compete visibly. Why students miss it: they answer from the louder change — doubling versus tripling is not a matter of volume; the ratio arithmetic is the referee, and it never argues.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
State the formula for pressure and name its SI unit. If a force of 10 N acts on an area of 2 m², what is the pressure in pascals?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Pressure = Force / Area = 10 N / 2 m² = 5 Pa. SI unit: pascal (Pa) = N/m². A wrong answer like “5 N” or “5 N/m” shows the student recalls the formula but does not track units, treating pressure as force or ignoring area’s dimension. **A wrong answer (newtons, or newtons per metre) reveals the student computed the ratio but lost the unit — pressure's unit is the pascal, newtons per square metre, and the squared metre is not decoration; it is the definition.** Train the habit on a market scale in Ranchi: rupees per kilogram is a different animal from rupees — likewise newtons per square metre is a different animal from newtons, and writing the full unit chain converts the slip into a check.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
A camel walks easily on sand while a horse of similar weight sinks. Explain this using the concept of pressure, force, and area. Why does increasing the contact area help?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The camel’s wide feet increase the contact area with sand. Since pressure = force/area, the same weight (force) spread over a larger area produces lower pressure, so the sand does not yield. A wrong answer like “camel is lighter” or “camel has special feet” reveals the student thinks weight changes or misses the inverse area–pressure relationship.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A rectangular brick of weight 30 N rests on a table. Its dimensions are 20 cm × 10 cm × 5 cm. Calculate the pressure exerted when the brick lies on its largest face and on its smallest face. Express answers in Pa.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Convert cm to m: 20 cm = 0.20 m, 10 cm = 0.10 m, 5 cm = 0.05 m.  
Step 2: Largest face area = 0.20 m × 0.10 m = 0.020 m². Pressure = 30 N / 0.020 m² = 1500 Pa.  
Step 3: Smallest face area = 0.10 m × 0.05 m = 0.0050 m². Pressure = 30 N / 0.0050 m² = 6000 Pa.  
Step 4: Unit check: N/m² = Pa.  
A wrong answer like 1500 N/m² (missing Pa) or 600 Pa (using wrong face) shows unit conversion or area identification error.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Two students push identical boxes with forces of 50 N and 80 N respectively. The first box has a base area of 0.25 m², the second 0.40 m². Which box exerts greater pressure on the floor? Show your reasoning.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Box 1: P = 50 N / 0.25 m² = 200 Pa. Box 2: P = 80 N / 0.40 m² = 200 Pa. Both exert equal pressure (200 Pa). A student who picks “Box 2 because force is larger” ignores the area increase; they treat pressure as dependent only on force, not the ratio. **A wrong answer (choosing the bigger force regardless of area) reveals the student treats pressure as a force contest, ignoring the denominator — equal answers are entirely possible when force and area grow in step.** Compare two shop boards in Patna: one heavy sign on a wide hanging rail, one lighter sign on a thin wire — the thinner support can feel more stress. Pressure always asks 'force per what?', and the per-part can cancel, reverse, or confirm the force story.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A classmate says: “If I double the force and double the area, the pressure stays the same.” Is this statement always correct? Justify with the formula.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, it is correct. P = F/A. If F → 2F and A → 2A, then new P = (2F)/(2A) = F/A = original P. The factors cancel. A student who says “pressure doubles because force doubles” or “pressure halves because area doubles” is changing only one variable at a time and not seeing the combined effect.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple experiment using household items (e.g., sand, coins, cardboard) to demonstrate that pressure depends on area. Describe the setup, what you will measure/observe, and how you will conclude the relationship.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Good answer: Place a coin flat on sand, then stand it on edge; measure depth of indentation with a ruler. Same coin (same force = weight), different contact area → deeper indentation for smaller area shows higher pressure. Poor answer: “Press thumb and palm on sand” without controlling force or measuring outcome reveals confusion between force and pressure and lack of controlled variables.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think “more force always means more pressure” and “larger area always means less pressure” as separate rules, but fail to combine them: they cannot compare scenarios where both force and area change together (e.g., a heavier person on skis vs. a lighter person on boots). They also confuse pressure with force, saying “the pressure of the brick is 30 N” instead of “the force is 30 N, pressure is 1500 Pa”.
## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Have the student press a sponge with a fingertip (small area) and then with the whole palm (large area) using the same perceived effort. Ask which sinks deeper. Step 2: Guide them to write P = F/A and label F (same) and A (different). Step 3: Ask: “If F is same and A increases, what happens to P?” Step 4: Repeat with a heavy book on a large tray vs. same book on a small block to vary force while keeping area constant. This concrete sequence forces the student to isolate one variable at a time before combining them.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A school bag weighs 120 N. Its straps are 4 cm wide and 30 cm long. If the bag rests on both straps, what is the pressure on the student’s shoulders? Give answer in Pa.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given: Force F = 120 N (weight of bag). Each strap area = width × length = 4 cm × 30 cm = 120 cm². Two straps → total area A = 2 × 120 cm² = 240 cm².
- Step 2: Convert area to m²: 1 m² = 10,000 cm², so A = 240 cm² ÷ 10,000 = 0.024 m².
- Step 3: Write formula P = F / A. Check units: N / m² = Pa.
- Step 4: Substitute: P = 120 N / 0.024 m² = 5000 Pa.
- Step 5: Verify: 5000 Pa is about 0.05 atm, reasonable for a school bag on thin straps.
REASONING: The weight (force) is distributed over the total contact area of both straps. Converting cm² to m² ensures SI consistency. The result shows why thin straps cause discomfort — 5000 Pa is high for skin.
ANSWER: 5000 Pa
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A tractor tyre has a contact patch of 0.6 m² with the ground. If the tractor’s weight is 24,000 N and it has four identical tyres, what pressure does each tyre exert on the soil?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Total force = weight = 24,000 N. Four tyres share the load equally → force per tyre F = 24,000 N ÷ 4 = 6,000 N.
- Step 2: Contact area per tyre A = 0.6 m² (given).
- Step 3: Formula P = F / A. Units: N / m² = Pa.
- Step 4: Compute: P = 6,000 N / 0.6 m² = 10,000 Pa.
- Step 5: Reasonableness: 10,000 Pa = 10 kPa ≈ 0.1 atm; low pressure prevents soil compaction, which matches the purpose of wide tyres.
REASONING: The total weight is divided among the tyres, so each tyre carries a fraction of the force. The large contact area further reduces pressure. This illustrates the design principle of increasing area to reduce pressure on soft ground.
ANSWER: 10,000 Pa (or 10 kPa)
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A brick of mass 2.5 kg rests on a table. The brick’s dimensions are 25 cm × 12 cm × 6 cm. (Take g = 10 m/s²). Find the maximum and minimum pressure the brick can exert on the table by changing its orientation.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate weight (force): F = m × g = 2.5 kg × 10 m/s² = 25 N.
- Step 2: Convert dimensions to metres: 25 cm = 0.25 m, 12 cm = 0.12 m, 6 cm = 0.06 m.
- Step 3: Determine face areas: Largest face = 0.25 m × 0.12 m = 0.030 m². Smallest face = 0.12 m × 0.06 m = 0.0072 m².
- Step 4: Pressure is maximum on smallest area: P_max = 25 N / 0.0072 m² = 3472.2 Pa ≈ 3470 Pa.
- Step 5: Pressure is minimum on largest area: P_min = 25 N / 0.030 m² = 833.3 Pa ≈ 833 Pa.
- Step 6: Check: P_max > P_min, consistent with inverse area relationship. Units are Pa.
REASONING: This combines mass-to-force conversion (using g from prior motion/force concepts) with area calculation and the pressure formula. The student must identify which face gives max/min area and apply the inverse proportionality correctly.
ANSWER: Maximum pressure = 3470 Pa, Minimum pressure = 833 Pa
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A circus elephant of weight 40,000 N stands on one leg. The foot is a circle of diameter 40 cm. A ballerina of weight 500 N stands on the tip of one pointe shoe, which has a contact area of 3 cm². Who exerts greater pressure on the floor? Explain your reasoning without calculating exact values, then calculate to confirm.
COMPETENCY: Predicting outcomes from changing variables and unit analysis
STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand the physical situation: compare pressure from a large force on a moderately large area vs. a small force on a tiny area.
- Step 2: Identify principle: P = F/A. Pressure depends on the ratio, not just force or area alone.
- Step 3: Estimate: Elephant area ≈ π × (0.2 m)² ≈ 0.126 m² → P ≈ 40,000 / 0.126 ≈ 317,000 Pa. Ballerina area = 3 cm² = 0.0003 m² → P ≈ 500 / 0.0003 ≈ 1,667,000 Pa. Ballerina’s pressure is higher.
- Step 4: Interpret: Even though the elephant is 80 times heavier, its foot area is over 400 times larger than the pointe shoe tip, so the ballerina exerts more pressure. This explains why pointe shoes damage wooden floors.
ANSWER: The ballerina exerts greater pressure (≈1.67 MPa vs ≈0.32 MPa). The huge difference in area outweighs the force difference.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A rectangular block of weight 50 N has dimensions 10 cm × 5 cm × 2 cm. It is placed on a layer of soft clay. The clay will deform permanently if pressure exceeds 20,000 Pa. Determine all possible orientations in which the block can rest without damaging the clay. Show your work.
COMPETENCY: Interpreting physical situations using formulas and applying constraints
STEPS:
- Step 1: Convert dimensions to metres: 0.10 m, 0.05 m, 0.02 m.
- Step 2: List three possible face areas: A1 = 0.10 × 0.05 = 0.0050 m²; A2 = 0.10 × 0.02 = 0.0020 m²; A3 = 0.05 × 0.02 = 0.0010 m².
- Step 3: Compute pressure for each: P1 = 50 / 0.0050 = 10,000 Pa; P2 = 50 / 0.0020 = 25,000 Pa; P3 = 50 / 0.0010 = 50,000 Pa.
- Step 4: Compare with limit 20,000 Pa: Only orientation 1 (largest face, 10 cm × 5 cm) gives pressure below the limit.
- Step 5: Conclude: The block must rest on its 10 cm × 5 cm face to avoid damaging the clay.
ANSWER: Only the orientation with the 10 cm × 5 cm face down (pressure = 10,000 Pa) is safe. The other two orientations exceed 20,000 Pa.
DIFFICULTY: hard
