## ELI5
Three-quarters of a litre of juice, glasses of one-eighth litre each - how many glasses? Counting the small portions hiding inside the big one is fraction division: keep the first fraction, flip the divisor, multiply. The count can exceed the litres you own, because each glass is small. The classic slips are flipping the wrong fraction or skipping the flip entirely and multiplying straight across. Ask 'how many of these fit into that?' and the reciprocal method follows like a recipe.

## DEFINITION
Dividing one fraction by another is the mathematical operation of finding how many times a second fraction (the divisor) fits into a first fraction (the dividend). This is computed by multiplying the dividend fraction by the reciprocal (inverse) of the divisor fraction.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
Can convert any division of two proper, improper, or mixed fractions into a multiplication problem using the reciprocal of the divisor, simplify the resulting terms, and express the final answer in lowest terms.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers the division of positive proper fractions, improper fractions, and mixed numbers. Does not cover complex fractions with multiple nested division bars or division by zero.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you have $\frac{3}{4}$ of a litre of juice, and you want to pour it into smaller glasses that each hold $\frac{1}{8}$ of a litre. How many glasses can you fill? To solve this, you need to find out how many groups of $\frac{1}{8}$ fit into $\frac{3}{4}$, which means dividing one fraction by another: $\frac{3}{4} \div \frac{1}{8}$. Just like in whole number division where dividing by a number is related to multiplying by its inverse, dividing by a fraction is the same as multiplying by its reciprocal. The key insight is to change the division sign to multiplication and flip the second fraction upside down.

**Takeaway:** fraction-by-fraction division counts how many divisors fit: keep, flip the divisor, multiply. **In the exam:** convert mixed numbers, flip only the second fraction, cross-cancel, and simplify the quotient. **Check yourself:** compute the juice-to-glasses count and check it against a rough estimate. **Why students miss it:** they invert the dividend instead of the divisor, or skip the flip and multiply across. Estimate the count before computing, and the exact answer has a common-sense cage to land in. Count the glasses a real litre would fill, and the flipped fraction stops looking arbitrary. Cross-cancel boldly, and the numbers stay small enough to finish in the margin.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the reciprocal of the fraction $\frac{5}{7}$?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The correct answer is $\frac{7}{5}$. A student who answers $-\frac{5}{7}$ is confusing the reciprocal with the additive inverse (negative). A student who answers $\frac{5}{7}$ has forgotten to flip the numerator and denominator.

**A wrong answer (negating instead of flipping) reveals the student pressed the wrong undo button** - the additive inverse changes the sign; the reciprocal flips the fraction, and the two are different operations.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why we multiply by the reciprocal of the divisor when dividing two fractions instead of dividing the numerators and denominators directly.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The correct explanation is that scaling by the reciprocal is equivalent to finding a common denominator and comparing how many parts fit, or using algebraic properties of fractions where dividing by $b/c$ is multiplying by the multiplicative inverse $c/b$. A student who says "it is just a magic rule with no reason" does not understand fraction division conceptually and will likely misapply the rule when faced with complex multi-step problems.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Calculate the value of $\frac{3}{5} \div \frac{9}{10}$ and write your answer in its simplest form.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The correct answer is $\frac{2}{3}$. Working steps: First, change the division to multiplication by the reciprocal of the second fraction: $\frac{3}{5} \times \frac{10}{9}$. Next, multiply numerators and denominators: $\frac{3 \times 10}{5 \times 9} = \frac{30}{45}$. Finally, divide the numerator and denominator by their greatest common factor, which is 15: $\frac{30 \div 15}{45 \div 15} = \frac{2}{3}$. A student who gets $\frac{27}{50}$ has incorrectly multiplied straight across without flipping the second fraction.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the results of $\frac{4}{7} \div \frac{2}{3}$ and $\frac{2}{3} \div \frac{4}{7}$. Which one is larger and why does the commutative property not hold for fraction division?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The first expression evaluates to $\frac{4}{7} \times \frac{3}{2} = \frac{12}{14} = \frac{6}{7}$. The second expression evaluates to $\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{7}{4} = \frac{14}{12} = \frac{7}{6}$. Since $\frac{7}{6}$ is greater than 1 and $\frac{6}{7}$ is less than 1, the second expression is larger. Division is not commutative because changing the order of the dividend and divisor alters the reciprocal being applied, completely reversing the ratio.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student solves $\frac{5}{8} \div \frac{3}{4}$ by writing: $\frac{5}{8} \times \frac{3}{4} = \frac{15}{32}$. Identify the error in the student's work and provide the correct solution.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The student made two errors: they kept the division sign as multiplication without changing the operation rule, and more importantly, they failed to write the reciprocal of the second fraction ($\frac{3}{4}$ remained $\frac{3}{4}$). The correct solution is: $\frac{5}{8} \div \frac{3}{4} = \frac{5}{8} \times \frac{4}{3} = \frac{20}{24} = \frac{5}{6}$.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a division problem involving two fractions where the final quotient is a whole number greater than 1. Show the working steps to verify your answer.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
An acceptable answer would be $\frac{7}{8} \div \frac{1}{4}$. Working steps: 1. Write the expression $\frac{7}{8} \div \frac{1}{4}$. 2. Change division to multiplication and invert the divisor: $\frac{7}{8} \times \frac{4}{1}$. 3. Multiply numerators and denominators: $\frac{28}{8}$. 4. Simplify by dividing by 4: $\frac{7}{2} = 3\frac{1}{2}$ (or another valid pair like $\frac{9}{10} \div \frac{3}{10} = 3$). A poor answer uses whole numbers disguised as fractions without demonstrating the core fraction operation.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often invert the *first* fraction (dividend) instead of the *second* fraction (divisor), or they mistakenly multiply all numerators and denominators straight across without changing the division sign into multiplication. For example, when solving $\frac{2}{3} \div \frac{4}{5}$, they might compute $\frac{3}{2} \times \frac{4}{5} = \frac{12}{10}$, which results in an entirely incorrect reciprocal application.
## ON_FAILURE
Remind the student of the role of the divisor: "The second fraction is the divisor—it is the one doing the dividing, so it is the one that gets flipped." Write out the rule clearly: **Keep** the first fraction, **Change** $\div$ to $\times$, **Flip** the second fraction (KCF method). Have them physically cover the second fraction with their finger, write its reciprocal next to it, and only then rewrite the expression as a multiplication problem.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A tailor has a ribbon of length $\frac{7}{8}$ metres. If small pieces of length $\frac{1}{4}$ metre are cut from it, how many full pieces can be made, and what fraction of a metre of ribbon will be left over?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the total length of ribbon ($\frac{7}{8}$ m) and the length of each cut piece ($\frac{1}{4}$ m). Set up the division expression: $\frac{7}{8} \div \frac{1}{4}$.
- step 2: Apply the reciprocal rule by changing the division sign to multiplication and writing the reciprocal of the divisor ($\frac{1}{4}$ becomes $\frac{4}{1}$).
- step 3: Rewrite the expression as fraction multiplication: $\frac{7}{8} \times \frac{4}{1}$.
- step 4: Multiply numerators and denominators: $\frac{7 \times 4}{8 \times 1} = \frac{28}{8}$.
- step 5: Simplify the improper fraction $\frac{28}{8}$ by dividing numerator and denominator by their HCF (4), yielding $\frac{7}{2} = 3\frac{1}{2}$ pieces. Thus, 3 full pieces can be made, and the remaining ribbon fraction is calculated from the leftover part.
REASONING: Dividing the total length by the length of one piece tells us how many pieces fit into the total. Converting division to multiplication by the reciprocal simplifies the calculation into standard fraction multiplication.
ANSWER: 3 full pieces (with $\frac{1}{8}$ m of ribbon remaining)
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Solve the following division of mixed numbers and fractions: $2\frac{1}{3} \div \frac{5}{6}$.
STEPS:
- step 1: Convert the mixed number $2\frac{1}{3}$ into an improper fraction: $\frac{(2 \times 3) + 1}{3} = \frac{7}{3}$.
- step 2: Rewrite the complete division sentence with the new improper fraction: $\frac{7}{3} \div \frac{5}{6}$.
- step 3: Apply the reciprocal method: change division to multiplication and invert the divisor $\frac{5}{6}$ to $\frac{6}{5}$, giving $\frac{7}{3} \times \frac{6}{5}$.
- step 4: Multiply across: $\frac{7 \times 6}{3 \times 5} = \frac{42}{15}$. Alternatively, cross-cancel common factors before multiplying: $\frac{7}{1} \times \frac{2}{5} = \frac{14}{5}$.
- step 5: Convert the resulting improper fraction back into a mixed number: $\frac{14}{5} = 2\frac{4}{5}$.
REASONING: Mixed numbers must always be converted to improper fractions before applying the reciprocal rule. Cross-canceling common factors (like 3 and 6) streamlines the multiplication step.
ANSWER: $2\frac{4}{5}$
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A container holds $\frac{15}{16}$ litres of paint. A smaller brush bucket can hold $\frac{3}{8}$ of a litre. If the container is currently $\frac{4}{5}$ full of its maximum capacity, and all of its current paint is poured into smaller buckets until empty, how many full small buckets are filled, and what fraction of a bucket is the last partially filled one?
STEPS:
- step 1: Determine the actual paint volume currently in the container by finding $\frac{4}{5}$ of $\frac{15}{16}$: $\frac{4}{5} \times \frac{15}{16} = \frac{60}{80} = \frac{3}{4}$ litres.
- step 2: Set up the division problem to find how many small buckets of size $\frac{3}{8}$ litre can be filled with $\frac{3}{4}$ litres of paint: $\frac{3}{4} \div \frac{3}{8}$.
- step 3: Apply the reciprocal rule: change division to multiplication and invert the divisor $\frac{3}{8}$ to $\frac{8}{3}$, giving $\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{8}{3}$.
- step 4: Simplify by canceling common factors: $\frac{3 \text{ (numerator)} \times 8 \text{ (numerator)}}{4 \text{ (denominator)} \times 3 \text{ (denominator)}} = \frac{8}{4} = 2$.
- step 5: Interpret the result: exactly 2 full small buckets are filled with no remainder.
REASONING: This problem combines fraction multiplication (to find fractional parts of a quantity) with fraction division (to distribute that quantity into equal-sized containers). Careful step-by-step evaluation prevents order-of-operations errors.
ANSWER: Exactly 2 full buckets
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Priya claims that dividing any proper fraction by another proper fraction always results in a number greater than 1. Is Priya correct? Support your answer with two different mathematical examples—one that supports her claim and one that refutes it.
COMPETENCY: Evaluating mathematical claims and providing counterexamples
STEPS:
- step 1: Analyze Priya's claim: "Proper fraction $\div$ Proper fraction $> 1$ always."
- step 2: Test with an example supporting her claim, such as $\frac{1}{2} \div \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{4}{1} = 2$, which is greater than 1.
- step 3: Test with a counterexample refuting her claim, such as $\frac{1}{4} \div \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4} \times \frac{2}{1} = \frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$, which is less than 1.
- step 4: Formulate the final conclusion: Priya is incorrect because the quotient depends on the relative sizes of the dividend and divisor, not just the fact that they are proper fractions.
ANSWER: Priya is incorrect. While $\frac{1}{2} \div \frac{1}{4} = 2$ (greater than 1), choosing $\frac{1}{4} \div \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2}$ gives a result less than 1. Thus, the statement does not always hold true.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A water tank contains $5\frac{1}{4}$ litres of water. A leak causes water to drain out at a rate of $\frac{3}{8}$ of a litre every hour. How many hours will it take for the tank to drain completely? If the tank started draining at 9:00 AM, at what time of day will it be empty?
COMPETENCY: Translating multi-step real-world scenarios into fraction division and time calculation
STEPS:
- step 1: Convert the mixed number $5\frac{1}{4}$ into an improper fraction: $\frac{21}{4}$ litres.
- step 2: Set up the division problem to find the total hours: $\frac{21}{4} \div \frac{3}{8}$.
- step 3: Apply the reciprocal method: $\frac{21}{4} \times \frac{8}{3} = \frac{21 \times 8}{4 \times 3} = \frac{168}{12} = 14$ hours.
- step 4: Add the elapsed time (14 hours) to the start time (9:00 AM). 9:00 AM + 12 hours = 9:00 PM; 9:00 PM + 2 more hours = 11:00 PM.
ANSWER: It will take 14 hours for the tank to drain completely, and the tank will be empty at 11:00 PM.
DIFFICULTY: hard
