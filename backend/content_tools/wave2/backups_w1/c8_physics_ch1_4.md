## ELI5
A hypothesis is a prediction wearing its reasons on its sleeve: if I change this, then that will happen, because this mechanism links them. All three parts matter — the change, the measurable outcome, and the cause connecting them. A guess without a because is merely a guess; a because without a measurable outcome cannot be tested. Written this way, a hypothesis is an experiment's advance booking: falsifiable, specific, and ready to be proven wrong.

## DEFINITION
A hypothesis is a tentative, testable explanation or prediction that proposes a relationship between variables based on prior observations or scientific knowledge, which can be supported or refuted through controlled experimentation.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
Student can formulate a testable hypothesis in "If... then... because..." format that identifies independent and dependent variables with measurable units, and explains the scientific reasoning linking them using concepts from prior physics topics.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers formulating hypotheses for simple controlled experiments involving one independent variable (Class 8 scope: length, mass, temperature, voltage, concentration). Does NOT cover statistical hypothesis testing, null/alternative hypothesis formalism, or multi-variable experimental designs.
## WALKTHROUGH
Watch a ceiling fan: when you change the regulator knob from 1 to 5, the fan speeds up. Ask: "What relationship exists between regulator position and fan speed?" This connects to Class 7's 'Speed: Definition and Formula' (c7_physics_ch04_006) and Class 6's 'Building a Simple Electrical Circuit' (c6_physics_ch03_012). Begin by identifying what you will change (regulator setting) and what you will measure (fan rotations per minute). Then propose a cause-effect prediction using "If I increase the regulator setting, then the fan speed will increase because higher voltage across the motor increases current." Finally, check if both variables can be measured with available tools (stopwatch, multimeter).

Takeaway: write hypotheses in the full if-then-because shape — the change you will make, the measurable outcome you expect, and the scientific reason bridging them. Exam format: expect 'formulate a hypothesis for this scenario' and 'is this hypothesis testable?' — units on the measured variable and a stated mechanism are the marking points. Verification beat: check the then-part is measurable with available tools and the because-part names a cause, not a restatement. Why students miss it: they write predictions without reasons — the because is what makes it scientific, and what the experiment will actually judge. Every good hypothesis carries its own refutation plan, and testability is the entry fee.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
A student observes that a pendulum takes 2 seconds per swing when the string is 1 m long. She writes: "If I make the string longer, the pendulum will swing differently." Why is this NOT a good scientific hypothesis for Class 8?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The statement is vague: "differently" is not measurable and does not specify the direction of change (increase/decrease in time period). A good hypothesis must predict a specific measurable relationship (e.g., "time period will increase") with units (seconds) and include reasoning ("because longer pendulums have greater arc length"). A wrong answer reveals the student cannot distinguish between a testable prediction and a vague guess.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Rahul hypothesizes: "If I add more salt to water, the boiling point will increase because salt makes water heavier." Identify the error in his reasoning and rewrite the hypothesis correctly using the concept of boiling point elevation from Class 7 heat topics.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The error: "heavier" confuses mass with colligative properties; boiling point elevation depends on number of solute particles, not mass alone. Correct hypothesis: "If I increase the concentration of salt (g/100 mL water), then the boiling point (°C) will increase because dissolved particles lower vapour pressure, requiring more heat to boil." A wrong answer shows the student recalls the trend but lacks conceptual understanding of the mechanism.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Priya wants to test how the thickness of a copper wire affects its electrical resistance. She has wires of 0.5 mm, 1.0 mm, and 1.5 mm diameter (all 1 m long, same temperature). Write a testable hypothesis for her experiment. Identify the independent variable (with units), dependent variable (with units), and one controlled variable.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Hypothesis: "If the diameter of copper wire (mm) increases, then its electrical resistance (Ω) will decrease because thicker wires provide more cross-sectional area for electron flow." Independent: diameter (mm); Dependent: resistance (Ω); Controlled: length (1 m), material (copper), temperature (room temp). A wrong answer reveals confusion between independent/dependent variables or inability to assign correct units.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Two students hypothesize about a toy car on a ramp:
Student A: "If I raise the ramp height, the car will go faster at the bottom."
Student B: "If I raise the ramp height, the car's speed at the bottom will increase because gravitational potential energy converts to kinetic energy."
Compare the two hypotheses. Which is better for a Class 8 physics investigation and why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Student B's hypothesis is better because it includes the scientific reasoning (energy conversion) linking the variables, uses precise language ("speed at the bottom" not just "faster"), and connects to prior concepts (potential/kinetic energy from Class 7). Student A's hypothesis is testable but lacks explanatory depth. A wrong answer indicates the student cannot evaluate the quality of scientific reasoning in a hypothesis.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A textbook states: "Hypothesis: Plants grow better with blue light than red light." A Class 8 student designs an experiment with two identical plants, one under blue LED, one under red LED, measuring height weekly. Evaluate whether this hypothesis is testable in a school lab. List two practical limitations.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The hypothesis is testable in principle but has limitations: (1) "Better" is ambiguous — must define measurable dependent variable (e.g., height in cm, leaf count, biomass in g). (2) Controlling all other variables (light intensity in lux, duration, soil, water, temperature) is difficult in a school lab; small differences could confound results. A wrong answer shows the student cannot critique experimental feasibility or identify confounding variables.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple hypothesis for an investigation using only household items (no lab equipment) that tests a physics concept from Class 6 or 7 (electricity, heat, light, motion). Write it in "If... then... because..." format with measurable variables and units.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Example: "If I increase the number of cells in a series circuit (1, 2, 3 cells), then the brightness of an LED (measured by a homemade light sensor using an LDR and multimeter in kΩ) will increase because more cells provide higher voltage, pushing more current through the LED." Variables: independent = number of cells (count), dependent = LDR resistance (kΩ) as proxy for brightness, controlled = LED type, wire length, ambient light. A poor answer uses unmeasurable variables, lacks reasoning, or chooses a non-physics concept.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students think a hypothesis is just an "educated guess" without requiring a mechanistic "because" clause linked to prior physics concepts. They write "If I heat water, it will boil faster" without explaining that increased temperature raises kinetic energy of molecules (Class 7 Heat). They also confuse hypothesis with procedure: "I will heat water and time it" is a method, not a prediction.
## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Have the student pick a familiar phenomenon (e.g., bicycle brakes stopping the wheel). Step 2: Ask "What variable will you change?" (brake lever force) and "What will you measure?" (stopping distance in cm). Step 3: Demand a "because" using a Class 7 concept: "because greater friction force does more work to remove kinetic energy." Step 4: Practice rewriting three vague guesses into testable hypotheses with units.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A student notices her bicycle stops in 5 m when she applies brakes gently at 10 km/h, but in 2 m when she brakes hard. She wants to investigate the relationship between braking force and stopping distance. Formulate a testable hypothesis with measurable variables and units.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the observation: stopping distance changes with how hard brakes are applied.
- Step 2: Choose independent variable (what you change): braking force applied at the lever. Since force is hard to measure directly at school, use a proxy: number of fingers pulling the lever (1, 2, 3, 4 fingers) or spring balance reading in newtons (N) if available.
- Step 3: Choose dependent variable (what you measure): stopping distance in metres (m) or centimetres (cm), measured with a tape measure.
- Step 4: Choose controlled variables: same bicycle, same rider mass, same initial speed (use a gentle slope to start), same road surface, same tyre pressure.
- Step 5: Write hypothesis in "If... then... because..." format: "If the braking force (measured by spring balance in N) increases, then the stopping distance (m) will decrease because greater friction force does more negative work per metre, removing kinetic energy faster (Work = Force × Distance)."
REASONING: The hypothesis links the independent variable (force) to dependent variable (distance) using the work-energy principle from Class 7 (work done by friction = change in kinetic energy). Both variables have SI units (N, m) and the prediction is directional (decrease). The "because" clause uses prior physics knowledge, not just a guess.
ANSWER: Hypothesis: "If braking force (N) increases, then stopping distance (m) decreases because increased friction force does more work per unit distance to dissipate kinetic energy." Independent: force (N), Dependent: distance (m), Controlled: mass, initial speed, surface, tyres.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: During a summer afternoon, a student places two identical steel tumblers in the sun: one wrapped in aluminium foil, one bare. After 30 minutes, the bare tumbler's water is hotter. Design a hypothesis to test how surface reflectivity affects heating rate.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Observe: shiny surface reflects more sunlight, so water heats slower.
- Step 2: Independent variable: surface type (categorical: bare steel vs aluminium foil) OR reflectivity percentage (if measured with a light sensor). For Class 8, use categorical with two levels.
- Step 3: Dependent variable: temperature rise of water (°C) measured with a laboratory thermometer after fixed time (30 min = 1800 s).
- Step 4: Controlled variables: same tumbler material, same water volume (e.g., 200 mL), same initial water temperature, same sun exposure (side by side), same ambient temperature, same thermometer.
- Step 5: Formulate hypothesis: "If the outer surface is changed from bare steel to aluminium foil (higher reflectivity), then the temperature rise of water (°C) after 1800 s will be lower because the foil reflects more incident solar radiation, reducing absorption and heat transfer by conduction to the water."
REASONING: Uses Class 7 Radiation (c7_physics_ch03_006) and Conduction (c7_physics_ch03_002) concepts. The hypothesis predicts a lower temperature rise for the reflective surface, with clear units (°C, s) and a physical mechanism (reflection → less absorption → less conduction).
ANSWER: Hypothesis: "If surface reflectivity is increased (bare steel → aluminium foil), then water temperature rise (°C) after 1800 s will decrease because higher reflectivity reduces absorbed radiation, lowering conductive heat transfer to water." Independent: surface type (categorical), Dependent: ΔT (°C), Controlled: volume, initial temp, exposure time, ambient conditions.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Combine electricity and motion: A student builds a simple electric car with a DC motor, wheels, and a battery pack. She wants to test how the number of 1.5 V cells in series affects the car's speed over a 2 m track. Formulate a hypothesis that connects voltage, electrical power, and mechanical speed.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Recall Class 6 Series Circuits (c6_physics_ch03_017): cells in series add voltages (1.5 V, 3.0 V, 4.5 V, 6.0 V).
- Step 2: Independent variable: number of cells (1, 2, 3, 4) → total voltage (V) = 1.5 × number of cells.
- Step 3: Dependent variable: average speed (m/s) = track length (2 m) / time (s) measured with stopwatch.
- Step 4: Controlled variables: same motor, same car mass, same track surface, same wheel alignment, fresh cells each trial.
- Step 5: Scientific reasoning: Higher voltage → higher current (Ohm's Law, Class 7) → higher electrical power (P = VI) → higher mechanical power at wheels → greater acceleration → higher average speed over fixed distance.
- Step 6: Write hypothesis: "If the supply voltage (V) is increased by adding cells in series, then the average speed of the car over 2 m (m/s) will increase because higher voltage drives more current through the motor, increasing electrical power (P = VI) and thus mechanical power output, resulting in greater acceleration."
REASONING: Integrates Class 6 circuits (series voltage addition), Class 7 electricity (current, power), and Class 7 motion (speed = distance/time). The hypothesis is quantitative (V, m/s), testable with school equipment (cells, stopwatch, tape), and the "because" chain links electrical to mechanical energy conversion.
ANSWER: Hypothesis: "If supply voltage (V) increases (1.5 V steps), then average speed over 2 m (m/s) increases because higher voltage increases motor current and electrical power (P = VI), producing more mechanical power for acceleration." Independent: voltage (V), Dependent: speed (m/s), Controlled: motor, mass, track, distance.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A Class 8 student reads: "Detergent removes oil stains better in hot water than in cold water." She writes this hypothesis: "If water temperature is higher, then detergent will clean better because heat makes molecules move faster." Critique this hypothesis. Identify two specific improvements needed to make it testable in a school lab, and rewrite it correctly.
COMPETENCY: Interpreting physical situations using formulas and scientific reasoning
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the vague terms: "clean better" is not measurable; "heat makes molecules move faster" is true but not directly linked to detergent action.
- Step 2: Choose a measurable dependent variable: e.g., time (s) to remove a standard oil stain, or mass (g) of oil removed after fixed agitation time.
- Step 3: Define independent variable with units: water temperature (°C) using a thermometer, range 20–60 °C in 10 °C steps.
- Step 4: Link reasoning to detergent chemistry: higher temperature increases kinetic energy of molecules, increasing collision frequency between detergent and oil, and reduces oil viscosity, enhancing emulsification.
- Step 5: Rewrite: "If water temperature (°C) increases from 20 to 60 °C, then the time (s) required to remove a standard 0.1 g oil stain from a cotton cloth (with fixed detergent concentration and agitation) will decrease because higher temperature increases molecular kinetic energy and reduces oil viscosity, accelerating emulsification."
ANSWER: Improvements: (1) "Clean better" → measurable: time to remove stain (s) or mass of oil removed (g). (2) Reasoning must connect temperature to detergent-oil interaction, not just generic molecular motion. Rewritten hypothesis includes specific variables, units, controlled conditions, and mechanistic reasoning.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Two groups investigate "How does the angle of a ramp affect the speed of a ball at the bottom?"
Group A hypothesis: "If the ramp angle increases, the ball will roll faster."
Group B hypothesis: "If the ramp angle (degrees) increases from 10° to 40°, then the final speed (m/s) of a 50 g steel ball rolling down a 1.5 m long smooth wooden ramp will increase because a steeper angle increases the component of gravity parallel to the ramp (g sinθ), producing greater acceleration."
Evaluate which hypothesis demonstrates deeper physics understanding. Then, predict what would happen to the final speed if the ball were replaced with a 100 g steel ball of the same size, and justify using energy concepts.
COMPETENCY: Predicting outcomes from changing variables and applying energy principles
STEPS:
- Step 1: Compare hypotheses: Group B specifies variables with units (angle in °, speed in m/s), controlled conditions (ball mass, ramp length, material), and uses physics reasoning (component of gravity, acceleration).
- Step 2: Group A lacks units, controls, and mechanistic reasoning.
- Step 3: For the prediction: final speed depends on height loss (mgh = ½mv²), not mass. Since height = length × sinθ, and mass cancels out, doubling mass to 100 g does not change final speed (neglecting friction/air resistance).
- Step 4: Justify using conservation of energy: gravitational potential energy converts to translational kinetic energy; mass appears on both sides and cancels.
- Step 5: State prediction clearly: final speed remains the same for 100 g ball under ideal conditions.
ANSWER: Group B's hypothesis is superior — it quantifies variables, states controls, and explains the mechanism (g sinθ). Prediction: Final speed will be unchanged when mass is doubled to 100 g because v = √(2gh) is independent of mass (energy conservation: mgh = ½mv² → v = √(2gh)). Real-world note: slightly lower speed if friction/air resistance becomes relatively more significant, but at Class 8 level, ideal prediction is "no change".
DIFFICULTY: hard
