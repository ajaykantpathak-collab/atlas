## ELI5
Some forces insist on a handshake. Muscular force lifts a bucket only while your hand grips it; friction pushes back only while surfaces touch; a spring pushes only while compressed. These are contact forces — born at the meeting point of two objects, dying the instant they part. Watch a bicycle: the hand steers by touch, the brake pads grip the rim by touch. No contact, no force — a rule with no exceptions in this family.

## DEFINITION
Contact forces are forces that act between two objects only when they are in direct physical contact with each other. The SI unit for all forces, including contact forces, is the newton (N). Examples include muscular force (applied by muscles), friction (opposing relative motion between surfaces), and spring force (exerted by a compressed or stretched spring).

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY

It does NOT cover quantitative laws of friction (coefficients), Hooke's law for springs, or non-contact forces (gravity, magnetic, electrostatic) which are addressed in subsequent nodes.
## WALKTHROUGH
Observe a bicycle coming to a stop when you squeeze the brake lever: your fingers apply muscular force on the lever, the brake pads press against the wheel rim (spring force from the brake cable tension), and friction between the pads and rim slows the wheel. Notice that every force here requires touching – your hand touches the lever, the pads touch the rim. Connect this to the earlier idea that a force is a push or pull (c8_physics_ch3_1) and that multiple forces combine as a net force (c8_physics_ch3_6). Conclude that whenever two objects touch and exert a push or pull on each other, a contact force is at work.

Takeaway: contact forces — muscular, friction, spring — exist only while two objects touch, acting at the interface; remove the contact and the force vanishes with it. Exam format: expect classification tasks (contact versus non-contact) with the reason attached, and identification of the touching pair in each example. Verification beat: for every force you name, point to the two surfaces in contact; if none exist, it belongs to the other family. Why students miss it: they call the pulling of a string or the push of water 'mysterious' — both are contact forces, just distributed over an area instead of concentrated at a point. A rope's pull is contact distributed along its length.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Name the three types of contact forces discussed in this topic and give one everyday example of each.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The three contact forces are muscular force (e.g., pushing a school bag), friction (e.g., a book sliding to a stop on a table), and spring force (e.g., a stretched rubber band pulling back). A wrong answer that lists gravity or magnetism reveals the student is confusing contact forces with non-contact forces; listing only two types shows incomplete recall of the classification.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why a book resting on a table experiences a contact force from the table, but the Earth's pull on the book is NOT a contact force.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The table pushes up on the book only because their surfaces touch – remove the table and the push disappears, proving contact is necessary. Earth pulls the book even when no object touches it (e.g., the book in mid-air), so gravity acts without contact. A student who says both are contact forces thinks any push/pull requires touch, missing that gravity works at a distance.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A student pushes a 2 kg box horizontally with a muscular force of 10 N. The friction force opposing the motion is 3 N. A spring attached to the box pulls backward with 2 N. Calculate the net horizontal force on the box. (Use g = 10 m/s² if needed, but only horizontal forces matter here.)
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Identify all horizontal contact forces with direction: muscular force = +10 N (forward), friction = -3 N (backward), spring force = -2 N (backward).  
Step 2: Net force formula: F_net = ΣF (vector sum).  
Step 3: Substitute: F_net = 10 N + (-3 N) + (-2 N) = 10 N - 3 N - 2 N = 5 N.  
Step 4: Unit check: all forces in newtons, result in newtons.  
Step 5: Reasonable? Yes, a small net force on a 2 kg box gives modest acceleration.  
Answer: 5 N forward.  
A wrong answer of 15 N (adding magnitudes) shows the student ignores direction; 10 N (ignoring friction/spring) shows they don't yet combine multiple contact forces.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the contact forces acting on a cricket ball when: (a) a bowler holds it before release, (b) the ball is in mid-air after release, (c) the ball hits the bat. List which contact forces are present in each case.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
(a) Muscular force (fingers gripping), spring force (if the ball deforms slightly), friction (between fingers and ball).  
(b) No contact forces (only gravity and air resistance, which is a contact force with air molecules – but at this level we treat air resistance as contact friction).  
(c) Muscular force (bat pushed by batter), spring force (ball and bat deform), friction (ball slides on bat surface).  
A student who lists muscular force in (b) thinks the bowler's force continues after release – a classic "impetus" misconception.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A classmate says: "Friction is not a contact force because you can't see the two surfaces touching when a book slides on a table – there are tiny gaps." Evaluate this statement and correct the error.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is incorrect. Friction arises from electromagnetic interactions between atoms of the two surfaces at the microscopic contact points. Even if surfaces look smooth, they touch at many tiny asperities. The gaps are between non-contacting regions, but contact exists where atoms are close enough for forces to act. A student who agrees thinks "contact" means perfectly flat macroscopic touching, not understanding the microscopic nature of surface contact.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple classroom activity using only a spring balance, a wooden block, a rubber band, and a rough cloth to demonstrate all three contact forces (muscular, friction, spring) in one sequence. Describe the steps and what students should observe.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Steps: (1) Hang the block from the spring balance – students pull down (muscular force) and see the spring stretch (spring force). (2) Place the block on the rough cloth and pull horizontally with the spring balance – the reading shows friction opposing motion. (3) Stretch the rubber band between two fingers – muscular force stretches it, spring force pulls back.  
Observations: In each step, force only appears when objects touch. A poor answer that uses a magnet or drops the block shows the student cannot distinguish contact from non-contact forces.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that muscular force is a non-contact force because the muscles are inside the body and "push from within," so they think the force acts at a distance. For example, when pushing a wall, they say "my force reaches the wall without my hand touching it" – confusing the internal biological process with the physical contact of hand on wall.
## ON_FAILURE
Have the student press their palm firmly against a wall and feel the compression of their skin and the wall's resistance. Then ask them to push the wall *without* touching it – they cannot. Next, use a spring balance: hook it to a block, pull until the block moves, and point out the hook touching the block. Finally, rub hands together to feel friction heat, emphasizing both surfaces must touch. This tactile sequence directly counters the "internal force acts remotely" idea.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A girl pulls a 3 kg sled horizontally using a rope. She exerts a muscular force of 25 N. The friction between sled and snow is 8 N. The rope acts like a spring and pulls back on her hands with a spring force of 5 N. What is the net horizontal force on the sled?
STEPS:
- Step 1: List all horizontal contact forces on the sled with signs: muscular force (forward) = +25 N; friction (backward) = -8 N; spring force from rope on sled (backward) = -5 N.
- Step 2: Recall net force is the vector sum: F_net = F_muscular + F_friction + F_spring.
- Step 3: Substitute values with units: F_net = (+25 N) + (-8 N) + (-5 N).
- Step 4: Compute: 25 - 8 - 5 = 12 N. Unit remains newton (N).
- Step 5: Check reasonableness: Net force forward, so sled accelerates forward; magnitude 12 N on 3 kg gives 4 m/s², plausible for a sled.
REASONING: Each force is a contact force because the girl's hands touch the rope (muscular), sled touches snow (friction), and rope touches sled (spring). Directions matter: forces opposing motion are negative. The net force determines acceleration via Newton's second law (later topic).
ANSWER: 12 N forward
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A student hangs a 500 g mass from a spring balance. The balance reads 5 N. Identify all contact forces acting on the mass and on the spring balance hook. State the agent and receiver for each.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Draw the system: mass (object) and hook (part of balance). They touch at the hook-mass interface.
- Step 2: On the mass: Earth pulls down (gravity, non-contact). The hook pulls up on the mass – this is a spring force (contact, because hook touches mass). Agent: hook; Receiver: mass.
- Step 3: On the hook: The mass pulls down on the hook (spring force, contact). Agent: mass; Receiver: hook. The student's hand pulls up on the balance (muscular force, contact). Agent: hand; Receiver: balance casing (transmitted to hook).
- Step 4: Note the spring balance reads 5 N because the spring inside stretches until its upward spring force on the hook balances the downward pull of the mass.
- Step 5: Verify: All listed forces involve touching. Gravity is the only non-contact force here.
REASONING: Contact forces always come in action-reaction pairs at the interface. The spring force appears twice (hook on mass, mass on hook) – Newton's third law pair. Muscular force requires the student's hand to touch the balance.
ANSWER: On mass: upward spring force (hook on mass). On hook: downward spring force (mass on hook), upward muscular force (hand on balance). All are contact forces except gravity on mass.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A 200 g toy car is pushed by a child's finger (muscular force 0.5 N) across a rough floor. Friction is 0.2 N. A compressed spring on the car pushes forward with 0.3 N when released. The car moves 1.5 m before stopping. Calculate the net force while all three contact forces act, and explain why the car eventually stops.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify horizontal contact forces during the push+spring phase: muscular = +0.5 N, spring = +0.3 N, friction = -0.2 N.
- Step 2: Net force F_net = 0.5 + 0.3 - 0.2 = 0.6 N forward.
- Step 3: Unit check: all in newtons, result in newtons.
- Step 4: After the child stops pushing and spring expands fully, only friction acts: F_net = -0.2 N (deceleration).
- Step 5: Use work-energy idea (qualitative): friction does negative work over 1.5 m, removing kinetic energy until car stops.
- Step 6: Connect to prior motion concepts: net force causes acceleration (Class 7 speed change), friction causes deceleration to zero speed.
REASONING: The problem combines three contact forces acting simultaneously, then asks to reason about motion after forces change. This links force types (this node) with motion effects (c8_physics_ch3_2, c8_physics_ch3_3) and net force (c8_physics_ch3_6).
ANSWER: Net force during push+spring = 0.6 N forward. Car stops because after push/spring end, only backward friction acts, causing deceleration to zero.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A book lies on a tilted wooden plank. The book does not slide. Identify all contact forces acting on the book and explain why the book remains at rest even though gravity pulls it down.
COMPETENCY: Interpreting physical situations using force diagrams
STEPS:
- Step 1: Recognize the book touches the plank, so contact forces exist.
- Step 2: Identify the contact forces: normal force (plank pushes perpendicular to surface) and friction (plank pushes parallel to surface, up the incline).
- Step 3: Note gravity (non-contact) pulls vertically down.
- Step 4: Explain equilibrium: the vector sum of normal + friction balances gravity. Friction is a contact force that prevents sliding.
- Step 5: Conclude: without friction (a contact force), the book would slide.
ANSWER: Contact forces: normal force (perpendicular to plank) and static friction (up the plank). These two contact forces combine to balance the downward gravitational force, resulting in zero net force and no motion.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: In a tug-of-war, Team A pulls the rope with a total muscular force of 800 N. Team B pulls with 750 N. The rope is massless and the ground is rough. (a) What is the net force on the rope? (b) What contact force prevents Team A from sliding forward? (c) If Team A's shoes were on frictionless ice, what would happen?
COMPETENCY: Predicting outcomes from changing variables
STEPS:
- Step 1: Net force on rope = 800 N (Team A direction) - 750 N (Team B direction) = 50 N toward Team A.
- Step 2: Team A does not slide because friction between their shoes and ground acts backward (opposing their forward pull). This friction is a contact force from ground on shoes.
- Step 3: Maximum static friction depends on normal force and coefficient. If friction < 800 N, Team A slides.
- Step 4: On frictionless ice, friction ≈ 0. Team A cannot exert 800 N on the rope without sliding; they would accelerate toward Team B.
- Step 5: The rope's tension would drop to whatever force Team A can exert without slipping (near zero on ice).
ANSWER: (a) 50 N toward Team A. (b) Static friction from ground on Team A's shoes (contact force). (c) On ice, Team A slides toward Team B because no friction contact force can anchor them; they cannot maintain a large pull.
DIFFICULTY: hard
