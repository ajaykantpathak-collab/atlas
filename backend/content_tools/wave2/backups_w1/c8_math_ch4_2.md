## ELI5
A rectangle is a pushed-straight parallelogram: keep the sides parallel and equal, then insist the corners are perfect right angles — and once one corner is square, all four must be. Opposite sides still match, and the two diagonals are not only bisecting each other but equal into the bargain. It is the everyday hero of quadrilaterals: doors, books, phone screens, blackboards. One right angle is the gatekeeper — it upgrades an ordinary parallelogram into the rectangle you see everywhere around you.

## DEFINITION
A rectangle is a quadrilateral with four right angles (each measuring 90°). Equivalently, it is a parallelogram in which one angle is a right angle, which forces all angles to be right angles and makes opposite sides equal and parallel.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are designing a rectangular garden bed in your school courtyard. You have four wooden planks: two are 3 metres long and two are 2 metres long. You want all corners to be perfect 90° corners so the bed fits neatly against the wall. How can you be sure the shape you make is a rectangle and not just any parallelogram? Start by recalling that a quadrilateral with opposite sides equal is a parallelogram (c8_math_ch4_1). Now, if you also ensure one corner is 90°, the parallel-line properties force all corners to become 90°. Write down the given side lengths, mark the right angle, and use the property that opposite sides are equal to label the other sides. The key insight: in a rectangle, the right angle is the "gatekeeper" that upgrades a parallelogram to a rectangle.

Takeaway: a rectangle is a parallelogram with one right angle — that single angle forces all four; opposite sides stay equal and parallel, and the diagonals are equal as well as bisecting each other. Exam format: expect 'identify the rectangle and justify' and multi-step side/angle finds; verify the right-angle condition before claiming the name. Verification beat: use opposite-sides-equal to label every side before computing anything. Why students miss it: they call any four-sided box-like figure a rectangle without checking parallelism; the gatekeeper is the right angle plus parallel opposite sides, together — a right-angled trapezium is still not a rectangle.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
State the definition of a rectangle in one sentence using the words "quadrilateral", "right angles", and "parallelogram".
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
A rectangle is a quadrilateral with four right angles, or equivalently a parallelogram with one right angle. A wrong answer that only says "four equal angles" misses the 90° requirement; a student who says "a parallelogram with equal sides" is confusing rectangle with rhombus/square.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
In a rectangle ABCD, side AB = 8 cm and side BC = 5 cm. Without measuring, what are the lengths of CD and AD? Explain why you are certain.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
CD = 8 cm and AD = 5 cm because opposite sides of a rectangle (being a parallelogram) are equal. A wrong answer that gives CD = 5 cm and AD = 8 cm shows the student does not understand which sides are opposite; an answer of "cannot be determined" reveals the student does not connect rectangle to parallelogram properties.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Rectangle PQRS has diagonals PR and QS intersecting at O. If PR = 10 cm, what is the length of QS? What is the length of PO?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
QS = 10 cm (diagonals of a rectangle are equal). PO = 5 cm (diagonals bisect each other, so PO = half of PR). A wrong answer of QS = 5 cm or PO = 10 cm indicates confusion between "equal diagonals" and "bisecting diagonals"; a student who says PO = 2.5 cm might be halving the wrong segment.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
A carpenter builds a frame with opposite sides equal: 120 cm and 80 cm. He measures one angle and finds it is 85°. Is this frame a rectangle? Justify your answer using the definition.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
No, it is not a rectangle. Although opposite sides are equal (making it a parallelogram), a rectangle requires all angles to be 90° (or at least one angle 90°). An 85° angle violates the definition. A student who says "yes, because opposite sides are equal" is confusing parallelogram with rectangle; a student who says "no, because adjacent sides are not equal" is confusing rectangle with square.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Rohan claims: "Every rectangle is a parallelogram, but not every parallelogram is a rectangle." Evaluate this statement and give a counterexample for the second part.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is correct. A rectangle satisfies the parallelogram condition (opposite sides parallel and equal) and adds the right-angle condition. A counterexample for "not every parallelogram is a rectangle" is a rhombus with angles 60° and 120° (or any parallelogram without right angles). A wrong evaluation that says "false, all parallelograms are rectangles" shows a fundamental misunderstanding of the hierarchy; a student who gives a square as a counterexample doesn't realize a square IS a rectangle.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that the diagonals of a rectangle are perpendicular to each other, confusing the rectangle with a rhombus or square. For example, in rectangle ABCD with diagonals intersecting at O, a student might incorrectly state that ∠AOB = 90° and use this to solve for angles in triangle AOB, leading to wrong answers like assuming triangle AOB is an isosceles right triangle when it is only isosceles.
## ON_FAILURE
Draw a non-square rectangle (e.g., length 8 cm, breadth 4 cm) on grid paper. Draw the diagonals and measure the angle of intersection with a protractor. Show it is not 90°. Then fold the rectangle along the diagonal to see the two halves match (congruent triangles), proving diagonals are equal but not perpendicular. Explicitly contrast with a rhombus/square where diagonals are perpendicular. Have the student write: "Rectangle diagonals: equal, bisect each other, NOT perpendicular. Rhombus/Square diagonals: perpendicular bisectors."

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A rectangular playground has a length that is 5 metres more than its breadth. The perimeter of the playground is 90 metres. Find the length and breadth of the playground.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Let the breadth be \(b\) metres. Then length \(l = b + 5\) metres. (Use variable for unknown, express given relation.)
- Step 2: Recall perimeter of rectangle = \(2(l + b)\). Substitute the expressions: \(2((b+5) + b) = 90\). (Apply perimeter formula using opposite sides equal property.)
- Step 3: Simplify inside brackets: \(2(2b + 5) = 90\). Divide both sides by 2: \(2b + 5 = 45\). (Algebraic manipulation.)
- Step 4: Subtract 5: \(2b = 40\). Divide by 2: \(b = 20\). Then \(l = 20 + 5 = 25\). (Solve for breadth, then length.)
- Step 5: Verify: Perimeter = \(2(25 + 20) = 2 \times 45 = 90\) m. Matches given. (Check answer.)
REASONING: The property "opposite sides are equal" is embedded in the perimeter formula \(2(l+b)\). We used algebra to solve for the unknown dimensions because the problem gave a relationship between length and breadth rather than direct values. This connects Class 6 mensuration with Class 8 algebraic expressions.
ANSWER: Length = 25 m, Breadth = 20 m.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: In rectangle ABCD, diagonals AC and BD intersect at O. If ∠OAB = 30°, find ∠OBA, ∠AOB, and ∠DOC.
STEPS:
- Step 1: In rectangle ABCD, diagonals are equal and bisect each other. So OA = OB = OC = OD. (State diagonal properties: equal and bisect.)
- Step 2: In triangle AOB, OA = OB (from Step 1), so ∠OAB = ∠OBA = 30° (angles opposite equal sides). (Isosceles triangle property.)
- Step 3: Sum of angles in triangle AOB = 180°. So ∠AOB = 180° - (30° + 30°) = 120°. (Angle sum property of triangle.)
- Step 4: ∠DOC is vertically opposite to ∠AOB, so ∠DOC = 120°. (Vertically opposite angles are equal.)
- Step 5: Verify: In triangle DOC, OD = OC, so ∠ODC = ∠OCD = (180° - 120°)/2 = 30°. Consistent with rectangle symmetry. (Cross-check.)
REASONING: We combined rectangle diagonal properties (equal and bisect) with triangle angle properties (isosceles triangle, angle sum, vertically opposite angles). This shows how rectangle properties interact with prior geometry knowledge to find multiple unknown angles.
ANSWER: ∠OBA = 30°, ∠AOB = 120°, ∠DOC = 120°.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A rectangular sheet of metal measures 40 cm by 24 cm. Squares of side \(x\) cm are cut from each corner and the sides are folded up to form an open-top box. Express the volume \(V\) of the box as a function of \(x\). What is the domain of \(x\)?
STEPS:
- Step 1: After cutting squares of side \(x\) from each corner, the new length of the box = \(40 - 2x\) cm (two squares removed from length). (Use opposite sides equal: cuts on both ends.)
- Step 2: The new breadth = \(24 - 2x\) cm (two squares removed from breadth). (Same reasoning.)
- Step 3: The height of the box = \(x\) cm (the side of the folded square). (Visualize folding.)
- Step 4: Volume \(V = \text{length} \times \text{breadth} \times \text{height} = (40 - 2x)(24 - 2x)x\). (Volume formula for cuboid.)
- Step 5: Expand: \(V = x(960 - 80x - 48x + 4x^2) = 4x^3 - 128x^2 + 960x\). (Algebraic expansion.)
- Step 6: Domain: \(x > 0\) and \(40 - 2x > 0\) and \(24 - 2x > 0\) → \(x < 12\) and \(x < 20\). So \(0 < x < 12\). (Physical constraints: dimensions must be positive.)
REASONING: This problem integrates rectangle properties (opposite sides equal, right angles for folding), algebraic manipulation, and real-world modelling. The key geometric insight is that cutting equal squares from opposite corners reduces both dimensions by \(2x\), relying on the rectangle's opposite sides being equal and parallel.
ANSWER: \(V(x) = 4x^3 - 128x^2 + 960x\) cm³, domain: \(0 < x < 12\).
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A student says, "If a quadrilateral has equal diagonals, it must be a rectangle." Is this statement true? If not, draw a counterexample and explain which property of a rectangle is missing in your counterexample.
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction / Counterexample construction
STEPS:
- Step 1: Recall the definition of a rectangle (four right angles) and its diagonal properties (equal, bisect each other).
- Step 2: Consider if "equal diagonals" alone guarantees four right angles. Think of other quadrilaterals with equal diagonals.
- Step 3: Construct an isosceles trapezium (trapezoid) with equal diagonals but non-right angles. Label it.
- Step 4: Explain that the counterexample has equal diagonals but lacks right angles (and parallel opposite sides).
ANSWER: The statement is false. An isosceles trapezium has equal diagonals but is not a rectangle because its angles are not 90° (and only one pair of sides is parallel). The missing property is "all angles are right angles" (or "both pairs of opposite sides are parallel").
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: In rectangle PQRS, the diagonals intersect at O. The angle between the diagonals is 70° (i.e., ∠POQ = 70°). Find all angles of triangle POS. Justify each step using rectangle and triangle properties.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions / Multi-step geometric reasoning
STEPS:
- Step 1: Diagonals of rectangle are equal and bisect each other → OP = OQ = OR = OS.
- Step 2: In triangle POQ, OP = OQ → ∠OPQ = ∠OQP. Given ∠POQ = 70°, so ∠OPQ = (180° - 70°)/2 = 55°.
- Step 3: ∠POS = 180° - ∠POQ = 110° (linear pair).
- Step 4: In triangle POS, OP = OS → ∠OPS = ∠OSP. Sum of angles = 180° → ∠OPS = (180° - 110°)/2 = 35°.
- Step 5: Angles of triangle POS: ∠POS = 110°, ∠OPS = 35°, ∠OSP = 35°.
ANSWER: 110°, 35°, 35°. The solution uses diagonal bisection (isosceles triangles), linear pair, and angle sum property.
DIFFICULTY: hard
