## ELI5
Area is the amount of floor a shape covers, and for a rectangle the recipe is one multiply: length times width. Think of laying square tiles across a room — the number of tiles along the wall times the number of rows is exactly the area. One catch: both sides must speak the same unit before you multiply, so convert first. And the recipe runs backwards too — if you know the area and one side, divide, and the missing side steps out of the shadow.

## DEFINITION
The area of a rectangle is the measure of the two-dimensional space enclosed within its four sides, calculated by multiplying its length (the longer side) by its width (the shorter side), provided both measurements are in the same unit. The formula is \( A = l \times w \), where \( A \) is the area, \( l \) is the length, and \( w \) is the width.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are helping your family tile the kitchen floor. The floor is a rectangle measuring 4.5 metres by 3.2 metres. Tiles are sold by the square metre. How many square metres of tiles do you need? Start by identifying the given measurements: length = 4.5 m, width = 3.2 m. Recall the area formula from Class 6: Area = length × width. Substitute the values: \( 4.5 \times 3.2 \). Multiply as decimals: \( 45 \times 32 = 1440 \), then place the decimal two places from the right (one from each factor) to get 14.40. The area is 14.4 m². The key insight is that the formula works for any real-number side lengths, not just whole numbers, because area is a continuous measure.

Takeaway: area of a rectangle = length × width, but only after both sides carry the same unit — convert first, multiply second. Exam format: expect decimals, fractions and inverse problems; method marks come from writing the formula and the unit conversion before arithmetic. Verification beat: check the decimal place — count digits after the decimal point in the factors and match them in the answer. Why students miss it: they multiply mixed units directly, and the answer is then wrong by a huge factor even though every arithmetic step inside the multiplication was perfect.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
State the formula for the area of a rectangle and explain what each letter represents.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The formula is \( A = l \times w \), where \( A \) is the area, \( l \) is the length, and \( w \) is the width. A wrong answer (e.g., \( A = l + w \) or confusing with perimeter) reveals the student has not internalised the multiplicative structure of area and may be conflating perimeter (additive) with area (multiplicative).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why does multiplying length by width give the area? Explain using a 4 cm by 3 cm rectangle drawn on a grid.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
A 4 cm by 3 cm rectangle can be tiled by 4 rows of 3 unit squares each (or 3 columns of 4). Multiplication counts the total unit squares: \( 4 \times 3 = 12 \) cm². If a student says "because the formula says so" or adds the sides, they lack the conceptual understanding of area as an array of unit squares and are relying on rote memorisation.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A rectangular garden is 12.5 m long and 8.4 m wide. Find its area in square metres. Show your working.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
\( 12.5 \times 8.4 = 105.00 \) m². Steps: ignore decimals → \( 125 \times 84 = 10500 \). Total decimal places = 2 (one from each) → place decimal two from right → 105.00. A wrong answer like 1050 m² or 10.5 m² reveals a decimal placement error: the student likely miscounted decimal places or forgot to adjust after multiplying as whole numbers.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
A rectangle has length \( (2x + 3) \) cm and width \( (x - 1) \) cm. Write an algebraic expression for its area. If \( x = 4 \), what is the numerical area?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Area = \( (2x+3)(x-1) = 2x^2 -2x +3x -3 = 2x^2 + x -3 \) cm². For \( x=4 \): \( 2(16)+4-3 = 32+4-3 = 33 \) cm². A wrong answer (e.g., \( 2x^2 -3 \) or substituting before expanding incorrectly) shows the student cannot apply the distributive property to algebraic side lengths or confuses the order of operations.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Ravi calculated the area of a rectangle with length 5 m and width 300 cm as \( 5 \times 300 = 1500 \) m². Is he correct? Explain the error and give the correct area.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Ravi is incorrect. He multiplied metres by centimetres without converting to the same unit. 300 cm = 3 m. Correct area = \( 5 \times 3 = 15 \) m². If a student says 1500 is correct or converts 5 m to 500 cm but forgets to convert the final answer to m², they have not grasped that area units are squared and both dimensions must share the same unit before multiplication.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a word problem for a Class 8 student that requires finding the area of a rectangle where one side is a fraction and the other is a decimal. Provide the problem statement and the solution.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Example problem: "A rectangular cloth is \( \frac{3}{4} \) m wide and 2.5 m long. Find its area in m²." Solution: Convert \( \frac{3}{4} = 0.75 \). Area = \( 0.75 \times 2.5 = 1.875 \) m². A poor problem (e.g., both sides whole numbers, or no unit conversion needed) reveals the student cannot synthesise fraction-decimal conversion with area calculation, indicating a gap in connecting prior number skills to geometry.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often multiply length and width given in different units (e.g., metres and centimetres) without converting, producing an answer with mixed units like "1500 m cm" or incorrectly labelling it m². For example, a rectangle 2 m by 50 cm: they compute \( 2 \times 50 = 100 \) and write 100 m², ignoring that 50 cm = 0.5 m, so the correct area is 1 m². The error stems from treating units as labels rather than values that must be consistent before multiplication.
## ON_FAILURE
When this error occurs, have the student write both measurements with units, then explicitly convert the smaller unit to the larger (or both to the same) using a conversion factor (e.g., 1 m = 100 cm). Show that \( 2 \text{ m} \times 50 \text{ cm} = 2 \text{ m} \times 0.5 \text{ m} = 1 \text{ m}^2 \). Then have them redo the problem by converting both to centimetres: \( 200 \text{ cm} \times 50 \text{ cm} = 10,000 \text{ cm}^2 \), and verify that \( 10,000 \text{ cm}^2 = 1 \text{ m}^2 \). This dual-conversion cements the squared-unit concept.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A rectangular classroom floor is 8.4 metres long and 6.5 metres wide. The school wants to cover it with square tiles of side 0.5 m. How many tiles are needed? (Assume no cutting or wastage.)

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given values: length = 8.4 m, width = 6.5 m, tile side = 0.5 m. We need number of tiles.
- Step 2: Find area of floor using A = l × w. Area_floor = 8.4 × 6.5.
- Step 3: Compute 8.4 × 6.5: ignore decimals → 84 × 65 = 5460. Two decimal places total → 54.60 m².
- Step 4: Find area of one tile: 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25 m².
- Step 5: Divide floor area by tile area: 54.60 ÷ 0.25 = 5460 ÷ 25 = 218.4. Since tiles cannot be fractional, round up to 219 tiles (but problem says no cutting, so we check if 54.60 is exactly divisible by 0.25: 54.60 / 0.25 = 218.4 → not integer, so problem assumption fails; but mathematically 218.4 tiles). However, with no cutting, we must cover fully: need 219 tiles. But the question says assume no cutting or wastage, meaning the floor dimensions must be exact multiples of tile side. 8.4 / 0.5 = 16.8 (not integer), 6.5 / 0.5 = 13 (integer). So actually tiles won't fit exactly. Let's adjust: The problem likely expects area division. We'll compute number of tiles = floor area / tile area = 54.6 / 0.25 = 218.4 → 219 tiles if cutting allowed. But "no cutting" means dimensions must be multiples. Let's re-read: "Assume no cutting or wastage" might mean we only use whole tiles and the floor is exactly covered? Then dimensions must be multiples. 8.4 is not a multiple of 0.5. So maybe the problem expects us to find how many tiles fit without cutting? That would be 16 tiles along length (16 × 0.5 = 8.0 m) and 13 along width (13 × 0.5 = 6.5 m) → 16 × 13 = 208 tiles, leaving a strip uncovered. But typical textbook problems ignore this and just divide areas. We'll go with area division and note the assumption.
- Step 6: Answer: 218.4 tiles, so at least 219 tiles needed if cutting allowed; but with no cutting, the floor cannot be exactly covered. We'll state: 219 tiles (with cutting) or 208 whole tiles (without cutting, leaving a gap). Given typical Class 8 level, they expect area division: 54.6 / 0.25 = 218.4 → 219 tiles.

REASONING: This problem combines rectangle area with decimal multiplication and division, and introduces the practical concept of tiling where the number of tiles = total area ÷ area per tile. It also subtly checks unit consistency (all in metres) and the meaning of "no cutting" in real-world contexts.

ANSWER: 219 tiles (if cutting allowed to cover exactly); or 208 whole tiles fit without cutting, leaving a 0.4 m strip uncovered.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: The area of a rectangular plot is 240 m². Its length is 16 m. Find its width in metres and then convert the width to centimetres.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Given: Area = 240 m², length = 16 m. Need width (w).
- Step 2: Use formula A = l × w → 240 = 16 × w.
- Step 3: Solve for w: w = 240 ÷ 16 = 15 m.
- Step 4: Convert width to centimetres: 1 m = 100 cm → 15 m = 15 × 100 = 1500 cm.
- Step 5: Verify: Area = 16 m × 15 m = 240 m². Correct.
- Step 6: Present answer: width = 15 m = 1500 cm.

REASONING: This is an inverse problem where area and one side are known. It requires division (inverse of multiplication) and unit conversion, reinforcing that area units are squared while length units are linear. The conversion step ensures students distinguish between linear and square units.

ANSWER: Width = 15 m = 1500 cm.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A rectangular field has length \( (3x + 2) \) metres and width \( (2x - 1) \) metres. If the area is 35 m², find the value of x and the actual dimensions of the field. (Assume x is a positive integer.)

STEPS:
- Step 1: Write area expression: A = (3x+2)(2x-1).
- Step 2: Expand using distributive property: (3x)(2x) + (3x)(-1) + (2)(2x) + (2)(-1) = 6x² - 3x + 4x - 2 = 6x² + x - 2.
- Step 3: Set equal to given area: 6x² + x - 2 = 35.
- Step 4: Rearrange to standard quadratic: 6x² + x - 37 = 0.
- Step 5: Solve for x. Since x is a positive integer, test factors: 6x² + x = 37. Try x=2: 24+2=26. x=3: 54+3=57. Not integer. Wait, 6x² + x - 37 = 0 does not have integer roots. Let's adjust problem to have integer solution. Change area to 40? 6x²+x-2=40 → 6x²+x-42=0. Discriminant = 1+1008=1009 not perfect square. Let's design a problem that works: Let length = (x+3), width = (x-1), area = 15. (x+3)(x-1)=15 → x²+2x-3=15 → x²+2x-18=0 → not integer. Let's use: length = (2x+1), width = (x+2), area = 30. (2x+1)(x+2)=30 → 2x²+5x+2=30 → 2x²+5x-28=0 → (2x-? ) not integer. Let's make it simple: length = x+4, width = x, area = 12. x(x+4)=12 → x²+4x-12=0 → (x+6)(x-2)=0 → x=2. Good.
- Revised Problem: A rectangular field has length (x+4) m and width x m. Area = 12 m². Find x and dimensions.
- Step 1: A = x(x+4) = x²+4x.
- Step 2: x²+4x = 12 → x²+4x-12=0.
- Step 3: Factor: (x+6)(x-2)=0 → x = 2 or x = -6. Reject negative.
- Step 4: x = 2 m. Length = 2+4 = 6 m, Width = 2 m.
- Step 5: Check: 6 × 2 = 12 m². Correct.

REASONING: This problem integrates algebraic expansion, quadratic equation solving, and geometric interpretation. Students must translate the word problem into an equation, solve it, and reject extraneous solutions (negative length). It connects algebra (c6_math_ch05_055, c7_math_ch04_009) with geometry.

ANSWER: x = 2 m; length = 6 m, width = 2 m.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A farmer has a rectangular field 120 m long and 80 m wide. He wants to divide it into two equal rectangles by building a fence parallel to the width. What is the area of each smaller rectangle? What is the total length of the new fence needed? If fencing costs ₹50 per metre, what is the total cost?

COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions and multi-step problem solving.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand the problem: original field area, division into two equal rectangles by a fence parallel to width.
- Step 2: Find original area: 120 × 80 = 9600 m². Each smaller rectangle area = 9600 ÷ 2 = 4800 m².
- Step 3: The fence runs parallel to the width, so its length equals the width of the field = 80 m.
- Step 4: Cost = 80 m × ₹50/m = ₹4000.
- Step 5: Verify: Two rectangles each 60 m × 80 m (since length halved) → area = 4800 m² each. Fence length = 80 m. Cost = ₹4000.

ANSWER: Area of each smaller rectangle = 4800 m². Length of new fence = 80 m. Total cost = ₹4000.

DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A rectangular piece of land measures 2.5 km by 1.2 km. A farmer buys it at ₹25,00,000 per square kilometre. He then divides the land into 10 equal rectangular plots by drawing lines parallel to the breadth. What is the area of each plot in square metres? What is the total cost of the land? (1 km = 1000 m)

COMPETENCY: Multi-step reasoning with unit conversion, large numbers, and division of area.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Convert dimensions to km (already in km): length = 2.5 km, breadth = 1.2 km.
- Step 2: Total area = 2.5 × 1.2 = 3.0 km².
- Step 3: Total cost = 3.0 km² × ₹25,00,000/km² = ₹75,00,000.
- Step 4: Land divided into 10 equal plots → each plot area = 3.0 ÷ 10 = 0.3 km².
- Step 5: Convert each plot area to m²: 1 km² = 1000 m × 1000 m = 10,00,000 m². So 0.3 km² = 0.3 × 10,00,000 = 3,00,000 m².
- Step 6: Verify: 10 plots × 3,00,000 m² = 30,00,000 m² = 3 km². Correct.

ANSWER: Area of each plot = 3,00,000 m² (or 0.3 km²). Total cost of land = ₹75,00,000.

DIFFICULTY: hard
