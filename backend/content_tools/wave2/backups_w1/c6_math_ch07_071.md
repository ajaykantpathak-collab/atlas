## ELI5
Lines get along in three ways. Railway rails run side by side forever and never touch - parallel. Two streets cross at one junction - intersecting. And when the crossing makes a perfect square corner, like a notebook's edge, they are perpendicular - intersecting's well-behaved special case. Desks, window grills and railway tracks are the everyday gallery of all three.

## DEFINITION
Lines are classified by their mutual position: parallel lines never intersect despite infinite extension, intersecting lines share exactly one common point, and perpendicular lines are a special case of intersecting lines that meet at a 90-degree angle.

## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever looked at a railway track and wondered why the two iron rails never touch each other? Start by observing your classroom: look at the opposite edges of your desk (parallel), the corner of your notebook where two edges meet (perpendicular), and the criss-cross pattern of a window grill (intersecting). To begin, draw two lines on a paper and extend them; if they cross, they are intersecting. If they form a perfect 'L' shape, check with the corner of a sheet of paper to see if it fits exactly—if it does, they are perpendicular. If they stay exactly the same distance apart, you have found parallel lines. The key is to look for the "gap" between them and check for the point of contact.

**Takeaway:** parallel lines never meet, intersecting lines cross once, and perpendicular lines cross at a square corner. **In the exam:** in classification questions, look for the meeting point first, then test the corner for squareness. **Check yourself:** tour the walkthrough's classroom - desk edges, notebook corners and window grills show all three relationships. **Why students miss it:** students call close-but-untouched lines parallel - the walkthrough's rails are a no to that; parallel means constant separation, not closeness on the page. Rehearse the exam move once more - meet, corner, classify - and the answer arranges itself in exam-ready order with nothing left to chance.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What do we call two lines that cross each other at a single point?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
These are called intersecting lines. A student who says "parallel" reveals a fundamental confusion between lines that never meet and lines that cross.

**A wrong answer (calling the crossers parallel) reveals the student swapped the cast - crossing at a single point is the intersecting family's signature.**

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If two lines are perpendicular, what must be true about the angle they make?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
They must meet at a right angle (90 degrees). Answering "they just touch" ignores the specific requirement of the 90-degree turn, showing the student hasn't distinguished perpendicular lines from general intersecting ones.

**A wrong answer (accepting any touch as perpendicular) reveals the student missed the squareness test - only a square corner earns the perpendicular name.**

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Two lines are drawn on a paper. One is horizontal and one is vertical. They meet at a corner. Are they parallel, intersecting, or perpendicular?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
They are perpendicular. A student who says "intersecting" is technically correct but incomplete; missing the "perpendicular" classification shows they aren't identifying the 90-degree property.

**A wrong answer (stopping at intersecting) reveals the student half-classified - the horizontal-and-vertical meeting is the special square-corner case.**

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
If I extend two parallel lines for 1 kilometre, will they eventually meet? Why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
No, they will never meet because they maintain a constant distance from each other. Answering "yes" suggests the student thinks parallel lines are just "lines that haven't met yet" rather than lines that are physically incapable of meeting.

**A wrong answer (predicting an eventual meeting) reveals the student read parallel as not-yet-met - constant separation survives every extension.**

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Look at the letter 'H'. Identify the relationship between the two vertical bars and the horizontal bar.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The two vertical bars are parallel to each other, and the horizontal bar is perpendicular to both vertical bars. A student who misses the parallel relationship is not observing the relative positions of all parts of the shape.

**A wrong answer (missing one of the two relationships) reveals the student read the letter halfway - the uprights pair off in parallel while the crossbar meets each at a square corner.**

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Draw a shape using only parallel and perpendicular lines. Explain your choice.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A rectangle or a square is the best example. A poor answer (like a triangle) reveals the student doesn't understand that parallel/perpendicular lines require specific orientations that triangles don't consistently provide.

**A wrong answer (drawing a shape neither family can build) reveals the student ignored the orientations - the demanded figure must live entirely on parallels and square corners.**

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that if two lines are "close" to each other but don't touch on the paper, they are parallel. They fail to realize that if they were to extend them, they might cross. For example, they might label two lines that form a very thin 'V' shape as parallel because the gap is small.
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to place a ruler along each line and extend the lines further off the edge of the paper using a pencil. Ask: "If you keep drawing, will the gap between these lines grow or shrink?" If the gap changes, they are not parallel; explain that parallel lines must maintain the exact same gap at every point.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A carpenter is building a wooden frame. He joins two beams of wood, each 2 metres long, such that they form a perfect corner of a book. He then places a third beam parallel to the first one. Identify the relationships between the three beams.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the beams as Beam A, Beam B, and Beam C.
- Step 2: Beam A and Beam B meet at a corner (right angle), so they are perpendicular.
- Step 3: Beam C is placed parallel to Beam A.
- Step 4: Since Beam C is parallel to A, and A is perpendicular to B, Beam C must also be perpendicular to B.
REASONING: Parallel lines share the same direction, so any line perpendicular to one must be perpendicular to the other.
ANSWER: Beam A ⊥ Beam B; Beam A ∥ Beam C; Beam B ⊥ Beam C.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Identify the types of lines in the letter 'N'.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Observe the three segments of the letter 'N'.
- Step 2: The two vertical-like segments are connected by a diagonal segment.
- Step 3: Check if the two vertical segments are parallel (extend them). They will meet, so they are not parallel.
- Step 4: Check if any segments meet at 90 degrees. None do.
REASONING: Lines must maintain equal distance to be parallel and meet at 90 degrees to be perpendicular.
ANSWER: The segments are all intersecting lines, but none are parallel or perpendicular.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Draw two lines, $l$ and $m$, that intersect at point $P$. Then draw line $n$ that is perpendicular to $l$ at $P$. How many angles are formed at point $P$?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Draw intersecting lines $l$ and $m$ meeting at $P$.
- Step 2: Draw line $n$ through $P$ so it makes a 90-degree angle with $l$.
- Step 3: Count the lines passing through $P$: $l, m, n$.
- Step 4: Intersecting lines create 4 regions (angles) at the intersection point.
- Step 5: Adding a third line ($n$) creates more sub-divisions.
REASONING: Intersection points act as vertices; adding lines increases the number of angles formed at the central vertex.
ANSWER: 6 angles are formed at point $P$.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You are given a grid paper. Draw a line segment $AB$. Draw a line $CD$ parallel to $AB$ using the grid lines. Now, draw a line $EF$ that intersects both $AB$ and $CD$. If $EF$ is not perpendicular to $AB$, can it be perpendicular to $CD$? Explain your reasoning.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- Step 1: Use grid lines to ensure $AB$ and $CD$ are parallel (same distance apart).
- Step 2: Draw $EF$ at a slant across both.
- Step 3: Observe the angles at the intersections.
- Step 4: Since $CD$ is parallel to $AB$, any line cutting $AB$ at a specific angle will cut $CD$ at the same angle.
ANSWER: No, if $EF$ is not perpendicular to $AB$, it cannot be perpendicular to $CD$. Because the lines are parallel, they share the same orientation relative to any transversal line.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Look at a clock face at 3:00 and 6:00. Identify the relationship between the hour hand and the minute hand in both cases.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations
STEPS:
- Step 1: Visualize/draw a clock at 3:00 (Hour hand at 3, Minute hand at 12).
- Step 2: Visualize/draw a clock at 6:00 (Hour hand at 6, Minute hand at 12).
- Step 3: Analyze the 3:00 position: The hands form a perfect corner (90 degrees).
- Step 4: Analyze the 6:00 position: The hands form a straight line (180 degrees).
ANSWER: At 3:00, the hands are perpendicular. At 6:00, the hands are intersecting (but not perpendicular).
DIFFICULTY: hard
