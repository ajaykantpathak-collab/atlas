## ELI5
Heat and temperature are cousins, not twins. Temperature reads how fast a substance's particles jiggle - its average kinetic energy; heat is the energy that jiggles downstream, flowing from hotter to cooler until both agree. A bucket of warm bathwater holds more total heat than a cup of fierce tea, though the tea feels hotter. Heat is measured in joules; temperature in degrees - and Kelvin in the lab. Flow is the story; intensity is the caption.

## DEFINITION
Heat is a form of energy that transfers from a region of higher temperature to a region of lower temperature, measured in joules (J), while temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles within a substance, measured in degrees Celsius (°C) or Kelvin (K).

## WALKTHROUGH

When we touch different objects in a classroom, some feel colder than others despite being in the same room because of how heat transfers to or from our skin. Building on our Class 6 understanding of how heat flows, we now explore how temperature quantifies the "hotness" of this flow. We define temperature as a measure of how fast particles move, whereas heat is the total energy moving between bodies. Keep in mind that temperature dictates the *direction* of heat flow, always moving from high temperature to low temperature.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think that a larger object always has a higher temperature than a smaller object if both are exposed to the same sun, or they believe that "temperature" and "heat" are the exact same physical quantity. For example, they might think a massive ice sculpture at 0°C contains less thermal energy than a glowing hot spark, yet confuse which one causes a worse burn because they mix up temperature intensity with total heat energy.
## ON_FAILURE
To fix this, have the student perform a simple tactile comparison: take a large bucket of tap water and a small cup of the same tap water. Explain that both have the exact same temperature, but the bucket contains vastly more water molecules and thus more total thermal energy. Emphasize that temperature tells us *how hot* something feels (average speed of particles), while heat tells us *how much total energy* is moving (total energy of all particles combined).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A vessel containing water in a school laboratory absorbs 4200 Joules of heat energy. If the mass of the water is 0.5 kg and its specific heat capacity is 4200 J/kg·°C, calculate the rise in temperature of the water.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify given values: $Q = 4200 \text{ J}$, mass $m = 0.5 \text{ kg}$, specific heat capacity $c = 4200 \text{ J/kg·°C}$. Find $\Delta T$.
- step 2: State the formula linking these variables: $Q = m \times c \times \Delta T$, which rearranges to $\Delta T = \frac{Q}{m \times c}$.
- step 3: Substitute the given values with units into the rearranged formula: $\Delta T = \frac{4200 \text{ J}}{0.5 \text{ kg} \times 4200 \text{ J/kg·°C}}$.
- step 4: Compute the denominator: $0.5 \times 4200 = 2100 \text{ J/°C}$, then divide numerator by denominator: $\frac{4200}{2100} = 2$. Track unit cancellation: $\frac{\text{J}}{\text{kg} \times (\text{J/kg·°C})} = \text{°C}$.
- step 5: Verify the final answer is physically reasonable: a temperature rise of 2°C for a moderate water sample absorbing energy is entirely logical. State final answer: 2°C.
REASONING: By rearranging the heat energy formula, we isolate the temperature change variable. Tracking units ensures that kg and J cancel out properly, leaving the correct unit of degrees Celsius for temperature.
ANSWER: 2 °C
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: An iron pan of mass 1.5 kg in a kitchen in Delhi receives 675 Joules of heat. If the specific heat capacity of iron is 450 J/kg·°C, what is the resulting change in temperature of the iron pan?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify given values: $Q = 675 \text{ J}$, $m = 1.5 \text{ kg}$, $c = 450 \text{ J/kg·°C}$. Find $\Delta T$.
- step 2: Write the heat energy equation: $Q = m \times c \times \Delta T$. Rearrange to solve for temperature change: $\Delta T = \frac{Q}{m \times c}$.
- step 3: Substitute the values into the equation: $\Delta T = \frac{675 \text{ J}}{1.5 \text{ kg} \times 450 \text{ J/kg·°C}}$.
- step 4: Multiply the denominator terms: $1.5 \times 450 = 675 \text{ J/°C}$. Then divide: $\frac{675 \text{ J}}{675 \text{ J/°C}} = 1 \text{ °C}$. Units cancel correctly to °C.
- step 5: Verify physical reasonableness: a small iron pan gaining modest energy shifts temperature slightly by 1°C. State final answer: 1°C.
REASONING: This problem reinforces algebraic isolation of variables in thermal equations, showing how mass and specific heat capacity combine to resist temperature change.
ANSWER: 1 °C
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A copper block of mass 2 kg requires 3800 Joules of heat energy to raise its temperature. If the specific heat capacity of copper is 380 J/kg·°C, and it starts at an initial room temperature of 25°C, calculate its final temperature.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify given values: $Q = 3800 \text{ J}$, $m = 2 \text{ kg}$, $c = 380 \text{ J/kg·°C}$, initial temperature $T_1 = 25 \text{ °C}$. Find final temperature $T_2$.
- step 2: Use the formula for heat energy to find the temperature change: $\Delta T = \frac{Q}{m \times c}$.
- step 3: Substitute values: $\Delta T = \frac{3800 \text{ J}}{2 \text{ kg} \times 380 \text{ J/kg·°C}}$.
- step 4: Compute denominator ($2 \times 380 = 760$) and division ($\frac{3800}{760} = 5 \text{ °C}$). This is the temperature change ($\Delta T$).
- step 5: Relate change to initial temperature: Final Temperature $T_2 = T_1 + \Delta T = 25 \text{ °C} + 5 \text{ °C} = 30 \text{ °C}$. Verify that adding heat raises the temperature above the initial value.
REASONING: Combining the thermal energy formula with initial-final temperature relations allows us to track exact state changes in materials during heating processes.
ANSWER: 30 °C

QUESTION: Explain why two identical metallic blocks made of the same material can both be at a temperature of 50°C, yet one block can melt more ice than the other if their masses are different.
COMPETENCY: Predicting outcomes from changing variables

- step 3: Recognize that the heavier block contains more atoms/particles in total, thus holding a larger total amount of heat energy ($Q = m \cdot c \cdot \Delta T$).
- step 4: Conclude that the block with greater mass supplies more total heat energy to melt more ice despite sharing the same temperature.
ANSWER: The heavier block melts more ice because total heat energy depends on mass as well as temperature. Even at the same temperature, a larger mass contains more total thermal energy to transfer.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: During an experiment in a Chennai laboratory, a student places a hot copper ball into a beaker of cold water. Describe the microscopic particle motion changes in both the copper ball and the water until they reach thermal equilibrium, and explain how temperature indicates this process.
COMPETENCY: Interpreting physical situations using microscopic models
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the initial condition: hot copper ball has fast-moving particles (high temperature); cold water has slower-moving particles (low temperature).
- step 2: Apply the direction of heat transfer: energy flows from the copper ball to the water because heat moves from high to low temperature.
- step 3: Describe particle interactions: collisions between fast copper particles and water molecules cause copper particles to slow down and water molecules to speed up.
- step 4: Explain the final state (thermal equilibrium): particle speeds equalize across both substances, meaning their temperatures become equal, and net heat transfer stops.
ANSWER: As heat flows from the hot copper ball to the cold water, fast copper particles transfer energy through collisions, slowing down while water molecules speed up. Temperature reflects this average particle speed, and equilibrium is reached when both substances attain the same temperature and particle motion levels balance out.
DIFFICULTY: hard
