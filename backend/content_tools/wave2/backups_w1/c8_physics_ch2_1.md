## ELI5
Run electricity through a wire and something invisible wakes up around it: a magnetic field. Bring a compass near and its needle — usually a loyal pointer to north — twitches aside. The moment the current stops, the needle settles back. Electricity has a hidden magnetism, discovered by accident with exactly this experiment. The switch becomes a magic wand: on, the needle dances; off, it sleeps. Current and magnetism are not separate worlds; they are one phenomenon holding hands.

## DEFINITION
When an electric current flows through a conducting wire, it produces a magnetic field around it. This magnetic field interacts with the magnetic field of a nearby compass needle, causing the needle to deflect from its north-south alignment. The deflection demonstrates that electricity and magnetism are interrelated phenomena.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can predict the direction of compass needle deflection when given the direction of current in a straight wire, and can explain why the deflection occurs using the concept of magnetic fields produced by electric current. The student can also design a simple circuit to demonstrate this effect using materials available in a school laboratory.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the qualitative observation that a current-carrying wire deflects a compass needle (Oersted's experiment), the direction of deflection relative to current direction, and the conclusion that electric current creates a magnetic field. It does NOT cover quantitative magnetic field strength, the right-hand thumb rule (introduced in next node), electromagnets, or mathematical descriptions of magnetic fields.
## WALKTHROUGH
Start with a real-world observation: when you bring a magnetic compass near a wire connected to a battery and switch, the needle moves when the switch is pressed. Ask students what they think causes this movement - they know from Class 6 that a compass needle aligns with Earth's magnetic field (c6_physics_ch11_040). Connect this to their Class 7 knowledge of complete circuits (c7_physics_ch02_006) - the wire must be part of a closed circuit for current to flow. Guide them to observe that the needle deflects only when the circuit is closed, and returns to north-south when open. This establishes that the electric current itself creates a magnetic field that interacts with the compass needle's field.

Takeaway: a current-carrying wire produces a magnetic field around itself; a nearby compass detects it by deflecting, and only while the circuit is closed. Exam format: expect 'predict the deflection direction' and 'design the demonstration' — the closed-versus-open circuit contrast is the evidence the answer needs. Verification beat: confirm the needle returns to north-south when the circuit opens; that reset proves the field belongs to the current. Why students miss it: they say 'magnets affect compasses' and miss the causality — no battery, no deflection, so the electricity itself is the magnet's maker.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
A compass needle points north-south when placed far from any wire. When a straight wire carrying current from a battery is placed parallel to the needle (north-south direction) and the circuit is closed, the needle deflects. What does this observation tell us about electric current?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The observation tells us that an electric current produces a magnetic field around the wire. This magnetic field interacts with the magnetic field of the compass needle, causing deflection. A wrong answer (e.g., "the battery attracts the needle" or "the wire becomes a magnet permanently") reveals the student thinks the effect is due to static electricity or permanent magnetization, not understanding that the magnetic field exists only while current flows.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
In Oersted's experiment, the compass needle deflects when placed above a horizontal wire carrying current from south to north. If the battery connections are reversed so current flows north to south, how will the deflection direction change? Explain why.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The needle will deflect in the opposite direction. Reversing current reverses the direction of the magnetic field produced around the wire (as per the right-hand rule, though not formally taught yet). A wrong answer (e.g., "deflection stays the same" or "needle stops deflecting") reveals the student does not grasp that magnetic field direction depends on current direction - they may think the wire simply "becomes magnetic" regardless of current flow direction.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A student sets up a circuit with a 1.5 V cell, a switch, and a long straight wire. The compass is placed 5 cm below the wire. When the switch is closed, the needle deflects 30° from north-south. The student then adds a second identical cell in series (total 3.0 V) and repeats. Predict qualitatively how the deflection angle will change and explain your reasoning.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The deflection angle will increase (become larger than 30°). Higher voltage drives more current through the same wire (Ohm's law, though not formally required), producing a stronger magnetic field, which exerts a greater torque on the compass needle. A wrong answer (e.g., "angle stays 30°" or "angle decreases") reveals the student doesn't connect current magnitude to magnetic field strength - they may think the effect is binary (on/off) rather than proportional to current.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Two identical circuits are set up side by side. Circuit A has a thick copper wire; Circuit B has a thin copper wire of the same length. Both use the same 1.5 V cell. A compass placed at the same distance from each wire shows different deflections. Which circuit produces greater deflection and why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Circuit A (thick wire) produces greater deflection. Thicker wire has lower resistance, allowing more current to flow for the same voltage. More current creates a stronger magnetic field, causing larger deflection. A wrong answer (e.g., "thin wire deflects more" or "both same") reveals confusion between wire thickness and resistance, or failure to apply their Class 7 knowledge of conductors (c7_physics_ch02_009) to this magnetic context.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student claims: "The compass needle deflects because the electric current flows through the compass needle itself." Evaluate this claim. Is it correct? If not, identify the error and explain the correct mechanism.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The claim is incorrect. The compass needle is not part of the electrical circuit - it is just a magnet free to rotate. The current flows only through the wire. The wire's current creates a magnetic field around it, and this field exerts a torque on the magnetized needle, causing deflection. A wrong evaluation reveals the student confuses the path of current with the region of magnetic influence - a fundamental misunderstanding of action-at-a-distance via fields.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple experiment using only a 1.5 V cell, a switch, insulated copper wire, a compass, and cardboard to demonstrate that the magnetic field around a current-carrying wire forms concentric circles. Describe your setup, procedure, and what observation would confirm circular field lines.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Place the cardboard horizontally. Pass the wire vertically through a hole in the cardboard so it stands upright. Connect the wire to the cell and switch. Place the compass at various positions on the cardboard around the wire. Close the switch and mark the needle direction at each position. The needle will align tangentially to circles centered on the wire, confirming circular field lines. A poor answer (e.g., "move compass along wire" or "use iron filings") shows inability to translate the concept of field lines into a practical investigation using available materials.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe the wire itself becomes a permanent magnet when current flows, and that the compass needle is attracted to the wire like iron filings to a magnet. They think the deflection is due to magnetic attraction between the wire and needle, not understanding that the wire produces a magnetic field that exerts a torque on the magnetic dipole of the needle. For example, they may expect the needle to point directly toward the wire (radial attraction) rather than perpendicular to the radial direction (tangential alignment with field lines).
## ON_FAILURE
1. Have the student hold a compass near a vertical wire connected to a battery. 2. Ask them to observe the needle direction when current flows UP vs DOWN (reverse battery). 3. Guide them to see the needle aligns PERPENDICULAR to the wire, not pointing AT the wire. 4. Use iron filings on cardboard with vertical wire to visualize circular field lines. 5. Emphasize: the wire is not "magnetized" - it creates a field only while current flows. The needle is a dipole aligning with the field, not being "pulled" toward the wire.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: In a school laboratory, a student connects a 1.5 V dry cell, a switch, and a 1-meter long insulated copper wire in series. The wire is laid straight in the north-south direction on a wooden table. A magnetic compass is placed 3 cm directly above the wire. Initially the switch is open and the compass needle points geographic north. When the switch is closed, current flows from south to north through the wire. The student observes the compass needle deflects toward the west. Explain this observation step by step.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the given quantities - voltage = 1.5 V, wire orientation = north-south, current direction = south to north, compass position = 3 cm above wire, initial needle direction = north.
- Step 2: Recall the principle - electric current produces a magnetic field forming concentric circles around the wire (right-hand rule: thumb = current direction, fingers = field direction).
- Step 3: Apply the principle - for current south to north, the magnetic field above the wire points west (using right-hand rule: grasp wire with right hand, thumb points north, fingers curl west above wire).
- Step 4: Analyze the interaction - the compass needle aligns with the net magnetic field. Earth's field points north; wire's field points west. The needle deflects toward the resultant direction, which is northwest, appearing as a westward deflection from north.
- Step 5: Verify physically - the deflection is perpendicular to the wire, not toward it, confirming field is circular not radial. The observation matches the principle that current creates a magnetic field.

REASONING: The compass needle is a magnetic dipole that experiences torque in a magnetic field. Earth's magnetic field exerts torque aligning it north-south. The current-carrying wire adds a second magnetic field perpendicular to Earth's field above the wire. The needle aligns with the vector sum of both fields, deflecting westward. This demonstrates the magnetic effect of current qualitatively.

ANSWER: The needle deflects west because the magnetic field produced by the south-to-north current points west above the wire, and the needle aligns with the resultant of Earth's field (north) and wire's field (west).
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A student repeats the experiment with the same setup but places the compass 3 cm BELOW the wire (instead of above). Current still flows south to north. Predict the direction of deflection and explain why it differs from the "above" case.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the change - compass position is now below the wire, current direction unchanged (south to north).
- Step 2: Apply right-hand rule for magnetic field direction - thumb points north (current), fingers curl: above wire = west, below wire = east.
- Step 3: Determine wire's magnetic field at compass location - below the wire, the field points east.
- Step 4: Find resultant field - Earth's field (north) + wire's field (east) = northeast resultant.
- Step 5: Predict deflection - needle aligns with resultant, deflecting toward east from north.
- Step 6: Compare with above case - above wire: west deflection; below wire: east deflection. The deflection direction reverses because the magnetic field direction reverses on opposite sides of the wire.

REASONING: The magnetic field lines form concentric circles around the wire. On opposite sides of the wire, the field direction is opposite (tangential to circles in opposite senses). The compass needle always aligns tangentially to the field lines, so it deflects in opposite directions above vs below the wire. This confirms the circular nature of the field.

ANSWER: The needle deflects toward the east when placed below the wire with current flowing south to north.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A student wants to demonstrate that the magnetic effect of current depends on current magnitude. They have two 1.5 V cells, a switch, a long copper wire, and a compass. Design a procedure to show that doubling the voltage (using two cells in series) increases the deflection angle. Include how to keep other factors constant and what to measure.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Set up baseline circuit - one cell, switch, wire in series. Place compass at fixed distance (e.g., 2 cm) from wire. Mark initial north direction.
- Step 2: Close switch, observe and record deflection angle θ₁ using a protractor placed under compass. Open switch, confirm needle returns to north.
- Step 3: Modify circuit - add second cell in series (total 3.0 V), keeping same wire, same switch, same compass position.
- Step 4: Close switch again, record new deflection angle θ₂. Open switch.
- Step 5: Compare θ₂ with θ₁ - if θ₂ > θ₁, it confirms larger current produces stronger magnetic field and greater deflection.
- Step 6: Control variables - same wire (same resistance), same compass, same distance, same orientation. Only voltage changes.

REASONING: This experiment isolates current magnitude as the variable. From Ohm's law (V=IR), doubling voltage across the same resistance doubles the current. The magnetic field strength is proportional to current (B ∝ I), so the torque on the compass needle increases, causing larger deflection. This connects electrical quantity (current) to magnetic effect quantitatively.

ANSWER: The deflection angle increases (θ₂ > θ₁) when voltage is doubled, demonstrating that magnetic effect is proportional to current magnitude.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A compass is placed near a vertical wire carrying current upward. The needle deflects and aligns itself tangent to an imaginary circle centered on the wire. If the current is suddenly reversed (now downward), describe what happens to the compass needle and explain the physics. Does the needle rotate clockwise or counterclockwise as viewed from above?

STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand the physical situation - vertical wire, current initially upward, compass needle tangent to circular field line.
- Step 2: Identify the principle - magnetic field direction reverses when current reverses (right-hand rule).
- Step 3: Apply to initial state - current upward → field lines counterclockwise (viewed from above) → needle points tangent counterclockwise.
- Step 4: Apply to reversed state - current downward → field lines clockwise → needle must realign to new tangent direction (clockwise).
- Step 5: Determine rotation - needle rotates from counterclockwise tangent to clockwise tangent, which is a 180° flip. The direction of rotation depends on initial position but the final alignment is opposite.

ANSWER: The needle rotates 180° to align with the reversed magnetic field direction. If initially aligned counterclockwise (tangent to CCW circle), it flips to clockwise tangent. The rotation direction (CW or CCW during the flip) depends on which way is shorter, but the final orientation reverses.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Two students debate: Student A says "The compass needle deflects because the current in the wire attracts the north pole of the needle." Student B says "The needle deflects because the current creates a magnetic field that exerts a torque on the magnetic dipole of the needle." Design a simple experiment using only a compass, wire, battery, and switch that would prove Student B correct and Student A wrong. Explain what observation would support Student B.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Analyze Student A's claim - predicts radial attraction (needle points toward wire).
- Step 2: Analyze Student B's claim - predicts tangential alignment (needle perpendicular to radial line).
- Step 3: Design test - place compass at fixed distance east of vertical wire. Current upward.
- Step 4: Predict Student A: needle's north pole points west (toward wire).
- Step 5: Predict Student B: needle aligns north-south (tangent to circle, perpendicular to radial east-west line).
- Step 6: Conduct experiment - observe actual needle direction.
- Step 7: Interpret - if needle points north-south (tangential), Student B is correct; if points toward wire (radial), Student A is correct.

ANSWER: Place compass east of vertical wire with current upward. Student A predicts needle points west (toward wire). Student B predicts needle points north-south (tangential to circular field). Observation of north-south alignment confirms Student B - the needle aligns with the field direction, not attracted radially to the wire.

DIFFICULTY: hard
