## ELI5
Two school bells ring every 6 and every 8 minutes; listing their multiple timetables side by side finds their first duet. Write each bell's multiples in order, stop at the first number wearing both uniforms - that is the LCM, because every later meeting is only bigger. Listing has one rule: never skip a step, since the missing rung is often the very answer. And when one number divides the other outright, the larger number is already the meeting point.

## DEFINITION
Finding the Least Common Multiple (LCM) by the listing multiples method involves writing out the non-zero multiples of each given number in increasing order, identifying the common multiples they share, and selecting the smallest one among those common multiples.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine two school bell rings: one bell rings every 6 minutes and another bell rings every 8 minutes. If both bells ring together right now, after how many minutes will they ring together again for the first time? To solve this, we need to track the exact minutes when each bell sounds by writing down their multiples. Building on our knowledge of factors and multiples from Class 6, we can systematically list the table of multiples for each number. The key insight is that the "least common multiple" is simply the smallest number that appears on every list of multiples we generate.

**Takeaway:** LCM by listing: write successive multiples, stop at the first number common to both lists. **In the exam:** keep the lists in strict increasing order, both at once, and box the first match. **Check yourself:** extend the bell timetables one row further and confirm no earlier meeting exists. **Why students miss it:** they skip rungs in the list and miss the smallest common multiple hiding in the gap. Two columns in strict increasing order, and the first shared number simply cannot hide. The first shared number in those honest, orderly columns is the meeting both school bells are waiting for.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often confuse the listing of multiples with finding factors, or they stop listing too early before the first common multiple appears. For instance, when finding the LCM of 4 and 10, a student might list factors (1, 2, 4 and 1, 2, 5, 10) instead of multiples (4, 8, 12... and 10, 20...), or they might stop at 4 and 10, mistakenly concluding 4 or 10 is the LCM because they haven't extended the tables far enough to find 20.
## ON_FAILURE
Remind the student of the difference between factors (numbers that divide evenly *into* a number) and multiples (numbers obtained by multiplying the number by 1, 2, 3...). Have them draw two separate number rows or multiplication tables starting with $1 \times n$, $2 \times n$, $3 \times n$, and instruct them to write down at least 6 to 8 multiples for each number before looking for matches. Explicitly ask: "Are we looking for what goes *into* this number, or what this number grows *into*?"

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Two traffic lights at a busy crossing in Delhi flash their yellow warning lights. The first light flashes every 4 minutes, and the second light flashes every 6 minutes. If they flash together right now, after how many minutes will they flash together again?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the given intervals for each traffic light, which are 4 minutes and 6 minutes.
- Step 2: List the non-zero multiples of the first number (4): $4 \times 1 = 4$, $4 \times 2 = 8$, $4 \times 3 = 12$, $4 \times 4 = 16$, $4 \times 5 = 20$.
- Step 3: List the non-zero multiples of the second number (6): $6 \times 1 = 6$, $6 \times 2 = 12$, $6 \times 3 = 18$, $6 \times 4 = 24$.
- Step 4: Compare both lists to find the smallest number that appears in both: Multiples of 4 include 12, and multiples of 6 include 12.
- Step 5: State the final result with proper units: The LCM of 4 and 6 is 12, so the lights will flash together again after 12 minutes.
REASONING: Since the lights repeat at fixed intervals of 4 and 6 minutes respectively, the time when they flash together must be a common multiple of both numbers. By listing the multiples, we find the exact first meeting point, which represents the least common multiple.
ANSWER: 12 minutes
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Priya and Rahul are running on a circular running track at the school sports complex. Priya completes one round in 8 minutes, and Rahul completes one round in 12 minutes. If they start running from the same starting line at the same time in the same direction, after how many minutes will they meet again at the starting line?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the time taken for one round by each runner: Priya takes 8 minutes, Rahul takes 12 minutes.
- Step 2: Generate the sequence of multiples for Priya's time (8): $8 \times 1 = 8$, $8 \times 2 = 16$, $8 \times 3 = 24$, $8 \times 4 = 32$, $8 \times 5 = 40$.
- Step 3: Generate the sequence of multiples for Rahul's time (12): $12 \times 1 = 12$, $12 \times 2 = 24$, $12 \times 3 = 36$, $12 \times 4 = 48$.
- Step 4: Examine both lists to locate matching entries: The number 24 appears as the third multiple of 8 and the second multiple of 12.
- Step 5: Conclude the calculation with appropriate wording and units: The least common multiple of 8 and 12 is 24, so they will meet at the starting line after 24 minutes.
REASONING: Meeting at the starting line means both runners must have completed a whole number of rounds, which corresponds to elapsed times that are exact multiples of their individual lap times. The first time they meet is the smallest shared time, meaning the LCM of 8 and 12.
ANSWER: 24 minutes
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Three bells in a temple ring at intervals of 3 minutes, 5 minutes, and 6 minutes respectively. If all three bells ring together at 8:00 AM, find the exact time when they will all ring together again.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the individual ringing intervals: 3 minutes, 5 minutes, and 6 minutes.
- Step 2: List the multiples of the first interval (3): 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, 24, 27, 30, 33...
- Step 3: List the multiples of the second interval (5): 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40...
- Step 4: List the multiples of the third interval (6): 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36...
- Step 5: Compare all three lists to find the smallest number common to all three sequences: The number 30 is present in the table of 3 ($3 \times 10$), the table of 5 ($5 \times 6$), and the table of 6 ($6 \times 5$).
- Step 6: Calculate the new time by adding the LCM duration in minutes to the starting time: $8:00\text{ AM} + 30\text{ minutes} = 8:30\text{ AM}$.
REASONING: For three independent events recurring at different intervals to happen simultaneously, the elapsed time must be a common multiple of all three numbers. By extending the lists until a shared value appears across all three rows, we find the LCM, which is then added to the initial timestamp to get the exact clock time.
ANSWER: 8:30 AM
