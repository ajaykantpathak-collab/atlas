## ELI5
Spin a ceiling fan in the classroom and you hold a model of the Earth: a sphere whirling around an invisible rod. Our planet turns on its axis from west to east, one full lap in roughly a day - and that spin, not any celestial carousel, spins night and day across the surface. The side facing the sun shops in daylight; the far side sleeps. One rotation per day: the oldest clock still running.

## DEFINITION
The rotation of the Earth is the spinning of the Earth on its imaginary axis from west to east, completing one full rotation in approximately 24 hours, which causes the cycle of day and night. The physical quantity measured here is time period (hours, seconds) and angular displacement (degrees, radians), with the SI unit for time being the second (s).

## WALKTHROUGH
Observe how a ceiling fan spins around a fixed central metal rod in your classroom. If you stick a small glowing torch light on one wall and rotate a globe or a ball in front of it, you will notice that only one half of the ball gets illuminated by the torch light while the other half remains in shadow. As you slowly spin the ball, the side that was in darkness rotates into the light, and the side that was in light moves into darkness. This simple observation mirrors how the Earth rotates on its axis relative to the Sun. We measure this motion by tracking the passage of time over a 24-hour day-night cycle. Connecting back to our prior understanding of how light travels in straight lines and forms shadows, the Sun acts as the light source, and the opaque Earth blocks light to cast a massive shadow on its night-facing side.

**Takeaway:** Earth's rotation - west to east, once per day - is the engine of day and night; the sun merely illuminates the turning half. **In the exam:** in model answers, mark the axis, the spin direction, and which hemisphere faces the sun at the moment shown. **Check yourself:** watch the walkthrough's fan and map the metaphor: which blade is in noon, which in midnight? **Why students miss it:** students send the sun circling the Earth - the flagged ancient error. The apparent daily march is our own spinning viewed from inside. Fix the frame - Earth turns - and day-night, sunrise directions and star trails all resolve correctly.

This misconception completely ignores planetary scale physics, confusing the observer's rotating frame of reference with the actual movement of astronomical bodies.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A point on the Earth's equator travels a distance of approximately 40,075 km during one complete rotation of 24 hours. Calculate the average linear speed of a point on the equator in kilometers per hour (km/h).
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given values: Distance $d = 40,075\text{ km}$, Time $t = 24\text{ hours}$, and what to find: Speed $v$.
- Step 2: Identify the formula: $v = \frac{d}{t}$ and check that units are compatible ($\text{km}$ and $\text{hours}$).
- Step 3: Substitute the values into the formula with units: $v = \frac{40,075\text{ km}}{24\text{ hours}}$.
- Step 4: Perform the computation and track unit cancellation: $v = 1669.79\text{ km/h}$.
- Step 5: Verify the answer is physically reasonable: The Earth is very large, so spinning once a day means equatorial points move extremely fast; $1669.79\text{ km/h}$ is correct.
REASONING: Speed is calculated by dividing total distance by total time taken. Since the Earth completes one equatorial circumference in 24 hours, dividing these values yields the linear speed of rotation.
ANSWER: 1669.79 km/h
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: An Indian communication satellite observes that a specific city on Earth moves through an angle of 75 degrees from east to west relative to the Sun's position. How much time has elapsed for this rotation to occur?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given values: Angular displacement $\theta = 75\text{ degrees}$, rate of rotation $R = 15\text{ degrees/hour}$, and what to find: Time $t$.
- Step 2: Identify the formula: $t = \frac{\theta}{R}$ and check units ($\text{degrees}$ and $\text{degrees/hour}$).
- Step 3: Substitute the values into the formula with units: $t = \frac{75\text{ degrees}}{15\text{ degrees/hour}}$.
- Step 4: Compute and cancel matching units: $t = \frac{75}{15}\text{ hours} = 5\text{ hours}$.
- Step 5: Verify the answer: 5 hours * 15 degrees/hour = 75 degrees, which is correct and physically reasonable.
REASONING: By dividing the total angle rotated by the constant angular speed of the Earth (15 degrees per hour), we find the exact time duration required for that rotation.
ANSWER: 5 hours
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A school science project in New Delhi models the Earth's rotation. The student rotates a 30 cm diameter globe by 180 degrees. If the rotation takes 12 seconds, what is the angular speed of the globe in degrees per second, and how does this compare to the Earth's actual angular speed?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given values for the model: Angle $\theta = 180\text{ degrees}$, Time $t = 12\text{ seconds}$.
- Step 2: Identify the formula for angular speed ($\omega$): $\omega = \frac{\theta}{t}$.
- Step 3: Substitute the values with units: $\omega = \frac{180\text{ degrees}}{12\text{ seconds}}$.
- Step 4: Compute and track units: $\omega = 15\text{ degrees/second}$.
- Step 5: Compare with Earth's actual angular speed ($360^\circ / 86400\text{ s} = 0.00417^\circ/\text{s}$ or $15^\circ/\text{hour}$).
- Step 6: Verify physical reasonableness: The model spins much faster than the real Earth, which is expected for a classroom demonstration.
REASONING: Angular speed measures how many degrees of a circle are traversed per unit of time. Computing this for the model allows direct mathematical comparison with real planetary rotation rates.
ANSWER: 15 degrees/second (model spins much faster than real Earth)
