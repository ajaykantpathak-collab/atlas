## ELI5
Three and a half litres of buttermilk, cups of one-quarter litre each - how many cups? Division by a fraction asks exactly that, and the reciprocal method answers: keep the first fraction, flip the second, multiply. Flip-sense matters - the divisor turns upside down, never the dividend. Convert mixed numbers to improper fractions first, simplify after, and read the answer as a count of cups. Dividing by a number below one lands more cups than you had litres - the counter-intuitive truth of fraction division.

## DEFINITION
The division of fractions using the reciprocal method is a rule where dividing any fraction by a non-zero fraction is performed by multiplying the first dividend fraction by the multiplicative inverse (reciprocal) of the second divisor fraction. Specifically, for any fractions $\frac{a}{b}$ and $\frac{c}{d}$ (where $c, d \neq 0$), $\frac{a}{b} \div \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a}{b} \times \frac{d}{c} = \frac{a \times d}{b \times c}$.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you have $3\frac{1}{2}$ litres of sweet buttermilk prepared for a school sports day party, and you want to pour it into small paper cups that each hold $\frac{1}{4}$ of a litre. How many cups can you fill completely? To solve this, you need to divide the total quantity of buttermilk by the capacity of one cup, which means calculating $3\frac{1}{2} \div \frac{1}{4}$. In Class 6, you learned how to multiply fractions by flipping factors and multiplying numerators and denominators, as well as visualizing fraction division as finding how many groups fit inside a total. Today, we will expand that concept into a universal rule: instead of struggling with division directly, we transform division into multiplication by flipping the second fraction upside down—its reciprocal! The key insight is that dividing by a fraction yields the exact same result as multiplying by its reciprocal.

**Takeaway:** fraction division is keep-change-flip: keep the dividend, flip the divisor, multiply. **In the exam:** convert mixed numbers first, flip only the second fraction, and simplify the product fully. **Check yourself:** pour the buttermilk problem on paper and count how many quarter-litre cups the arithmetic grants. **Why students miss it:** they flip the first fraction out of excitement, or forget the flip and multiply across.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The correct answer is that division asks how many groups of the divisor fit into the dividend. By scaling both the dividend and the divisor by the reciprocal of the divisor, the divisor becomes 1, transforming the division of fractions into a simpler multiplication problem where the denominator becomes 1. ## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Calculate the value of $\frac{7}{8} \div \frac{3}{4}$ and express it in simplest form.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The correct answer is $\frac{7}{6}$ or $1\frac{1}{6}$. Working steps: 1) Keep the first fraction $\frac{7}{8}$. 2) Change the division sign ($\div$) to multiplication ($\times$). 3) Flip the second fraction $\frac{3}{4}$ to its reciprocal $\frac{4}{3}$. 4) Multiply numerators and denominators: $\frac{7 \times 4}{8 \times 3} = \frac{28}{24}$. 5) Simplify by dividing numerator and denominator by 4 to get $\frac{7}{6}$. A wrong answer like $\frac{21}{32}$ reveals that the student multiplied straight across without flipping the second fraction.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often flip the first fraction (dividend) instead of the second fraction (divisor), or they flip both fractions simultaneously when converting a division problem into multiplication. For example, when solving $\frac{3}{5} \div \frac{2}{7}$, a student incorrectly writes $\frac{5}{3} \times \frac{7}{2}$ instead of $\frac{3}{5} \times \frac{7}{2}$.
## ON_FAILURE
Remind the student of the role of each fraction using a simple whole number analogy: in $12 \div 3$, the 12 stays untouched while only the 3 is inverted as $\frac{1}{3}$. Say: "The first number is what you have; the second number tells you how to group it. Therefore, only the second fraction gets turned upside down into its reciprocal!" Have the student physically box the second fraction before writing down the multiplication sign.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A tailor has a ribbon of length $5\frac{1}{4}$ metres. If she cuts it into smaller pieces of length $\frac{3}{4}$ of a metre each, how many pieces of ribbon will she get?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the total length of the ribbon ($5\frac{1}{4}$ m) and the length of each cut piece ($\frac{3}{4}$ m). Write the division expression: $5\frac{1}{4} \div \frac{3}{4}$.
- step 2: Convert the mixed fraction $5\frac{1}{4}$ into an improper fraction: $\frac{(5 \times 4) + 1}{4} = \frac{21}{4}$.
- step 3: Apply the reciprocal method by changing the division sign to multiplication and writing the reciprocal of the divisor $\frac{3}{4}$, which is $\frac{4}{3}$. The expression becomes $\frac{21}{4} \times \frac{4}{3}$.
- step 4: Multiply numerators and denominators while cross-simplifying: $\frac{21 \times 4}{4 \times 3} = \frac{84}{12}$.
- step 5: Divide 84 by 12 to get the final whole number result: $7$ pieces of ribbon.
REASONING: Converting mixed numbers to improper fractions ensures uniformity before division. Transforming the division problem into a multiplication problem using the reciprocal rule allows us to simplify common factors across numerators and denominators easily.
ANSWER: 7 pieces
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Evaluate the expression $\frac{14}{15} \div \frac{7}{10}$ and reduce the answer to its simplest mixed fraction form.
STEPS:
- step 1: Write down the given division problem: $\frac{14}{15} \div \frac{7}{10}$.
- step 2: Identify the divisor fraction ($\frac{7}{10}$) and write its reciprocal ($\frac{10}{7}$).
- step 3: Rewrite the expression as multiplication: $\frac{14}{15} \times \frac{10}{7}$.
- step 4: Simplify before multiplying by canceling common factors: 14 and 7 share a factor of 7 ($14 \div 7 = 2$, $7 \div 7 = 1$), and 10 and 15 share a factor of 5 ($10 \div 5 = 2$, $15 \div 5 = 3$). The expression simplifies to $\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{2}{1}$.
- step 5: Multiply the remaining numbers to get the improper fraction $\frac{4}{3}$, which converts to the mixed fraction $1\frac{1}{3}$.
REASONING: Pre-canceling common factors in numerators and denominators simplifies large numbers and prevents calculation errors, yielding a clean improper fraction that is easily converted to a mixed number.
ANSWER: $1\frac{1}{3}$
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Rahul spends $2\frac{1}{2}$ hours studying across a weekend. If each study session lasts $\frac{3}{4}$ of an hour, how many full sessions did he complete, and what fraction of an hour is left over?
STEPS:
- step 1: Express total study time as $2\frac{1}{2}$ hours and session length as $\frac{3}{4}$ hour. Formulate the division expression: $2\frac{1}{2} \div \frac{3}{4}$.
- step 2: Convert the mixed fraction $2\frac{1}{2}$ into an improper fraction: $\frac{5}{2}$.
- step 3: Apply the reciprocal method: $\frac{5}{2} \div \frac{3}{4} = \frac{5}{2} \times \frac{4}{3}$.
- step 4: Multiply and simplify: $\frac{5 \times 4}{2 \times 3} = \frac{20}{6} = \frac{10}{3} = 3\frac{1}{3}$ sessions.
- step 5: Interpret the mixed result: Rahul completed 3 full sessions, and the remaining fraction represents $\frac{1}{3}$ of a session. Since one session is $\frac{3}{4}$ of an hour, the leftover time in minutes is $\frac{1}{3} \times \frac{3}{4} = \frac{1}{4}$ of an hour (or 15 minutes).
REASONING: Real-world problems involving mixed numbers require converting quotients into meaningful units. The whole number part represents complete sessions, while the fractional remainder can be converted back into standard time units.
ANSWER: 3 full sessions (with $\frac{1}{4}$ hour leftover)
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A container holds $7\frac{1}{2}$ litres of juice. A chef wants to pour equal amounts into small glasses, each holding $\frac{3}{8}$ of a litre. 
(a) How many glasses can be filled completely? 
(b) If each glass is sold for ₹25, what is the total revenue generated from all filled glasses?
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to multi-step mathematical expressions involving fraction division and financial calculation.
STEPS:
- step 1: Convert total juice volume ($7\frac{1}{2}$) and glass capacity ($\frac{3}{8}$) into improper fractions: $\frac{15}{2}$ and $\frac{3}{8}$.
- step 2: Set up and execute the division using the reciprocal method: $\frac{15}{2} \div \frac{3}{8} = \frac{15}{2} \times \frac{8}{3}$.
- step 3: Simplify and calculate: $\frac{15 \times 8}{2 \times 3} = \frac{120}{6} = 20$ glasses filled.
- step 4: Multiply the number of filled glasses by the price per glass to find revenue: $20 \times 25 = ₹500$.
ANSWER: (a) 20 glasses; (b) ₹500 total revenue.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Two students, Anita and Vikram, are solving the equation $\frac{9}{10} \div x = \frac{3}{5}$. 
Anita says: "To find $x$, I just multiply $\frac{9}{10}$ by $\frac{3}{5}$." 
Vikram says: "To find $x$, I rewrite it as $x = \frac{9}{10} \div \frac{3}{5}$ and then use the reciprocal method." 
Analyze both statements, identify who is correct mathematically, explain why the other person's method fails, and calculate the correct value of $x$.
COMPETENCY: Error identification, mathematical justification, and algebraic reasoning with fraction division relationships.
STEPS:
- step 1: Analyze the division equation structure: $\text{Dividend} \div \text{Divisor} = \text{Quotient}$, which is $\frac{9}{10} \div x = \frac{3}{5}$.
- step 2: Evaluate Vikram's approach: Using the relationship between multiplication and division, $x = \frac{9}{10} \div \frac{3}{5}$. Applying the reciprocal method gives $x = \frac{9}{10} \times \frac{5}{3} = \frac{45}{30} = \frac{3}{2}$. Vikram is correct.
- step 3: Evaluate Anita's approach: Anita multiplied $\frac{9}{10} \times \frac{3}{5} = \frac{27}{50}$. Testing her answer: $\frac{9}{10} \div \frac{27}{50} = \frac{9}{10} \times \frac{50}{27} = \frac{5}{3} \neq \frac{3}{5}$. Anita's method fails because she treated an unknown divisor problem as a direct multiplication problem without applying inverse operations.
- step 4: State the final validated value of $x$.
ANSWER: Vikram is correct. Anita's method is incorrect because finding an unknown divisor requires dividing the dividend by the quotient. The correct value of $x$ is $\frac{3}{2}$ (or $1\frac{1}{2}$).
DIFFICULTY: hard
