## ELI5
Water dissolves salt, sugar, and a crowd of other substances — more than any other liquid can claim — and the reason is its lopsided build. Each water molecule carries a slightly positive end and a slightly negative end, like a tiny magnet with two poles. These poles surround salt's ions and sugar's molecules, prying them apart and escorting them into the mix. Oil's molecules carry no such charges, so water simply cannot get a grip — hence the salad-dressing separation that never mixes.

## DEFINITION
Water is called a universal solvent because its polar molecular structure allows it to dissolve a wider variety of substances (ionic compounds, polar covalent molecules, and many gases) than any other liquid, forming homogeneous mixtures called aqueous solutions.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY

## WALKTHROUGH
Begin by showing a glass of tap water and adding a spoon of salt (NaCl) and a spoon of sugar (sucrose) – both disappear. Ask: "What happens at the particle level?" Connect to Class 7 learning about acids/bases dissolving in water (c7_physics_ch07_001). Then introduce water's bent shape and uneven electron sharing creating partial positive (H) and partial negative (O) ends. Demonstrate with a simple diagram how these poles attract Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions, pulling them apart. Finally, have students test three kitchen substances (salt, oil, chalk powder) in water and record observations to classify them as soluble/insoluble.

Takeaway: water is the universal solvent because its polar molecules — partial negative at the oxygen end, partial positive at the hydrogen ends — surround and separate ionic and polar solutes; non-polar substances like oil resist and float apart. Exam format: expect 'why does salt dissolve in water?' via the pole-attraction picture, and household predictions (sugar yes, oil no, chalk no). Verification beat: classify each substance by polarity before predicting — charged or polar dissolves, neutral and non-polar does not. Why students miss it: they say 'water dissolves everything' — the title overclaims; it dissolves the widest variety, and oil is the standing counterexample on every kitchen shelf. Draw the two-pole diagram once in your answer and the whole sodium-chloride separation explains itself.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the scientific reason water is called a "universal solvent"?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Water's polar covalent bonds give it partial positive charges on hydrogen atoms and a partial negative charge on oxygen, allowing it to attract and surround both positive and negative ions as well as other polar molecules. A wrong answer like "water is everywhere" or "water dissolves everything" reveals the student confuses the term "universal" (meaning wide range) with "all substances" and misses the molecular polarity explanation.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why does table salt (NaCl) dissolve in water but cooking oil does not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Salt is ionic – water's polar molecules pull Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions apart and surround them (hydration). Oil is non‑polar; its molecules have no charge separation, so water's poles cannot attract them, and oil forms a separate layer. If a student says "salt is solid, oil is liquid" they are focusing on physical state instead of molecular polarity.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A student adds 20 g of copper sulfate crystals to 100 mL of water at 25°C and stirs. After 5 minutes, 5 g of crystals remain at the bottom. Explain what this observation tells you about the solubility of copper sulfate in water at that temperature.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The solution has reached saturation: 15 g dissolved in 100 mL, so the solubility is 15 g/100 mL at 25°C. The remaining 5 g cannot dissolve because the water molecules are already fully occupied hydrating the dissolved ions. A wrong answer like "the student didn't stir enough" shows confusion between rate of dissolving (kinetics) and maximum amount that can dissolve (equilibrium solubility).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare how water dissolves oxygen gas (O₂) from the air versus how it dissolves hydrogen chloride gas (HCl). What property of water allows both, and what is the key difference in the interaction?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Water's polarity allows it to dissolve both. For O₂ (non‑polar), weak temporary dipoles induce slight attraction – physical dissolution. For HCl (polar covalent), water's poles strongly attract H⁺ and Cl⁻, causing ionization into H₃O⁺ and Cl⁻ – chemical reaction. A student who says "both just mix" misses the distinction between physical dissolution and ionization.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A classmate claims: "Since water is a universal solvent, it should dissolve all the plastic waste in the oceans." Evaluate this statement and explain why it is incorrect.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The claim is false. "Universal solvent" means water dissolves *many* substances, not *all*. Plastics are long non‑polar polymer chains; water's polar molecules cannot overcome the strong covalent bonds within the polymer or the hydrophobic interactions between chains. A student who agrees reveals the misconception that "universal" means "everything".
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple home experiment to test whether three unknown white powders (A, B, C) are ionic, polar covalent, or non‑polar based only on their behaviour in water. List the steps and what you would observe for each type.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Good answer: 1) Add equal amounts of each powder to separate cups of water. 2) Stir for 2 minutes. 3) Observe: Powder that disappears completely (forms clear solution) and conducts electricity (test with a simple LED circuit) → ionic. Powder that disappears but solution does not conduct → polar covalent (e.g., sugar). Powder that does not dissolve (floats/sinks) → non‑polar. A poor answer that only says "see which dissolves" misses the conductivity test to distinguish ionic from polar covalent.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think "universal solvent" means water dissolves *every* substance. They cite examples like salt and sugar but cannot explain why water fails to dissolve oil, wax, or plastic. The exact wrong reasoning: "Water is called universal, so it should dissolve anything." They confuse the colloquial meaning of "universal" (works for everything) with the scientific meaning (dissolves a wider range than any other liquid).
## ON_FAILURE
1. Show a demo: Add oil to water – it forms a layer. Ask: "If water dissolves everything, why doesn't the oil disappear?" 2. Have students draw water molecules (δ+ H, δ– O) and oil molecules (long C–H chains with no charge separation). 3. Guide them to see that water's poles have nothing to attract in oil. 4. Connect to the definition: "Universal" = "more than any other liquid", not "all". 5. Assign a home hunt: List 5 things that dissolve in water and 5 that don't; bring results to class.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A farmer wants to prepare a pesticide solution. He has 500 mL of water at 25°C. The pesticide label says its solubility in water is 12 g per 100 mL at 25°C. He needs to dissolve 70 g of pesticide. Will all the pesticide dissolve? Show your reasoning with calculations.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given quantities: Volume of water = 500 mL, Solubility = 12 g/100 mL, Mass of pesticide to dissolve = 70 g.
- Step 2: Calculate maximum mass that can dissolve in 500 mL: (12 g/100 mL) × 500 mL = 60 g.
- Step 3: Compare required mass (70 g) with maximum dissolvable mass (60 g). 70 g > 60 g.
- Step 4: Conclude that only 60 g will dissolve; 10 g will remain undissolved at the bottom.
- Step 5: Verify reasonableness: 500 mL is 5 times 100 mL, so 5 × 12 g = 60 g. The answer is physically plausible.
REASONING: This problem uses the concept of solubility (a quantitative measure of water's solvent capacity) to predict whether a given amount of solute will fully dissolve. The steps involve proportional reasoning and comparison, directly applying the idea that water's ability to dissolve has a limit at a given temperature.
ANSWER: No, only 60 g will dissolve; 10 g will remain undissolved.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: In a school lab, students test three gases for solubility in water: oxygen (O₂), carbon dioxide (CO₂), and ammonia (NH₃). They bubble each gas through 100 mL of water at 20°C for 10 minutes. The measured masses dissolved are: O₂ = 0.004 g, CO₂ = 0.145 g, NH₃ = 52 g. Explain these huge differences using water's molecular properties.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Note the masses dissolved per 100 mL water: O₂ (non-polar) = 0.004 g, CO₂ (polar, reacts slightly) = 0.145 g, NH₃ (polar, hydrogen bonds) = 52 g.
- Step 2: Recall water is polar with δ+ H and δ– O, and can form hydrogen bonds.
- Step 3: Analyse O₂: Non-polar molecules only experience weak induced dipole interactions → very low solubility.
- Step 4: Analyse CO₂: Linear but polar bonds; reacts with water to form carbonic acid (H₂CO₃) → moderate solubility.
- Step 5: Analyse NH₃: Polar, pyramidal shape with lone pair; forms strong hydrogen bonds with water and reacts to form NH₄⁺ and OH⁻ → very high solubility.
- Step 6: Conclude that water's polarity and hydrogen bonding ability explain the trend: non-polar < polar/reactive < polar/hydrogen-bonding/reactive.
REASONING: The example connects the macroscopic observation (mass dissolved) to molecular-level interactions (polarity, hydrogen bonding, chemical reaction). It shows that "universal solvent" does not mean equal solubility for all gases; the degree depends on specific solute-water interactions.
ANSWER: O₂ dissolves poorly due to only weak induced dipoles; CO₂ dissolves moderately due to polarity and reaction forming carbonic acid; NH₃ dissolves extremely well due to hydrogen bonding and ionization.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A student prepares two solutions: Solution A – 10 g of sodium chloride (NaCl) in 100 mL water; Solution B – 10 g of glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) in 100 mL water. Both are stirred at 25°C until no more solid dissolves. The student then tests electrical conductivity of each solution using a battery, wires, and an LED. Predict and explain the observation for each solution.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the nature of solutes: NaCl is ionic (Na⁺ Cl⁻), glucose is polar covalent (molecular).
- Step 2: Recall water's polarity: it hydrates ions by surrounding Na⁺ with δ– O and Cl⁻ with δ+ H, pulling them apart into mobile ions.
- Step 3: For glucose, water forms hydrogen bonds with –OH groups, dispersing molecules but they remain neutral.
- Step 4: Conductivity requires mobile charged particles. Solution A has free Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions → conducts electricity (LED lights).
- Step 5: Solution B has only neutral glucose molecules → no mobile ions → does not conduct (LED stays off).
- Step 6: Connect to water's universal solvent property: it dissolves both, but the resulting solutions have different electrical properties because of how water interacts with ionic vs. molecular solutes.
REASONING: This problem combines water's solvent properties (dissolving both ionic and polar covalent substances) with a testable consequence (conductivity). It requires understanding that dissolving does not always produce ions; water's interaction with the solute determines whether the solution conducts.
ANSWER: Solution A (NaCl) conducts electricity – LED lights up. Solution B (glucose) does not conduct – LED stays off.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You are given two clear, colourless liquids: Liquid X and Liquid Y. You know one is pure water and the other is a solution of salt in water. Without tasting, how can you identify which is which using only a battery, two wires, and an LED? Explain the principle.
COMPETENCY: Interpreting physical situations using formulas
STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand that pure water has very few ions (only H⁺ and OH⁻ from autoionization) and conducts electricity poorly.
- Step 2: Salt solution contains mobile Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions from dissolved NaCl, so it conducts well.
- Step 3: Set up a simple circuit: battery → wire → LED → wire → beaker with liquid (electrodes dipped in). Complete the circuit through the liquid.
- Step 4: Test each liquid. The one that lights the LED brightly is the salt solution; the one that does not light (or very dim) is pure water.
- Step 5: Principle: Water's polarity allows it to dissolve ionic compounds into mobile ions, enabling electrical conduction.
ANSWER: The liquid that completes the circuit and lights the LED is the salt solution. Pure water will not light the LED (or only very dimly) because it lacks sufficient mobile ions.
DIFFICULTY: easy
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A coastal power plant uses seawater for cooling. Over time, the pipes get coated with a white solid (scale) that reduces efficiency. The scale is mainly calcium carbonate (CaCO₃). Explain why CaCO₃ deposits form when seawater is heated, even though CaCO₃ is considered "sparingly soluble" in cold water. Relate your answer to water's solvent properties and the effect of temperature on gas solubility.
COMPETENCY: Predicting outcomes from changing variables
STEPS:
- Step 1: Recognize that seawater contains dissolved Ca²⁺ ions and bicarbonate ions (HCO₃⁻) from dissolved CO₂.
- Step 2: In cold water, CO₂ is fairly soluble; equilibrium: Ca²⁺ + 2HCO₃⁻ ⇌ CaCO₃(s) + CO₂(aq) + H₂O. The dissolved CO₂ keeps the equilibrium to the left (dissolved).
- Step 3: When water is heated, solubility of gases (including CO₂) decreases – CO₂ bubbles out.
- Step 4: Loss of CO₂ shifts equilibrium to the right, precipitating solid CaCO₃ (scale).
- Step 5: Water's role: as a universal solvent, it holds Ca²⁺ and HCO₃⁻ in solution; its reduced gas-holding capacity at higher temperature drives the precipitation.
ANSWER: Heating reduces CO₂ solubility; CO₂ escapes, shifting the equilibrium Ca²⁺ + 2HCO₃⁻ ⇌ CaCO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O to the right, depositing CaCO₃ scale. Water's solvent properties keep ions in solution until the gas leaves.
DIFFICULTY: hard
