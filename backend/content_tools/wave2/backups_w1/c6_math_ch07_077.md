## ELI5
Flat drawings live on paper; solid things own space. A box, an ice-cream cone, a ball - each has length, width and depth, which is why your hand can grip them. Their anatomy has three words: flat faces, straight edges where faces meet, and sharp corners called vertices. Count them on your geometry box and the whole family - cubes, cones, cylinders, spheres - opens up.

## DEFINITION
Three-dimensional (3D) shapes are solid objects that occupy space and possess three dimensions: length, width, and depth (or height). Unlike flat 2D shapes, they have thickness, allowing us to interact with them as physical objects.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are holding your geometry box or an ice cream cone; have you ever noticed why these objects feel solid while a drawing on your notebook looks flat? To understand 3D shapes, start by touching the corners (vertices), the straight lines (edges), and the flat sides (faces) of a cube-shaped box. Just like a building is made of rooms, a 3D shape is built from these components. Begin by observing common objects around you—a ball is a sphere, a soda can is a cylinder, and a brick is a cuboid. The key insight is that while 2D shapes have only area, 3D shapes have 'depth,' which allows them to contain things.

**Takeaway:** 3D shapes occupy space with length, width and depth, and are catalogued by counting faces, edges and vertices. **In the exam:** in shape questions, hold the object in mind, then tally faces, edges and vertices one family at a time. **Check yourself:** run a finger over the walkthrough's box - faces first, then the edges where two faces shake hands, then the corner dots. **Why students miss it:** students count curved surfaces as flat faces - the geometry box is a no to that; only flat faces meet at true edges and corners. Rehearse the exam move once more - solid, faces, edges, corners - and the answer arranges itself in exam-ready order with nothing left to chance.

No, by definition, a pyramid has a polygon base (a shape with straight edges) and triangular faces that meet at a point. If the base were circular, it would be a cone, not a pyramid.

**A wrong answer (rounding a pyramid's base) reveals the student broke the family rule - pyramids are built on straight-edged bases; round ones belong to the cone.**

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often confuse the 'face' of a 3D shape with its 'edge,' or they forget to count the 'hidden' faces and edges that are not visible from a single perspective. For example, when looking at a cube, a student might only count 3 faces because they only see the front, top, and side, failing to realize a solid object has 6 faces in total.
## ON_FAILURE
To fix this, use a physical object like a dice or a soap box. Ask the student to place a small sticker on every face they can identify, then turn the object over to find the ones they missed. Explain that an edge is specifically the line segment where two flat faces meet, and a vertex is the corner point where edges converge. Physically touching these parts is essential to bridge the gap between a 2D drawing and a 3D reality.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A rectangular biscuit packet (cuboid) has a length of 20 cm, a width of 10 cm, and a height of 5 cm. How many faces, edges, and vertices does this object have?
STEPS:
- Identify the shape: The packet is a cuboid.
- Recall properties: A cuboid has 6 rectangular faces.
- Count edges: It has 12 edges (4 on top, 4 on bottom, 4 connecting them).
- Count vertices: It has 8 corners (4 on top, 4 on bottom).
- Interpretation: The dimensions do not change the number of components.
REASONING: Since it is a cuboid, it follows the geometric definition of having 6 faces, 12 edges, and 8 vertices regardless of its size.
ANSWER: 6 faces, 12 edges, 8 vertices.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: An ice cream cone has a circular base and a curved surface. If you were to cut it exactly in half from the tip to the center of the base, what new 2D shapes would you see on the cut surface?
STEPS:
- Identify the shape: It is a cone.
- Visualize the structure: The vertical cut passes through the vertex and the center of the circular base.
- Identify the cross-section: The cut reveals a triangle.
- Verify: The base of the triangle is the diameter of the circular base, and the height is the height of the cone.
REASONING: Slicing a 3D shape reveals 2D cross-sections; a cone's vertical cross-section through the center creates a triangle.
ANSWER: A triangle.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A student has a wooden pyramid with a square base. If they count the vertices and edges, what will they find?
STEPS:
- Identify the base: Square base has 4 vertices and 4 edges.
- Identify the apex: There is 1 additional vertex at the top.
- Count total vertices: 4 (base) + 1 (apex) = 5 vertices.
- Count total edges: 4 (base edges) + 4 (slanting edges connecting base to apex) = 8 edges.
- Count faces: 1 (square base) + 4 (triangular sides) = 5 faces.
REASONING: By breaking the pyramid into its base and its apex, we can systematically count the connections.
ANSWER: 5 vertices, 8 edges, 5 faces.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If you place a cube and a sphere on a table, which one would roll if you pushed it and why? Explain this using the concepts of faces and curved surfaces.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- Identify the shape properties: A cube has 6 flat faces; a sphere has a continuous curved surface.
- Apply the concept of stability: Flat faces allow a cube to rest securely; a curved surface has no flat face to provide stability.
- Formulate the explanation: The sphere will roll because it lacks flat faces to stop the rotation.
ANSWER: The sphere will roll because it has a curved surface and no flat faces, whereas the cube has 6 flat faces that allow it to remain stationary.
DIFFICULTY: medium
