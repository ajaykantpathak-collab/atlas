## ELI5
A class survey of 40 students picks favourite sports from cricket to kabaddi, and the principal wants the verdict at a glance. Bar graphs and pictographs are the translators: uniform-width bars whose heights carry the numbers, or rows of symbols where a legend declares each picture's worth - one smiley might stand for four students. Choose a scale that fits the paper, label both axes honestly, and keep every bar equally wide so the picture argues fairly.

## DEFINITION
Visualising data through bar graphs and pictographs involves representing numerical information graphically using rectangular bars of uniform width or symbols/icons where each symbol represents a specific numerical quantity to enable easy comparison and analysis.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL

## MASTERY_CRITERIA

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers the construction and basic interpretation of single bar graphs and pictographs with scaled symbols. Does not cover double bar graphs, histograms, or advanced statistical plots like pie charts.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you conducted a survey in your class of 40 students asking for their favourite sports (Cricket, Football, Badminton, Kabaddi), and you have a list of tally marks. How can you show this information to the principal or your classmates so they can see which sport is the most popular in a single glance? We begin by choosing an appropriate scale—such as letting 1 symbol represent 5 students for a pictograph, or letting 1 centimetre represent 5 units on the vertical axis of a bar graph. Building on our prior knowledge of organising data into frequency tables, we translate those numbers into visual lengths or picture counts. The key insight is that a graph transforms numbers into spatial dimensions, making comparisons immediate and intuitive.

**Takeaway:** bar graphs and pictographs trade numbers for shapes the eye can compare in one glance. **In the exam:** choose a scale that fits the page, label both axes, and let the legend declare each symbol's worth. **Check yourself:** convert the class sports survey into bars with a scale of one unit per ten students and label everything. **Why students miss it:** students draw bars of different widths, or drop the legend and let every smiley silently stand for one. Unequal bars exaggerate, missing keys mislead - the graph must argue fairly or not at all.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Why is it important that all bars in a bar graph have equal widths and equal spaces between them?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Equal widths and spaces ensure that the visual comparison is fair and unbiased; if one bar is wider, it might visually exaggerate its quantity even if its height is the same. A wrong answer suggests it is purely for decoration or aesthetics rather than accurate mathematical comparison.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, it is misleading because starting the axis at a non-zero value distorts visual proportions, making differences look much larger or smaller than they actually are. A wrong answer claims it is fine as long as the label says 50, missing the psychological and visual distortion of the baseline.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a scale for a pictograph where data categories have values of 120, 180, 240, and 300 students. State your chosen symbol and what it represents.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good answer will choose a scale factor that divides all numbers cleanly, such as choosing 1 star symbol to represent 60 students. Then 120 = 2 stars, 180 = 3 stars, 240 = 4 stars, and 300 = 5 stars. A poor answer chooses an awkward scale like 1 symbol = 45 students, which results in fractions of symbols.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often fail to maintain a consistent scale or forget to calculate the value of partial symbols in a pictograph. For instance, if one apple symbol represents 10 kilograms of grain, and a student sees half an apple, they might count it as 5 kg without checking if the symbol can be cleanly halved, or they might completely ignore partial symbols when totalling. Another common error is mixing up the horizontal and vertical axes when plotting categories versus frequencies.
## ON_FAILURE
Remind the student to always check the scale box first before looking at any bars or pictures. Write down the scaling rule explicitly as an equation: `1 symbol = [X] units`, then for any given count, calculate `Total Value = Number of Symbols × X`. When drawing, check that every increment on the axis increases by the exact same amount.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A school library recorded the number of story books borrowed over 4 days: Monday = 45, Tuesday = 30, Wednesday = 60, Thursday = 75. Construct a bar graph choosing a suitable scale where 1 cm represents 15 books. Find the height of each bar.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the given frequencies: Monday ($45$), Tuesday ($30$), Wednesday ($60$), Thursday ($75$).
- Step 2: Identify the given scale factor: $1\text{ cm} = 15\text{ books}$.
- Step 3: Set up the formula for bar height: $\text{Bar Height} = \frac{\text{Frequency}}{\text{Scale Factor}}$.
- Step 4: Calculate the height for each day:
  - Monday: $\frac{45}{15} = 3\text{ cm}$
  - Tuesday: $\frac{30}{15} = 2\text{ cm}$
  - Wednesday: $\frac{60}{15} = 4\text{ cm}$
  - Thursday: $\frac{75}{15} = 5\text{ cm}$
- Step 5: Verify by checking that all heights fit standard graph paper and represent the data proportionally.
REASONING: Dividing each frequency by the scale factor converts raw numbers into physical lengths on graph paper while maintaining equal proportions.
ANSWER: Heights are: Monday = 3 cm, Tuesday = 2 cm, Wednesday = 4 cm, Thursday = 5 cm.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: In a pictograph representing the sale of notebooks in a stationery shop, one notebook symbol ($\bigodot$) represents 25 notebooks. If the total sales for Friday was 175 notebooks, how many symbols should be drawn?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the total frequency for Friday: $175\text{ notebooks}$.
- Step 2: Identify the value assigned to one symbol: $1\text{ symbol} = 25\text{ notebooks}$.
- Step 3: Set up the division equation to find the number of symbols: $\text{Number of Symbols} = \frac{\text{Total Frequency}}{\text{Value per Symbol}}$.
- Step 4: Substitute the values and compute: $\text{Number of Symbols} = \frac{175}{25} = 7$.
- Step 5: Verify the calculation by multiplying back: $7 \times 25 = 175$, which matches the given frequency.
REASONING: Dividing the total frequency by the value of a single icon tells us how many complete symbols are needed to represent the dataset accurately.
ANSWER: 7 notebook symbols ($\bigodot$).
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A survey of 120 students shows their preferred mode of transport to school: Bus = 50, Bicycle = 30, Walking = 40. You need to draw a bar graph on an axis where maximum height is 6 cm. Determine the most efficient scale, and calculate the exact height of each bar.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the maximum frequency in the data set, which is $50$ (Bus).
- Step 2: Determine the maximum available height on graph paper, which is $6\text{ cm}$.
- Step 3: Calculate a minimum scale factor by dividing max frequency by max height: $\frac{50}{6} \approx 8.33$. To make drawing easy, choose a clean round number larger than $8.33$, such as $1\text{ cm} = 10\text{ students}$.
- Step 4: Compute the bar height for each category using the chosen scale of $10\text{ students/cm}$:
  - Bus: $\frac{50}{10} = 5.0\text{ cm}$
  - Bicycle: $\frac{30}{10} = 3.0\text{ cm}$
  - Walking: $\frac{40}{10} = 4.0\text{ cm}$
- Step 5: Verify that the maximum bar height ($5.0\text{ cm}$) fits within the $6\text{ cm}$ limit and all calculations are exact.
REASONING: Selecting a clean scale factor based on the maximum data value ensures that the graph fits neatly on the page while remaining easy to read and plot.
ANSWER: Scale: 1 cm = 10 students; Heights: Bus = 5 cm, Bicycle = 3 cm, Walking = 4 cm.

- Step 1: Compare the number of symbols drawn in each approach ($15$ symbols vs $6$ symbols).
- Step 2: Analyse Rahul's choice ($1\text{ symbol} = 10\text{ cars}$): requires counting $15$ symbols, which takes more space but allows representing smaller sub-quantities easily.
- Step 3: Analyse Priya's choice ($1\text{ symbol} = 25\text{ cars}$): requires counting only $6$ symbols, which is compact and quick to scan, but harder if data values do not end in multiples of $25$.

ANSWER: Rahul's scale provides higher visual granularity and makes it easier to represent smaller numbers without fractions, but takes up more space. Priya's scale is very compact and fast to draw, but loses fine detail if numbers are not neat multiples of 25.
