## ELI5
Ratios come in two camera angles. Part-to-part zooms between two groups: apples versus oranges in the basket. Part-to-whole pulls back: apples against the entire basket, every fruit counted. Same basket, two shots - and the whole is just the parts added up. Choose the lens the question asks for, because comparing a slice to the whole plate is a different sentence entirely.

## DEFINITION
A part-to-part ratio compares two specific groups or quantities within a whole, while a part-to-whole ratio compares one specific group to the entire collection of all groups combined.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify whether a ratio relationship describes a "part versus part" or "part versus total" scenario and correctly calculate both types given a set of values.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the distinction between part-to-part and part-to-whole relationships using two quantities. It does not cover compound ratios, continued ratios (three or more quantities), or complex algebraic proportions.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you have a fruit basket with 3 apples and 5 oranges. If I ask you how many apples there are compared to oranges, you are looking at a "part-to-part" relationship: 3:5. But if I ask how many apples there are compared to the *entire* basket of 8 fruits, you are looking at a "part-to-whole" relationship: 3:8. To begin, always count your groups separately first, then add them together to find the "whole." Remember, the "whole" is the total number of all items combined, while the "parts" are the individual categories inside.

**Takeaway:** part-to-part ratios compare two groups inside a whole; part-to-whole ratios compare one group against everything combined. **In the exam:** in ratio questions, read the wording first - versus a group or versus the total - and build the pair to match. **Check yourself:** re-shoot the walkthrough's fruit basket once zoomed and once wide, and name both ratios aloud. **Why students miss it:** students reuse the part pair for whole questions - the walkthrough's wide shot is a no to that; the whole demands the parts be added first, then the single group compared against the combined total. Rehearse the exam move once more - read, add, compare - and the answer arranges itself in exam-ready order with nothing left to chance.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
If a box contains 4 red pens and 6 blue pens, what is the total number of pens in the box?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The total is 10 pens. A student who says "4" or "6" is confusing a single part for the whole, ignoring the fact that the total includes all items combined.

**A wrong answer (reporting one part as the total) reveals the student stopped at a single group - the whole is every pen in the box combined.**

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
In a class, the ratio of boys to girls is 2:3. Does this ratio tell you the total number of students in the class? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
No, it does not. This is a part-to-part ratio; it only tells us the relationship between the two groups, not the actual total number of students, which could be 5, 10, or 50.

**A wrong answer (reading headcounts from a ratio) reveals the student asked the wrong lens - a part-to-part pair gives the relationship, never the actual size of the class.**

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A basket has 2 mangoes and 8 bananas. What is the ratio of mangoes to the total number of fruits?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The ratio is 2:10, which simplifies to 1:5. A student who writes 2:8 is mistakenly using a part-to-part ratio instead of a part-to-whole ratio.

**A wrong answer (recycling the part pair) reveals the student kept the zoom lens - the whole adds every fruit before the comparison is made.**

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
If the ratio of dogs to cats in a shelter is 5:2, can you determine the ratio of cats to total animals? Explain your logic.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Yes, the ratio is 2:7. You take the "part" (cats = 2) and compare it to the "whole" (dogs + cats = 5 + 2 = 7); a student failing this likely forgot to add the parts to find the denominator.

**A wrong answer (forgetting to assemble the whole) reveals the student missed the add-up step - the denominator of a part-to-whole ratio is the parts combined.**

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it possible for the sum of two part-to-whole ratios (e.g., girls/total + boys/total) to be greater than 1? Explain.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, the sum must equal 1 (or the whole). If a student thinks it can be greater, they are likely adding parts incorrectly or losing track of the total value.

**A wrong answer (letting the shares overflow) reveals the student broke the whole - both part-to-whole shares together must account for exactly everything.**

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a scenario with two types of marbles where the part-to-whole ratio is 3:10. How many marbles of each type must you have?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The student must identify that 3 is the part and 10 is the total, meaning the other part must be 7. An answer like "3 blue and 7 red" is correct; an answer like "3 blue and 10 red" shows a failure to distinguish between the part and the whole.

**A wrong answer (making the whole a part) reveals the student misplaced the total - the demanded figure is the entire collection, so the second group is whatever remains.**

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often mistake part-to-part for part-to-whole by using the second part of the ratio as the denominator. For example, if given 5 apples and 10 oranges, they might say the ratio of apples to total fruit is 5:10 instead of 5:15. This happens because they see two numbers and automatically put them in a fraction without summing them to find the "whole."
## ON_FAILURE
If a student makes this error, have them physically draw the items or list them out. Ask them, "Is the total number of items just the oranges, or is it the apples AND the oranges combined?" Once they identify the total (15), guide them to write the ratio as "Part / Total" (5/15) rather than "Part / Other Part."

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A flower shop has 12 roses and 18 lilies. What is the ratio of roses to lilies (part-to-part), and the ratio of roses to the total number of flowers (part-to-whole)?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the groups: Roses = 12, Lilies = 18.
- Step 2: Find the total: 12 + 18 = 30.
- Step 3: Write part-to-part: 12:18. Simplify by dividing by 6: 2:3.
- Step 4: Write part-to-whole: 12:30. Simplify by dividing by 6: 2:5.
- Step 5: Verify: Do these ratios make sense? 2 parts roses and 3 parts lilies = 5 parts total. 2 parts roses to 5 total is correct.
REASONING: By simplifying the ratios, we make them easier to compare. The total must be calculated before finding the part-to-whole relationship.
ANSWER: Part-to-part is 2:3; Part-to-whole is 2:5.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: In a cricket match, a player hits 8 fours and 2 sixes. What is the ratio of sixes to the total boundaries hit?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify parts: Fours = 8, Sixes = 2.
- Step 2: Calculate total boundaries: 8 + 2 = 10.
- Step 3: Set up part-to-whole ratio: 2 (sixes) to 10 (total).
- Step 4: Simplify the fraction: 2/10 = 1/5.
- Step 5: Interpret: For every 5 boundaries, 1 is a six.
REASONING: The question specifically asked for sixes to the total, not sixes to fours. The total is the sum of all boundaries.
ANSWER: 1:5.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A bag contains 20 marbles. 5 are green and the rest are blue. Find the ratio of blue marbles to the total marbles.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given: Total = 20, Green = 5.
- Step 2: Calculate missing part (Blue): 20 - 5 = 15.
- Step 3: Define ratio: Blue marbles (15) to Total marbles (20).
- Step 4: Simplify: 15:20 = 3:4.
- Step 5: Check: If 3/4 are blue, then 1/4 must be green. 1/4 of 20 is 5. Matches!
REASONING: Since the total was provided, we subtract one part to find the other, then compare that part to the total given.
ANSWER: 3:4.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You have a mix of 10 red beads and 20 white beads. Your friend says the ratio of red beads to white beads is 1:3. Is your friend correct? Why or why not?
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Look at the numbers: Red = 10, White = 20.
- Step 2: Write the actual ratio: 10:20.
- Step 3: Simplify: 1:2.
- Step 4: Compare to friend's answer (1:3).
ANSWER: The friend is incorrect. The ratio of red to white is 10:20, which simplifies to 1:2. The friend likely added the red to the white to get 30 as a denominator, which would be correct for a part-to-whole ratio (10:30 = 1:3), but incorrect for a part-to-part ratio.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: In a library, there are 40 science books and 60 history books. If you take away 10 science books, how does the ratio of science books to the total number of books change?
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate initial state: 40 sci, 60 hist, 100 total. Ratio 40:100 = 2:5.
- Step 2: Update state after removal: 30 sci, 60 hist.
- Step 3: Calculate new total: 30 + 60 = 90.
- Step 4: Calculate new ratio: 30:90 = 1:3.
- Step 5: Compare: 2/5 (0.4) vs 1/3 (0.33).
ANSWER: The ratio changes from 2:5 to 1:3. By removing science books, the science books represent a smaller "part" of the "whole" library.
DIFFICULTY: hard
