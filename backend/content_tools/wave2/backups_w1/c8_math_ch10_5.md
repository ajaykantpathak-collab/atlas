## ELI5
Sharing a total in a given ratio is like dividing sweets among three friends: first add the ratio numbers to find how many equal parts the whole breaks into, then give each friend their own count of those parts. The formula says it directly — each share is that ratio number over the sum of all ratio terms, times the total quantity. The ratio gives the shape of the cutting; the total decides how big each piece of money or rice truly is. One known share is enough to unlock all the others.

## DEFINITION
Sharing a quantity in a given ratio means dividing a total amount into parts that are proportional to the given ratio numbers. If a quantity \( Q \) is to be shared in the ratio \( a:b:c \), then the sum of the ratio terms is \( S = a+b+c \), and each share is calculated as \( \frac{a}{S} \times Q \), \( \frac{b}{S} \times Q \), \( \frac{c}{S} \times Q \) respectively.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can independently solve multi-step word problems where a total quantity (money, length, mass, etc.) must be divided among three or more parties in a given ratio, including problems where one share is known and the total or other shares must be found. They can also explain why the sum of the parts equals the original quantity.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers sharing a single quantity in a given ratio of two or more terms (including multi-term ratios from c8_math_ch10_4), finding individual shares, finding the total when one share is known, and verifying the shares sum to the total. It does NOT cover inverse proportion sharing (e.g., work sharing based on efficiency) or continued proportion problems, which are addressed in later nodes.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine your grandmother gives you ₹1,200 to share among your two cousins and yourself in the ratio 2:3:5 based on your ages. How much does each person get? First, recognise that the ratio 2:3:5 represents the relative sizes of the shares, not the actual amounts. The total number of parts is 2+3+5=10. Since the total money is ₹1,200, each part is worth ₹1,200 ÷ 10 = ₹120. Then multiply each ratio number by ₹120 to get the shares. This builds on the unitary method from Class 6 (c6_math_ch06_064) and extends it to multi-term ratios. The key insight is that the ratio gives the relative number of equal-sized parts, and the total quantity determines the value of one part.

Takeaway: sum the ratio terms to get S, divide the total by S to price one part, then multiply each person's ratio number by that one-part value — shares in order, no guesswork. Exam format: word problems hand you a total and a ratio; the setup marks come from writing S and the one-part value clearly before any share. Verification beat: add your shares back — the sum must equal the original total exactly. Why students miss it: they divide by one ratio number instead of by S, and the shares then never reassemble into the total they came from.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the first step when sharing a quantity in a given ratio like 3:5:7?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The first step is to find the sum of the ratio terms (3+5+7=15). This sum represents the total number of equal parts the quantity is divided into. A wrong answer (e.g., directly multiplying the total by 3) reveals the student thinks the ratio numbers are the actual shares or does not understand the concept of "parts" in a ratio.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
A sum of ₹600 is divided between A and B in the ratio 2:3. A student calculates A's share as \( \frac{2}{3} \times 600 = ₹400 \). Why is this incorrect?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The student used the wrong denominator. The correct denominator is the sum of the ratio terms (2+3=5), not the second term (3). The correct share for A is \( \frac{2}{5} \times 600 = ₹240 \). A wrong answer reveals the misconception that the ratio a:b means the fraction a/b of the total, instead of a/(a+b).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Three friends share a 2.4 kg bag of rice in the ratio 1:2:3. Calculate the share of each friend in grams.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Sum of ratio terms = 1+2+3 = 6 parts.
Step 2: Total quantity = 2.4 kg = 2400 grams.
Step 3: Value of one part = 2400 g ÷ 6 = 400 g.
Step 4: Shares: 1st = 1 × 400 = 400 g, 2nd = 2 × 400 = 800 g, 3rd = 3 × 400 = 1200 g.
Step 5: Check: 400+800+1200 = 2400 g. Correct.
A wrong answer (e.g., 400, 800, 1200 without unit conversion, or using 2.4 directly) reveals errors in unit conversion or forgetting to sum the ratio terms.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the two methods for sharing ₹500 in ratio 2:3:
Method X: Find 1 part = 500 ÷ (2+3) = 100, then shares = 200 and 300.
Method Y: Share of first = \( \frac{2}{2+3} \times 500 \), share of second = \( \frac{3}{2+3} \times 500 \).
Are these methods equivalent? Explain why or why not.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Yes, they are equivalent. Method X explicitly calculates the value of one part (unitary method), while Method Y uses the fraction of the total directly. Both rely on the same principle: each share is (ratio term / sum of terms) × total. A student who says they are different may not see the algebraic equivalence \( \frac{a}{a+b} \times Q = a \times \frac{Q}{a+b} \).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student solves: "Divide 60 cm in the ratio 1:2:3" and writes: 1st = 10 cm, 2nd = 20 cm, 3rd = 30 cm. Is this correct? Justify your answer.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The answer is incorrect. Sum of ratio terms = 1+2+3 = 6. Value of one part = 60 ÷ 6 = 10 cm. Correct shares: 10 cm, 20 cm, 30 cm. Wait, the student's numbers match! But the student likely just multiplied the ratio numbers by 10 without showing the division step. If they got the right numbers by coincidence (e.g., thinking ratio numbers are the shares directly), the working is flawed. A correct justification must show the sum of parts step. If the student only wrote the final numbers without the sum step, it reveals a lack of procedural understanding.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a real-life problem where a total quantity is shared in a ratio of 3:4:5, and one of the shares is 600 mL. Ask a question that requires finding the total quantity.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Example problem: "Three jars contain juice in the ratio 3:4:5. The middle jar has 600 mL. What is the total amount of juice?" A good answer sets up the ratio, identifies that the middle share corresponds to 4 parts, finds 1 part = 150 mL, then total = 12 parts = 1800 mL. A poor answer might assume 600 mL is the total or use the wrong ratio term, revealing confusion about which share corresponds to which ratio number.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often treat the ratio numbers as the actual shares (e.g., for ratio 2:3 and total 500, they say shares are 2 and 3, or 200 and 300 without dividing by 5). Another specific error: when the ratio has three terms like 2:3:5 and the total is 100, they might add 2+3+5=10 but then multiply 100 by 2, 3, 5 separately (getting 200, 300, 500) instead of multiplying by the fraction (2/10, 3/10, 5/10). This shows they confuse "multiply by ratio number" with "multiply by ratio fraction".
## ON_FAILURE
If a student makes the above error, guide them through a concrete representation: draw 10 equal boxes for the ratio 2:3:5, shade 2 for first, 3 for second, 5 for third. Ask: "If the total is 100, how much is in each box?" (100 ÷ 10 = 10). Then count boxes for each person. This visual "part-whole" model connects the unitary method to the fraction method. Emphasise: "Ratio numbers tell you HOW MANY parts each gets, not the amount."

## THIS_FORMULA
Share_i = (ratio_i / sum_of_all_ratio_terms) × Total_Quantity
## FORMULA_VARIABLES
ratio_i = the i-th term in the given ratio (e.g., for 2:3:5, ratio_1=2, ratio_2=3, ratio_3=5), sum_of_all_ratio_terms = total number of parts (e.g., 2+3+5=10), Total_Quantity = the quantity to be shared (e.g., ₹1200, 5 kg, etc.)
## FORMULA_UNITS
The units of the share are the same as the units of Total_Quantity (e.g., rupees, metres, kilograms, litres). The ratio terms are dimensionless.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A father wants to divide ₹15,000 among his three children in the ratio of their ages: 10 years, 15 years, and 20 years. How much does each child receive?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Write the ratio from the given ages: 10:15:20. Simplify by dividing by 5 to get 2:3:4.
- Step 2: Find the sum of the simplified ratio terms: 2 + 3 + 4 = 9 parts.
- Step 3: Calculate the value of one part: Total amount ÷ Total parts = ₹15,000 ÷ 9 = ₹1,666.67 (approx). Keep as fraction for accuracy: ₹15,000/9 = ₹5,000/3 per part.
- Step 4: Compute each share by multiplying the ratio term by the value of one part:
  - 1st child: 2 × (₹5,000/3) = ₹10,000/3 ≈ ₹3,333.33
  - 2nd child: 3 × (₹5,000/3) = ₹5,000
  - 3rd child: 4 × (₹5,000/3) = ₹20,000/3 ≈ ₹6,666.67
- Step 5: Verify: ₹3,333.33 + ₹5,000 + ₹6,666.67 = ₹15,000. Answer in rupees.
REASONING: The ratio of ages gives the relative proportions. Simplifying the ratio makes calculation easier but does not change the shares. Using the fraction ₹5,000/3 avoids rounding errors until the final step. The sum of shares must equal the original total.
ANSWER: 1st child ≈ ₹3,333.33, 2nd child = ₹5,000, 3rd child ≈ ₹6,666.67
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A 5-metre long rope is cut into three pieces in the ratio 2:3:5. Find the length of each piece in centimetres.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the ratio terms: 2, 3, 5. Sum = 2+3+5 = 10 parts.
- Step 2: Convert total length to centimetres: 5 m = 500 cm (since 1 m = 100 cm).
- Step 3: Find length of one part: 500 cm ÷ 10 = 50 cm.
- Step 4: Calculate each piece:
  - 1st piece = 2 × 50 cm = 100 cm
  - 2nd piece = 3 × 50 cm = 150 cm
  - 3rd piece = 5 × 50 cm = 250 cm
- Step 5: Check: 100 + 150 + 250 = 500 cm = 5 m. Correct.
REASONING: Unit conversion must be done before or after finding the value of one part, but consistently. Working in centimetres avoids decimals. The ratio terms directly give the number of 50-cm units each piece gets.
ANSWER: 100 cm, 150 cm, 250 cm
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: In a school, the ratio of boys to girls in Class 8 is 7:5. If there are 240 students in total, how many more boys than girls are there?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Ratio of boys:girls = 7:5. Total parts = 7+5 = 12.
- Step 2: Total students = 240. Value of one part = 240 ÷ 12 = 20 students.
- Step 3: Number of boys = 7 × 20 = 140. Number of girls = 5 × 20 = 100.
- Step 4: Difference = 140 - 100 = 40.
- Step 5: Alternatively, difference in ratio terms = 7-5 = 2 parts. So difference = 2 × 20 = 40. Both methods agree.
REASONING: The question asks for the difference, not the individual numbers. Recognising that the difference in shares corresponds to the difference in ratio terms multiplied by the value of one part is a more efficient strategy. This connects sharing to comparison of parts.
ANSWER: 40 more boys than girls.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A sum of money is divided among P, Q, and R in the ratio 4:5:6. If R gets ₹1,200 more than P, find the total sum and the share of Q.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions
STEPS:
- Step 1: Let the shares be 4x, 5x, 6x (where x is the value of one part).
- Step 2: Given R - P = ₹1,200 → 6x - 4x = 2x = 1,200 → x = 600.
- Step 3: Total sum = 4x+5x+6x = 15x = 15 × 600 = ₹9,000.
- Step 4: Share of Q = 5x = 5 × 600 = ₹3,000.
- Step 5: Verify: P=₹2,400, Q=₹3,000, R=₹3,600. Difference R-P = 1,200. Sum = 9,000.
ANSWER: Total sum = ₹9,000; Q's share = ₹3,000.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A 2.5 kg packet of tea is divided into three packets in the ratio 3:5:7. The largest packet is then repacked into small bags of 50 g each. How many small bags are obtained from the largest packet?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying multi-step solutions
STEPS:
- Step 1: Convert total to grams: 2.5 kg = 2500 g.
- Step 2: Ratio sum = 3+5+7 = 15 parts.
- Step 3: Value of one part = 2500 g ÷ 15 = 500/3 g ≈ 166.67 g.
- Step 4: Largest packet = 7 parts = 7 × (500/3) = 3500/3 g ≈ 1166.67 g.
- Step 5: Number of 50 g bags = (3500/3) ÷ 50 = 3500/150 = 70/3 = 23.33. Since bags must be whole, only 23 full bags can be made (with some tea left over).
- Step 6: Justify: 23 bags × 50 g = 1150 g used, leaving 16.67 g. 24 bags would need 1200 g which exceeds the packet.
ANSWER: 23 small bags (with 16.67 g remaining).
DIFFICULTY: hard
