## ELI5
A ratio is a comparison between two quantities of the same kind, written with a colon — how many times the first holds the second. Forty minutes to sixty minutes is a ratio, but forty minutes to sixty rupees is not — comparisons need a shared unit, like comparing heights with heights. The two terms have names: the first is the antecedent, the second the consequent. And the colon is just a fraction in disguise: the ratio says the same thing as the division, but keeps both quantities standing visibly side by side.

## DEFINITION
A ratio is a comparison of two quantities of the same kind and in the same units, expressed as an ordered pair \(a : b\) (read as "a is to b") where \(a\) is the antecedent and \(b\) is the consequent. It represents the multiplicative relationship \(a/b\) and indicates how many times the first quantity contains the second.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are comparing the price of two notebooks: one costs ₹40 and the other ₹60. You could say the second is ₹20 more (additive), but in proportional reasoning we ask "how many times?" – the second costs 1.5 times the first. This multiplicative comparison is a ratio. Start by writing the two quantities with units: 40 rupees and 60 rupees. Check they are the same unit (yes, both rupees). Write the ratio as 40:60, naming 40 the antecedent and 60 the consequent. Connect to Class 6 learning where you compared quantities using "for every" language. The key insight: a ratio \(a:b\) is exactly the fraction \(a/b\), but the colon notation keeps the two quantities visible for scaling up or down later.

Takeaway: a ratio compares same-kind, same-unit quantities as a:b, which is exactly the fraction a/b; name antecedent and consequent, and keep part-part distinct from part-whole. Exam format: expect 'write the ratio from the word problem' and 'explain why units must match' — the unit conversion shown before the colon earns the mark. Verification beat: convert both quantities to one unit, then form the ratio. Why students miss it: they ratio rupees to minutes or skip conversion — a ratio of mismatched units is a number without meaning, and the fraction view makes the scaling logic visible.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Write the ratio of 75 cm to 1.5 m in the form \(a:b\). Do not simplify.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Correct answer: 75:150 (after converting 1.5 m to 150 cm). A wrong answer of 75:1.5 reveals the student ignores the unit requirement and compares numbers directly without conversion. A wrong answer of 1.5:75 shows confusion about order (antecedent vs consequent) or part-whole vs part-part thinking.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
In a class of 40 students, 25 are girls. A student writes the ratio of girls to boys as 25:40. Is this correct? Explain why or why not.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Correct answer: No, it is incorrect. The ratio of girls to boys should compare girls (25) to boys (15), so it is 25:15. The student compared girls to total students (part-whole) instead of girls to boys (part-part). This reveals the misconception that the second term in a ratio can be the whole rather than the other part.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A recipe uses 3 cups of flour and 2 cups of sugar. Write the ratio of flour to sugar. Then write the ratio of sugar to total dry ingredients (flour + sugar). Express both in \(a:b\) form.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Flour:sugar = 3:2. Sugar:total = 2:5. Steps: (1) Identify quantities: flour=3, sugar=2. (2) First ratio: antecedent=flour=3, consequent=sugar=2 → 3:2. (3) Total dry ingredients = 3+2=5. (4) Second ratio: antecedent=sugar=2, consequent=total=5 → 2:5. A wrong answer of 2:3 for the second ratio shows the student used flour as the consequent instead of the total.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Two ratios are given: \(a:b = 4:5\) and \(c:d = 8:10\). Without simplifying, how can you tell they represent the same multiplicative relationship? What does this suggest about the definition of a ratio?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The ratios are the same because \(4/5 = 0.8\) and \(8/10 = 0.8\). This shows that a ratio captures the relative size (the quotient \(a/b\)), not the absolute numbers. A student who says "they are different because 4≠8 and 5≠10" treats ratio as an ordered pair of specific numbers rather than an equivalence class of pairs with the same quotient.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student says: "The ratio of 5 kg to 500 g is 5:500 because both are masses." Evaluate this statement and correct the error.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is incorrect. Although both are masses, the units differ (kg vs g). The correct ratio is 5000:500 (or 10:1 after conversion). The error is assuming "same kind" means "same unit" – the definition requires both same kind AND same unit. A student who accepts 5:500 confuses dimension with unit.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a real-world problem (Indian context) where the ratio of two quantities is 3:7. The quantities must be in the same unit but different from the examples used in class. Write the problem and the ratio with correct antecedent and consequent labels.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Example: "Rohan saves ₹300 per month and his sister saves ₹700 per month. The ratio of Rohan's savings to his sister's savings is 300:700 = 3:7. Antecedent = Rohan's savings (₹300), Consequent = sister's savings (₹700)." A poor answer uses different units (e.g., 3 kg and 7 g) or reverses the order, showing lack of attention to unit consistency and order significance.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students treat the ratio \(a:b\) as just two numbers written with a colon, ignoring that (1) the quantities must be in identical units before forming the ratio, and (2) the order \(a:b\) is not interchangeable – \(a:b\) is the multiplicative comparison \(a/b\), so \(3:5\) means 0.6 while \(5:3\) means 1.67. They also confuse part-part ratios (e.g., boys:girls) with part-whole ratios (e.g., boys:total students), writing 25:40 instead of 25:15 for 25 girls and 15 boys.
## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Ask the student to read the problem and underline the two quantities with their units. Step 2: Check if units match; if not, convert the larger unit to the smaller (e.g., m to cm, kg to g, rupees to paise). Step 3: Identify which quantity is the antecedent (first mentioned or "to" part) and which is the consequent. Step 4: Write the ratio as "antecedent : consequent" with the converted numbers. Step 5: Verify by forming the fraction and asking "does this fraction represent the intended comparison?"

## THIS_FORMULA
Ratio of quantity \(a\) to quantity \(b\) (same units) = \(a : b\) = \(\frac{a}{b}\)
## FORMULA_VARIABLES
a = antecedent (first quantity), b = consequent (second quantity), both measured in identical units
## FORMULA_UNITS
dimensionless (units cancel in the fraction \(a/b\)), but the original quantities carry units like cm, kg, rupees, litres
## COMPUTATION_TYPE
conceptual

## PREREQ_CONCEPT

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A rectangular garden is 12 m long and 8 m wide. Write the ratio of (i) length to width, (ii) width to perimeter, (iii) length to area. Express all ratios in \(a:b\) form without simplifying.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the quantities and their units. Length = 12 m, width = 8 m. Both are in metres, so units match.
- Step 2: For (i) length to width: antecedent = length = 12, consequent = width = 8. Write ratio 12:8.
- Step 3: For (ii) width to perimeter: first compute perimeter = 2×(12+8) = 40 m. Antecedent = width = 8, consequent = perimeter = 40. Write ratio 8:40.
- Step 4: For (iii) length to area: first compute area = 12×8 = 96 m². Note: length is in m, area is in m² – different units! Cannot form a ratio directly. Convert length to "square metres" is not meaningful. Instead, recognize that ratio requires same physical dimension. So length:area is not a valid ratio because units differ (m vs m²). The problem likely expects recognition of this error or a re-interpretation (e.g., length to breadth is valid, but length to area is not a standard ratio).
- Step 5: Conclude: (i) 12:8, (ii) 8:40, (iii) not a valid ratio because units differ (metres vs square metres).
REASONING: This example tests the critical condition that ratios compare quantities of the same kind and same unit. Length and area are different dimensions, so their ratio is not defined in the same way. It also reinforces antecedent/consequent order from the phrasing "A to B".
ANSWER: (i) 12:8, (ii) 8:40, (iii) Not a valid ratio (units: m vs m²).
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: In a school, the ratio of teachers to students is 1:35. If there are 28 teachers, how many students are there? Use the ratio notation to set up the relationship.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Write the given ratio: teachers : students = 1 : 35. This means for every 1 teacher there are 35 students.
- Step 2: Let the number of students be \(x\). The actual numbers must be in the same ratio: 28 : x = 1 : 35.
- Step 3: Express both ratios as fractions: \(\frac{28}{x} = \frac{1}{35}\).
- Step 4: Cross-multiply (or use equivalent ratio logic): \(28 \times 35 = 1 \times x\).
- Step 5: Compute \(x = 980\). Verify: 28:980 simplifies to 1:35 (divide both by 28).
REASONING: The ratio notation \(a:b\) directly translates to the fraction \(a/b\). Equating the two fractions uses the property that equivalent ratios have the same value. This bridges to the next node (equivalent ratios) and the unitary method.
ANSWER: 980 students.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A paint mixture requires red and blue paint in the ratio 3:5 by volume. If you have 2.5 litres of blue paint, how many millilitres of red paint are needed to maintain the ratio?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Write the ratio: red : blue = 3 : 5. Antecedent = red (3 parts), consequent = blue (5 parts).
- Step 2: Convert blue paint volume to millilitres for consistency: 2.5 L = 2500 mL.
- Step 3: Let red paint needed be \(r\) mL. Set up proportion: \(r : 2500 = 3 : 5\).
- Step 4: As fractions: \(\frac{r}{2500} = \frac{3}{5}\).
- Step 5: Solve for \(r\): \(r = 2500 \times \frac{3}{5} = 500 \times 3 = 1500\).
- Step 6: Interpret: 1500 mL = 1.5 L of red paint needed. Check: 1500:2500 simplifies to 3:5 (divide by 500).
REASONING: This combines unit conversion (L to mL), ratio notation, and proportional reasoning. The order of the ratio (red:blue) must match the problem statement. The fraction method is efficient and prepares for cross-multiplication in c8_math_ch7_7.
ANSWER: 1500 mL (or 1.5 L) of red paint.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A student claims that the ratio of 40 paise to ₹2 is 40:2 because "both are money". Identify the error, correct it, and explain why the correction is necessary using the definition of ratio.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions
STEPS:
- Step 1: Recognize that 40 paise and ₹2 are different units (paise vs rupees).
- Step 2: Convert ₹2 to paise: ₹2 = 200 paise.
- Step 3: Write the correct ratio: 40:200 (or simplified 1:5).
- Step 4: Explain that ratio requires same unit, not just same kind. The definition states "quantities of the same kind and in the same units".
- Step 5: Note that 40:2 would give a fraction of 20, while 40:200 gives 0.2, completely changing the multiplicative relationship.
ANSWER: Error: units not converted. Correct ratio: 40:200 (or 1:5). Necessary because ratio \(a:b\) represents \(a/b\); different units make the quotient meaningless.
DIFFICULTY: easy
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: In a class, the ratio of boys to girls is 4:5. The ratio of girls to total students is 5:9. A new student says: "So the ratio of boys to total students must be 4:9." Is the student correct? Justify your answer using ratio notation and part-whole relationships.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- Step 1: Let boys = 4k, girls = 5k (from 4:5).
- Step 2: Total students = boys + girls = 4k + 5k = 9k.
- Step 3: Ratio boys:total = 4k:9k = 4:9.
- Step 4: Check girls:total = 5k:9k = 5:9 (matches given).
- Step 5: Conclude the student is correct. The part-part ratio (boys:girls) and part-whole ratios (boys:total, girls:total) are consistent because total = sum of parts.
- Step 6: Note that this works only because the categories (boys, girls) are exhaustive and mutually exclusive.
ANSWER: Yes, the student is correct. Boys:total = 4:9. The part-part ratio 4:5 implies parts 4 and 5 out of total 9, giving part-whole ratios 4:9 and 5:9.
DIFFICULTY: medium
