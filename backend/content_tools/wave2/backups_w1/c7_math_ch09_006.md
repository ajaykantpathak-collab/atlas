## ELI5
Two Delhi garden beds must be exact copies, and the carpenter wants shortcuts: measure two sides and the angle caught between them - SAS. The word included is the fine print: the angle must sit between the measured sides, not at some other corner. With that caught angle equal plus both adjacent sides equal, the triangles are locked congruent, and every remaining part matches by correspondence. Give the angle at the wrong corner and the free side simply pivots - twins dissolve into strangers.

## DEFINITION
The SAS (Side-Angle-Side) Congruence Criterion states that two triangles are congruent if two sides and the included angle of one triangle are equal respectively to two sides and the included angle of the other triangle. The "included angle" is the exact angle formed between the two given sides.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
Can identify the included angle between two given sides of a triangle, apply the SAS criterion to prove triangle congruence, and use corresponding parts of congruent triangles (CPCTC) to find unknown side lengths or angles in geometric figures.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers the identification and application of the SAS congruence criterion for triangles in 2D Euclidean geometry, including multi-step proofs and numerical problem-solving. It does NOT cover non-Euclidean geometries or congruence criteria involving sides and non-included angles (such as SSA, which is invalid).
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are crafting two triangular wooden garden beds for your school project in New Delhi. To ensure both garden beds are exact carbon copies (congruent) so that they fit identical metal frames, your carpenter asks you for measurements. Instead of measuring all three sides and three angles, you realize you only need to measure two wooden planks and the exact corner angle where they meet. When you bring those exact measurements back, the carpenter knows for sure the triangle is unique and will match every time. To begin working with SAS, always draw a quick rough sketch of both triangles and highlight the two known sides and the angle *sandwiched* right between them. We will build on our knowledge of basic line segments, angles, and general congruence from Class 6 and early Chapter 9. The key insight is that the angle must be strictly *included* between the two sides—if the angle is outside those sides, the triangle shape can flex and change!

**Takeaway:** SAS congruence needs the included angle - the one sitting between the two measured sides. **In the exam:** locate the angle between the sides first; if it is not included, the criterion cannot be applied. **Check yourself:** point at the angle between two given sides of the garden bed sketch before writing SAS. **Why students miss it:** they accept any equal angle in the triangle as 'the' angle and claim SAS wrongly.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3

- A) $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DFE$
- B) $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DEF$
- C) $\triangle ACB \cong \triangle FED$
- D) $\triangle BAC \cong \triangle EFD$
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The correct answer is B) $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DEF$.
Working steps:
1. Identify the matching vertices based on equal corresponding parts: Side $AB$ ($4\text{ cm}$) corresponds to side $DE$ ($4\text{ cm}$), so vertex $A$ pairs with $D$, and $B$ pairs with $E$.
2. Identify the included angle: $\angle B$ is between $AB$ and $BC$; $\angle E$ is between $DE$ and $EF$. Thus, vertex $B$ corresponds to vertex $E$.
3. Identify the third side: $BC$ ($7\text{ cm}$) corresponds to $EF$ ($7\text{ cm}$), meaning vertex $C$ corresponds to $F$.
4. Form the correct correspondence matching vertices in order: $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DEF$.
What a wrong answer reveals: Choosing shuffled letters like A or C shows a failure to maintain vertex correspondence order, which is essential in geometry to show which sides and angles match up.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students frequently confuse the "included angle" with *any* random angle in the triangle. For example, if given sides $AB$ and $AC$, students will mistakenly look for or use angle $\angle B$ or angle $\angle C$ instead of the correct included angle $\angle A$, which is formed right where side $AB$ and side $AC$ meet. Another common error is writing vertex correspondence out of order in the final congruence statement (e.g., writing $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DFE$ when vertex $B$ corresponds to $E$ and $C$ corresponds to $F$).
## ON_FAILURE
If a student struggles with identifying the included angle, have them physically trace the two given sides with two colored pencils starting from their common endpoint (vertex). Tell them: "The vertex where your two colored pencils touch and pinch together is your included angle!" Practice pointing to the vertex letter that appears in both side labels (e.g., in side $PQ$ and side $QR$, letter $Q$ is shared, so $\angle Q$ is the included angle).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A local park designer in Jaipur is creating two triangular flower beds. In $\triangle PQR$, side $PQ = 5\text{ m}$, side $QR = 7\text{ m}$, and $\angle Q = 60^\circ$. In $\triangle XYZ$, side $XY = 5\text{ m}$, side $YZ = 7\text{ m}$, and $\angle Y = 60^\circ$. Prove that $\triangle PQR \cong \triangle XYZ$ and find the length of side $PR$ if $XZ = 6\text{ m}$.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify all given matching components between the two triangles: $PQ = XY = 5\text{ m}$, $QR = YZ = 7\text{ m}$, and $\angle Q = \angle Y = 60^\circ$.
- Step 2: Check if the given angle is the included angle. Since $\angle Q$ is formed between sides $PQ$ and $QR$, and $\angle Y$ is formed between sides $XY$ and $YZ$, the SAS condition is satisfied.
- Step 3: Write the congruence relation: By the SAS congruence criterion, $\triangle PQR \cong \triangle XYZ$.
- Step 4: Use corresponding parts of congruent triangles (CPCTC) to find unknown parts: Since corresponding sides are equal, side $PR$ must equal side $XZ$.
- Step 5: Substitute the given value: Given $XZ = 6\text{ m}$, therefore $PR = 6\text{ m}$.
REASONING: Because two pairs of sides and their exact included angle match in both triangles, the triangles are rigidly identical (congruent). Once congruence is proved, all other corresponding sides and angles match automatically via CPCTC.
ANSWER: $\triangle PQR \cong \triangle XYZ$ and $PR = 6\text{ m}$
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: In a quadrilateral $ABCD$, diagonal $AC$ bisects $\angle A$, and $AB = AD = 6\text{ cm}$. Prove that $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle ADC$.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Separate the figure into the two triangles sharing diagonal $AC$: $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle ADC$. List the known equal parts from the problem statement: $AB = AD = 6\text{ cm}$ (given).
- Step 2: Identify the common side shared by both triangles: Side $AC = AC$ (reflexive property of equality).
- Step 3: Analyze the angles using the given information: Diagonal $AC$ bisects $\angle A$, which means $\angle BAC = \angle DAC$.
- Step 4: Check criteria: We have side $AB$ equal to $AD$, angle $\angle BAC$ equal to $\angle DAC$ (included between the sides), and side $AC$ equal to $AC$.
- Step 5: Conclude congruence: By SAS congruence criterion, $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle ADC$.
REASONING: The reflexive property allows us to use side $AC$ as a shared side for both triangles. Because the angle is bisected directly between the two given sides, SAS applies perfectly.
ANSWER: $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle ADC$ proved by SAS criterion.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Two triangular aluminum roof trusses are being tested. For $\triangle ABC$, $AB = 8\text{ cm}$, $\angle B = 50^\circ$, and $BC = 10\text{ cm}$. For $\triangle DEF$, $DE = 8\text{ cm}$, $EF = 10\text{ cm}$, and $\angle E = 50^\circ$. If the cost of aluminum sheet per square centimeter is ₹2, and the area of $\triangle ABC$ is $30.6\text{ cm}^2$, find the total cost of materials to build *both* identical trusses.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Verify triangle congruence. In $\triangle ABC$, sides are $AB = 8\text{ cm}$, $BC = 10\text{ cm}$, and included angle is $\angle B = 50^\circ$. In $\triangle DEF$, sides are $DE = 8\text{ cm}$, $EF = 10\text{ cm}$, and included angle is $\angle E = 50^\circ$.
- Step 2: Conclude $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DEF$ by the SAS congruence criterion.
- Step 3: Use CPCTC to determine areas. Since congruent figures have identical areas, Area($\triangle DEF$) = Area($\triangle ABC$) = $30.6\text{ cm}^2$.
- Step 4: Calculate the total combined area of both triangular trusses: $\text{Total Area} = 30.6\text{ cm}^2 + 30.6\text{ cm}^2 = 61.2\text{ cm}^2$.
- Step 5: Calculate total cost by multiplying total area by cost per square centimeter: $\text{Total Cost} = 61.2 \times 2 = \text{₹}122.40$.
REASONING: First, establishing congruence via SAS ensures both triangles have identical dimensions and area. Then, arithmetic multiplication handles the cost scaling from unit area to total cost.
ANSWER: Total cost is ₹122.40
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A carpenter cuts two triangular wooden tiles for mosaic flooring. Tile 1 has side lengths $9\text{ cm}$ and $12\text{ cm}$ with an included angle of $55^\circ$. Tile 2 has side lengths $9\text{ cm}$ and $12\text{ cm}$ with an angle of $55^\circ$ positioned opposite to the $12\text{ cm}$ side instead of between the two sides. Are Tile 1 and Tile 2 guaranteed to be congruent? Justify your geometric reasoning completely.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying geometric criteria and identifying invalid congruence assumptions
STEPS:
- Step 1: Analyze Tile 1 data: Sides are $9\text{ cm}$ and $12\text{ cm}$, and $55^\circ$ is explicitly stated as the *included* angle between them.
- Step 2: Analyze Tile 2 data: Sides are $9\text{ cm}$ and $12\text{ cm}$, but the $55^\circ$ angle is *not* included between them (it is opposite one of the sides).
- Step 3: Compare conditions against criteria: Tile 1 satisfies the SAS (Side-Angle-Side) condition because the angle is sandwiched between the two known sides. Tile 2 forms an SSA (Side-Side-Angle) condition.
- Step 4: Formulate conclusion: Tile 1 is a unique rigid triangle because of SAS. Tile 2 can pivot at the non-included angle vertex to form multiple different triangle shapes (the ambiguous case). Therefore, they are not guaranteed to be congruent.
ANSWER: No, Tile 1 and Tile 2 are not guaranteed to be congruent. Tile 1 satisfies the SAS criterion (unique triangle), whereas Tile 2 represents an SSA condition, which does not guarantee a unique shape because the non-included angle allows the side to swing.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: In an engineering blueprint for a bridge support, line segments $AD$ and $BC$ bisect each other at point $O$, such that $AO = DO$ and $BO = CO$. 
(a) Prove that $\triangle AOB \cong \triangle DOC$. 
(b) Hence, prove that side $AB$ is parallel to side $DC$.
COMPETENCY: Multi-step geometric proof combining triangle congruence and parallel line properties
STEPS:
- Step 1: Examine $\triangle AOB$ and $\triangle DOC$. Note the given vertical side relations: $AO = DO$ and $BO = CO$.
- Step 2: Identify the vertically opposite angles formed at intersection point $O$: $\angle AOB = \angle DOC$.
- Step 3: Apply SAS criterion: In $\triangle AOB$ and $\triangle DOC$, we have two sides ($AO, BO$) and their included angle ($\angle AOB$) equal respectively to two sides ($DO, CO$) and their included angle ($\angle DOC$). Thus, $\triangle AOB \cong \triangle DOC$ by SAS.
- Step 4: Use CPCTC from the congruence result to establish corresponding angles: $\angle OAB = \angle ODC$ (or $\angle ABO = \angle DCO$).
- Step 5: Connect to parallel lines: Since alternate interior angles formed by transversal line $AD$ intersecting lines $AB$ and $CD$ are equal ($\angle OAB = \angle ODC$), line $AB$ must be parallel to line $DC$ ($AB \parallel CD$).
ANSWER: (a) Proved $\triangle AOB \cong \triangle DOC$ by SAS using $AO=DO$, $BO=CO$, and vertically opposite angles $\angle AOB = \angle DOC$. (b) By CPCTC, alternate interior angles are equal, proving $AB \parallel CD$.
DIFFICULTY: hard
