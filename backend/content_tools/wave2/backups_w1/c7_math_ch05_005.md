## ELI5
The corner where your classroom wall meets the floor is the perfect picture of perpendicular lines: two lines meeting at an exact right angle. At such a crossing all four angles are 90 degrees, the figure carries a small square mark, and the symbol between the lines reads 'perpendicular to'. One right angle at a crossing forces the other three, because straight angles and vertical opposites leave no freedom. Walls, door frames and notebook margins all lean on this square-corner promise.

## DEFINITION
Perpendicular lines are two intersecting lines that meet at an exact right angle ($90^\circ$). When line $AB$ is perpendicular to line $CD$ (written symbolically as $AB \perp CD$), all four angles formed at their point of intersection measure precisely $90^\circ$.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine walking into your classroom and looking at the corner where the walls meet the floor—the vertical wall and the horizontal floor form a square corner, which is a right angle! In geometry, when two lines intersect to create this exact $90^\circ$ square corner, we call them perpendicular lines. To begin working with them, you must remember that intersecting lines form four angles around a central point, and if just one of those angles measures $90^\circ$, all adjacent and opposite angles must also equal $90^\circ$. Building on your prior knowledge of basic angle types and intersecting lines from Class 6 geometry, you will now learn how to spot perpendicular indicators (like the square symbol at an intersection) and use them to solve missing angle puzzles. The key insight is that perpendicularity is a special case of intersection where angles are locked at $90^\circ$.

**Takeaway:** perpendicular lines intersect at right angles, so all four angles at the crossing measure exactly a quarter-turn. **In the exam:** for algebra items, set the marked angle expression equal to the right-angle measure and solve. **Check yourself:** check a room corner with the set-square and count the four equal angles it makes. **Why students miss it:** they assume one right angle leaves the others free, forgetting the straight-line pairs force all four.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What symbol is universally used in geometry to indicate that two lines or line segments are perpendicular to each other?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The correct answer is the perpendicular symbol ($\perp$), or a small square ($\llcorner$) drawn inside the angle at the intersection. If a student answers with an X or a parallel sign ($\parallel$), it reveals a confusion between intersection types or parallel lines. Remind them that a square corner indicates a $90^\circ$ right angle.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If line $P$ intersects line $Q$ at a single point and forms one angle measuring $90^\circ$, what must the measures of the other three angles formed at that intersection be? Explain why.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The correct answer is that all three remaining angles must also measure $90^\circ$. This is because adjacent angles on a straight line sum to $180^\circ$ (linear pair), and vertically opposite angles are equal. If a student thinks the other angles could be different (e.g., guessing $45^\circ$ or $135^\circ$), they do not understand that a single right angle at an intersection forces the entire set of four angles to form four right angles.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Line segment $EF$ is perpendicular to line segment $GH$ at point $O$. If $\angle EOG$ is represented algebraically as $(3x)^\circ$, what is the exact numerical value of $x$?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The correct answer is $x = 30$. 
Working steps:
1. Identify that $EF \perp GH$, which means the angle between them ($\angle EOG$) is a right angle ($90^\circ$).
2. Set up the algebraic equation: $3x = 90$.
3. Divide both sides by 3 to isolate $x$: $x = \frac{90}{3}$.
4. Compute the final value: $x = 30$.
If a student gets an incorrect value like $3$ or $270$, it shows they either tried to multiply instead of divide or did not recognize that perpendicular lines always measure $90^\circ$.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Two lines, $AB$ and $CD$, intersect at point $O$. Angle $AOC$ is given as $2x + 10$ and angle $BOD$ is a right angle formed because $AB \perp CD$. Can you find the value of $x$, and are lines $AB$ and $CD$ perpendicular? Explain.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The correct answer is $x = 40$, and yes, they are perpendicular. 
Working steps:
1. Recognize that if $AB \perp CD$, all angles at intersection $O$ are $90^\circ$.
2. Set $\angle AOC = 2x + 10$ equal to $90^\circ$ because all four angles at a perpendicular intersection are $90^\circ$.
3. Solve the linear equation: $2x + 10 = 90 \implies 2x = 80 \implies x = 40$.
4. Conclude that since the angle equals $90^\circ$, the lines are indeed perpendicular. 
If a student states they cannot solve it because two different angles were mentioned, they failed to connect the symbol $\perp$ to the universal $90^\circ$ rule for all four intersecting quadrants.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student claims that if two line segments $AB$ and $CD$ intersect and form four angles, and if we add all four angles together, the sum must be $360^\circ$. Furthermore, if the lines are perpendicular, each angle is $90^\circ$, so $90 \times 4 = 360^\circ$. Is this student's reasoning completely correct, partially correct, or flawed? Justify your answer.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The correct answer is that the student's reasoning is completely correct. 
Justification: 
1. The sum of angles around a single point (complete angle) is always $360^\circ$. 
2. Perpendicular lines divide this $360^\circ$ space into four equal quadrants. 
3. Dividing $360^\circ$ by 4 gives $90^\circ$ per angle. 
If a student claims the reasoning is flawed, they likely confuse angles around a point with triangles ($180^\circ$) or straight lines ($180^\circ$).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a real-world scenario in a school playground involving two boundary chalk lines where you must guarantee they are perpendicular using only a measuring tape and the properties of numbers. Describe your steps.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The expected response should use the Pythagorean relationship (or right-angle properties) as a practical extension: measure a length of 3 metres along one line, 4 metres along the second line from the same vertex, and adjust the angle until the diagonal distance connecting those two points measures exactly 5 metres ($3^2 + 4^2 = 5^2$). A poor answer simply states "draw it with a square tool" without explaining how to mathematically verify or construct a perpendicular line in an open field using numerical measurements.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often assume that any two intersecting lines that look "sort of upright" or form a cross are perpendicular, or they confuse perpendicular lines ($\perp$) with parallel lines ($\parallel$). Another frequent error is setting an algebraic expression equal to $180^\circ$ instead of $90^\circ$ when solving for an unknown angle in a perpendicular intersection, mixing up a linear pair with a single right angle. For example, given $5x = 90$, a student might incorrectly write $5x = 180$ because they confuse perpendicularity with a straight line.
## ON_FAILURE
If a student makes these errors, take a sheet of paper and fold it in half twice so the crease forms a crisp "plus" sign. Explain: "When you unfold this paper, every single corner where the creases meet is a square corner measuring exactly $90^\circ$—that is what perpendicular means." Next, write down the equation $90^\circ$ in bold red marker next to the intersection and remind them that the symbol $\perp$ is a shorthand way of writing "equals $90$ degrees". Have them practice rewriting every word problem statement like "$AB \perp CD$" into the numerical equation "$\angle = 90^\circ$" before solving any algebra.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: In a school courtyard, a vertical flagpole $AB$ stands on a flat horizontal playground pavement $CD$. The flagpole is perfectly perpendicular to the ground, so that $AB \perp CD$. If a guy-wire attached to the top of the flagpole makes an angle of $(5x)^\circ$ with the ground on the right side, calculate the exact value of $x$.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the given conditions and what is asked. We are given that flagpole $AB$ is perpendicular to ground $CD$ ($AB \perp CD$), which means the angle between them is $90^\circ$. We are given the angle expression $5x = 90$. We need to find $x$.
- Step 2: Set up the equation using the definition of perpendicular lines. Since perpendicular lines intersect at a right angle, $5x = 90^\circ$.
- Step 3: Isolate the variable $x$ by dividing both sides of the equation by 5.
- Step 4: Perform the division calculation: $x = \frac{90}{5}$.
- Step 5: Compute the final value: $x = 18$, and verify by substituting back: $5(18) = 90^\circ$.
REASONING: Perpendicular lines guarantee a $90^\circ$ intersection. By translating the geometric symbol $\perp$ into an algebraic equation equal to $90$, we can easily solve for unknown variables using basic division.
ANSWER: $x = 18$
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Two metal rods representing lines $LM$ and $NO$ are welded together such that $LM \perp NO$ at intersection point $P$. An angle formed at this intersection is labeled as $(4x + 10)^\circ$. Find the numerical value of $x$.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given information: $LM \perp NO$, meaning any angle formed at the intersection point $P$ equals $90^\circ$.
- Step 2: Set up the algebraic equation connecting the given angle to the right angle: $4x + 10 = 90$.
- Step 3: Subtract 10 from both sides of the equation to isolate the term with $x$: $4x = 90 - 10 \implies 4x = 80$.
- Step 4: Divide both sides by 4 to solve for $x$: $x = \frac{80}{4}$.
- Step 5: Compute the final value and check: $x = 20$. Verification: $4(20) + 10 = 80 + 10 = 90^\circ$.
REASONING: Because the rods are perpendicular, the algebraic expression representing the angle must be equated to $90^\circ$. Following standard linear equation steps (subtracting then dividing) yields the correct value for $x$.
ANSWER: $x = 20$
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A carpenter is building a rectangular wooden tabletop. Two adjacent edges, representing lines $AB$ and $BC$, must be strictly perpendicular ($AB \perp BC$). However, a measuring tool shows that the angle between them is given by $(3x + 15)^\circ$, while the complementary adjacent angle on the straight line is part of a linear pair. Find the value of $x$ and determine the measure of the angle adjacent to it on the straight line $BC$.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Note the condition that $AB \perp BC$, which means the main angle $\angle ABC = 90^\circ$.
- Step 2: Set up the equation for the given angle: $3x + 15 = 90$.
- Step 3: Solve for $x$ by subtracting 15 from 90, giving $3x = 75$, then dividing by 3 to get $x = 25$.
- Step 4: Identify the second part of the problem: find the adjacent angle on the straight line. Since the line is straight ($180^\circ$) and one angle is $90^\circ$, the linear pair rule gives $180^\circ - 90^\circ = 90^\circ$.
- Step 5: Verify all calculations: $3(25) + 15 = 75 + 15 = 90^\circ$, and $90^\circ + 90^\circ = 180^\circ$.
REASONING: Combining perpendicular line properties with linear pair rules allows us to check consistency across the intersection. Perpendicularity forces both the primary angle and its linear pair supplement to be right angles ($90^\circ$).
ANSWER: $x = 25$; adjacent angle is $90^\circ$
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A road planner is sketching a map where Main Street and Cross Avenue intersect at a $90^\circ$ angle, denoted as Main Street $\perp$ Cross Avenue. A traffic sign is placed in one of the corners, and its angle is marked as $(7x - 5)^\circ$. A local inspector claims that if $x = 13$, the sign is set at the correct perpendicular angle. Verify whether the inspector's claim is correct by showing all steps, and explain what happens if $x$ is instead $14$.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions and verifying solutions
STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand that perpendicular lines require the angle to measure exactly $90^\circ$.
- Step 2: Substitute $x = 13$ into the expression $7x - 5$ and calculate: $7(13) - 5 = 91 - 5 = 86^\circ$.
- Step 3: Compare the result to $90^\circ$. Since $86^\circ \neq 90^\circ$, the inspector's claim is false.
- Step 4: Test $x = 14$ to see what happens: $7(14) - 5 = 98 - 5 = 93^\circ$ (still not perpendicular).
ANSWER: The inspector's claim is incorrect because substituting $x = 13$ results in an angle of $86^\circ$, not $90^\circ$. For the lines to be perpendicular, the expression must evaluate to exactly $90^\circ$.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Two intersecting lines, $PQ$ and $RS$, intersect at point $O$. It is known that $PQ \perp RS$. Two adjacent angles formed at this intersection are given as $(5y)^\circ$ and $(2z + 10)^\circ$. Additionally, a student writes that because the lines are perpendicular, $5y = 90$ and $2z + 10 = 90$. Calculate both $y$ and $z$, and explain why the student's method of setting each separate expression to $90^\circ$ works in this specific geometric context.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying multi-variable geometric relationships
STEPS:
- Step 1: Recognize that because $PQ \perp RS$, all four angles formed around intersection point $O$ are right angles ($90^\circ$).
- Step 2: Take the first expression and set it equal to $90$: $5y = 90 \implies y = \frac{90}{5} = 18$.
- Step 3: Take the second expression and set it equal to $90$: $2z + 10 = 90 \implies 2z = 80 \implies z = 40$.
- Step 4: Justify why this works: perpendicular lines divide the intersection into four independent right-angle quadrants, so any single angle occupying one of those quadrants must equal $90^\circ$.
ANSWER: $y = 18$ and $z = 40$. The method works because perpendicular lines guarantee that every single one of the four angles meeting at the intersection point measures $90^\circ$, allowing us to solve for each variable independently by equating its expression to $90$.
DIFFICULTY: hard
