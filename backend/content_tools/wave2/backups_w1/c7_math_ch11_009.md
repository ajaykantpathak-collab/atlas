## ELI5
Pack notebooks and pens at the school shop and a quiet law emerges: for 12 and 18, the HCF 6 times the LCM 36 lands exactly on 12 times 18. HCF times LCM always equals the product of the two numbers - a seesaw that lets any seat be found from the other three. Missing an LCM? Divide the product by the HCF. The law holds for every pair of positive integers, which is why shopkeepers and examiners both trust it.

## DEFINITION
The product of the Highest Common Factor (HCF) and the Least Common Multiple (LCM) of any two positive integers is always equal to the product of the two numbers themselves ($HCF \times LCM = Product\text{ of the two numbers}$). This fundamental property allows us to find the LCM or HCF of two numbers quickly if their product and one of the values are known.

Step 2: Substitute the known values into the equation: $10 \times LCM = 1500$. 
Step 3: Solve for LCM by dividing both sides by 10: $LCM = 1500 \div 10 = 150$. 
Step 4: Verify the result: $10 \times 150 = 1500$. 
The correct LCM is 150. A wrong answer like 15000 or 15 usually comes from multiplying instead of dividing, or misplacing decimal places during the inverse operation.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students frequently confuse the operations when using the formula $HCF \times LCM = a \times b$. When asked to find the LCM given the HCF and the product, students often multiply the HCF by the product instead of dividing the product by the HCF (e.g., calculating $10 \times 1500 = 15000$ instead of $1500 \div 10 = 150$). Another common error is trying to apply $HCF \times LCM = a \times b$ to *three* numbers, assuming the product of three numbers equals the product of their HCF and LCM, which is mathematically false.
## ON_FAILURE
When a student makes this error, stop them and have them write out the formula in words first: "(HCF) times (LCM) equals (First Number times Second Number)". Ask them: "If we know the total product and one part of the left side, do we multiply or do we use the opposite operation (division) to find the missing part?" Guide them to check their answer by multiplying their calculated LCM back by the HCF to see if it remakes the original product.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: The HCF of two numbers is 14 and their LCM is 210. If one of the numbers is 42, find the second number by using the relationship between HCF and LCM.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the given values from the problem statement: $HCF = 14$, $LCM = 210$, and first number $a = 42$. Let the unknown second number be $x$.
- Step 2: Write down the standard relationship formula: $HCF \times LCM = a \times b$.
- Step 3: Substitute the known values into the equation: $14 \times 210 = 42 \times x$.
- Step 4: Multiply the left side: $2940 = 42x$, then solve for $x$ by dividing both sides by 42: $x = 2940 \div 42$.
- Step 5: Compute the final division: $x = 70$, and verify by checking $42 \times 70 = 2940$ and $14 \times 210 = 2940$.
REASONING: The formula connects the product of any two numbers directly to the product of their HCF and LCM. By treating the unknown number as a variable $x$, we form a simple linear equation that can be solved using division.
ANSWER: 70
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: The product of two numbers is 1800. If their LCM is 300, find their HCF without listing out all multiples or factors.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the given values: Product of the two numbers $= 1800$, and $LCM = 300$. Let the unknown HCF be $h$.
- Step 2: State the relationship formula linking HCF, LCM, and product: $HCF \times LCM = Product$.
- Step 3: Substitute the known values into the formula: $h \times 300 = 1800$.
- Step 4: Isolate $h$ by dividing both sides of the equation by 300: $h = 1800 \div 300$.
- Step 5: Perform the division: $h = 6$, confirming that the HCF is 6.
REASONING: When the product of two numbers and one of their divisor-multiple values (LCM or HCF) is known, division immediately yields the other value due to the multiplicative property of prime factors.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Two co-prime numbers have a product of 221. What is their LCM, and what is their HCF? Use the HCF-LCM property to justify your answer.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Recall the definition of co-prime numbers: two numbers whose only common factor is 1. Therefore, their $HCF = 1$.
- Step 2: Write down the HCF-LCM relationship formula: $HCF \times LCM = Product\text{ of numbers}$.
- Step 3: Substitute the known HCF ($1$) and the given product ($221$) into the formula: $1 \times LCM = 221$.
- Step 4: Solve for the LCM: $LCM = 221 \div 1 = 221$.
- Step 5: Interpret the result: For any two co-prime numbers, their HCF is 1 and their LCM is always equal to their product ($221$).
REASONING: Co-prime numbers share no common prime factors other than 1. This special property forces their HCF to be 1, which in turn makes their LCM equal to their direct product according to our formula.
ANSWER: HCF = 1, LCM = 221
