## ELI5
Buy one extra notebook and your bill grows by exactly one notebook's price — not by the whole new total. That is the pattern: bump one factor by one and the product gains precisely the other factor. The distributive property explains it in one breath: the enlarged factor splits into the old factor plus one, and the plus-one portion contributes just the untouched partner. Mental arithmetic loves this: compute the old product, add the partner, done.

## DEFINITION
When one factor in a multiplication is increased by 1, the product increases exactly by the value of the other factor. For any two numbers \(a\) and \(b\), \((a+1) \times b = a \times b + b\) and \(a \times (b+1) = a \times b + a\).
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can compute the new product after increasing one factor by 1 without performing full multiplication, by adding the other factor to the original product. The student can also explain why this works using the distributive property.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the effect of increasing exactly one factor by exactly 1 on the product of two numbers (integers, decimals, or algebraic terms). It does not cover increasing by more than 1, decreasing a factor, or changing both factors simultaneously (those are covered in later nodes).
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are buying notebooks for your class. Each notebook costs ₹15. You initially plan to buy 12 notebooks, so the total cost is \(12 \times 15 = ₹180\). At the last moment, one more student joins, so you need 13 notebooks. How much extra money do you need? Instead of calculating \(13 \times 15\) from scratch, think: the new total is \((12+1) \times 15\). Using the distributive property, this equals \(12 \times 15 + 1 \times 15 = 180 + 15 = ₹195\). The extra cost is exactly the price of one notebook (₹15). This pattern holds for any numbers: increasing one factor by 1 adds the other factor to the product. Write the original product, identify the other factor, and add it.

Takeaway: increasing one factor by one adds the other factor to the product — from the distributive view, the extra slice of the enlarged factor is exactly the untouched partner. Exam format: expect 'find the new product without full multiplication' and 'explain why' — the split-into-old-plus-one line is the reasoning. Verification beat: state which factor was increased; the added amount must be the other one, not the increased one. Why students miss it: they add the increased factor instead of its partner — the change comes from the new slice, whose size is the partner's value.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
State the rule: If we have a product \(a \times b\) and we increase \(a\) by 1, by how much does the product increase? Write the new product in terms of the original product.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The product increases by \(b\). The new product is \(a \times b + b\). A wrong answer (e.g., "increases by 1" or "increases by \(a\)") reveals the student thinks the increment transfers directly to the product or confuses which factor is added. They have not connected the distributive expansion \((a+1)b = ab + b\) to the change in product.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why \((25 \times 37) + 37\) is equal to \(26 \times 37\) without calculating the actual products. Use the distributive property in your explanation.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
\(26 \times 37 = (25+1) \times 37 = 25 \times 37 + 1 \times 37 = 25 \times 37 + 37\). A wrong answer that just says "because 26 is one more than 25" shows the student sees the pattern but cannot justify it algebraically. They lack the understanding that the distributive property is the mathematical reason behind the pattern.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If \(48 \times 12 = 576\), what is \(48 \times 13\)? Solve using the increment rule, showing your steps.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
\(48 \times 13 = 48 \times (12+1) = 48 \times 12 + 48 \times 1 = 576 + 48 = 624\). A wrong answer like 577 (adding 1) or 624 but with incorrect steps (e.g., \(576 + 12\)) reveals the student either adds 1 to the product or adds the wrong factor. They are not identifying which factor was increased.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the increase in product when you increase the first factor by 1 versus increasing the second factor by 1 for the product \(15 \times 8\). Which change gives a larger increase? Explain.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Increasing 15 by 1: new product = \(16 \times 8 = 120 + 8 = 128\), increase = 8. Increasing 8 by 1: new product = \(15 \times 9 = 120 + 15 = 135\), increase = 15. Increasing the second factor (8→9) gives a larger increase (15) because the increase equals the other factor (15), which is larger than 8. A wrong answer that says both increases are equal or picks the wrong one shows the student does not realize the increase amount depends on the *other* factor's value.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student solved: \(23 \times 14 = 322\). To find \(24 \times 14\), they wrote \(322 + 1 = 323\). Is this correct? If not, identify the error and give the correct answer.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Incorrect. The student added 1 instead of adding the other factor (14). Correct: \(24 \times 14 = (23+1) \times 14 = 23 \times 14 + 14 = 322 + 14 = 336\). A wrong evaluation (saying it's correct) reveals the student shares the misconception that increasing a factor by 1 increases the product by 1.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a real-life word problem (using rupees, metres, or kilograms) where increasing one quantity by 1 changes the total by a specific amount. State the original product, the change, and the new total.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Example: "A rope is cut into 12 pieces each 5 metres long. Total length = 60 m. If one more piece of 5 m is added, the new total is 65 m." A good answer shows the original product (12×5), identifies the other factor (5) as the increase, and computes the new total (65). A poor answer might change the piece length instead of the number of pieces, or add 1 metre instead of 5 metres, showing confusion about which factor corresponds to the increment.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think increasing a factor by 1 increases the product by 1 (e.g., \(12 \times 15 = 180\), so \(13 \times 15 = 181\)). They treat the increment as an absolute addition to the product rather than a multiplicative addition of the other factor. This stems from additive thinking: "I added 1 to one number, so I add 1 to the answer." They fail to apply the distributive property \((a+1)b = ab + b\) and instead do \(ab + 1\).
## ON_FAILURE
Draw an area model: a rectangle of length \(a\) and breadth \(b\) (area \(ab\)). Extend the length by 1 unit. The new area is the original rectangle plus a thin strip of dimensions \(1 \times b\). The strip's area is \(b\). Ask: "What is the area of the added strip?" Then write \((a+1)b = ab + b\). Practice with 3 numerical examples where the student must circle the "other factor" that gets added. Emphasize: "The increase is the *other* factor, not 1."

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A school orders 25 boxes of pencils. Each box contains 12 pencils. The clerk calculates the total as 300 pencils. Just before delivery, the school requests 1 more box. How many pencils will be delivered in total? Use the increment rule to find the answer without multiplying 26 × 12 directly.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the original product: 25 boxes × 12 pencils/box = 300 pencils.
- Step 2: Identify the change: number of boxes increases by 1 (from 25 to 26). The other factor (pencils per box) is 12.
- Step 3: Apply the increment rule: new total = original total + other factor = 300 + 12.
- Step 4: Compute: 300 + 12 = 312.
- Step 5: Verify: 26 × 12 = (20+6)×12 = 240+72 = 312. The answer is 312 pencils.
REASONING: Increasing the number of boxes by 1 adds exactly one box's worth of pencils (12) to the total. This is the distributive property: (25+1)×12 = 25×12 + 1×12.
ANSWER: 312 pencils
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A rectangular garden is 18 metres long and 7 metres wide. Its area is 126 m². The owner extends the length by 1 metre. What is the new area? Solve using the increment rule and also by direct multiplication to check.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Note original dimensions: length = 18 m, width = 7 m. Original area = 18 × 7 = 126 m².
- Step 2: The length increases by 1 m (new length = 19 m). The other factor (width) remains 7 m.
- Step 3: Apply rule: new area = original area + width = 126 + 7.
- Step 4: Compute: 126 + 7 = 133 m².
- Step 5: Check by direct multiplication: 19 × 7 = (20-1)×7 = 140 - 7 = 133 m². Both methods agree.
REASONING: Extending the length by 1 metre adds a strip of area 1 m × 7 m = 7 m² to the garden. The increment rule captures this added strip without recalculating the whole area.
ANSWER: 133 m²
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A shopkeeper sells rice at ₹45 per kg. On Monday he sold 32 kg. On Tuesday he sold 1 kg more than Monday. If he uses the increment rule to find Tuesday's revenue quickly, what calculation does he do? If Monday's revenue was ₹1440, what is Tuesday's revenue? Also, find the increase in revenue.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify factors: price per kg = ₹45 (constant), quantity sold on Monday = 32 kg.
- Step 2: Monday's revenue = 32 × 45 = ₹1440 (given).
- Step 3: Tuesday's quantity = 32 + 1 = 33 kg. The factor that increased is quantity; the other factor is price (₹45).
- Step 4: Apply increment rule: Tuesday's revenue = Monday's revenue + price per kg = 1440 + 45.
- Step 5: Compute: 1440 + 45 = ₹1485.
- Step 6: Increase in revenue = ₹45 (which is exactly the price per kg). Verify: 33 × 45 = (30+3)×45 = 1350+135 = 1485.
REASONING: The increment rule works because the price per kg is constant. Selling 1 extra kg adds exactly ₹45 to the revenue. This is a direct application of (q+1)×p = q×p + p.
ANSWER: Tuesday's revenue = ₹1485; Increase = ₹45
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Ravi says: "If I know 17 × 24 = 408, then 18 × 24 = 409 because I added 1 to 17." Priya says: "No, 18 × 24 = 432 because you add 24." Who is correct? Explain the reasoning using the distributive property. Then, without multiplying, find 17 × 25 using a similar rule.
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction; Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the original product (17×24=408) and the change (17→18).
- Step 2: Apply the increment rule: increasing the first factor by 1 adds the second factor (24) to the product.
- Step 3: Compute correct new product: 408 + 24 = 432. Priya is correct.
- Step 4: Explain Ravi's error: he added 1 instead of the other factor.
- Step 5: For 17×25, note the second factor increased by 1 (24→25). Add the first factor (17) to 408: 408 + 17 = 425.
ANSWER: Priya is correct. 18 × 24 = 432. Ravi incorrectly added 1 instead of 24. Using the rule for the second factor: 17 × 25 = 408 + 17 = 425.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A factory produces 240 toys per day working 8 hours. The manager increases the working day by 1 hour (to 9 hours) assuming the hourly production rate stays the same. By how many toys does the daily production increase? If the manager instead hires 1 more worker (keeping 8 hours) and each worker produces the same hourly rate, by how much does production increase? Compare the two increases and explain which strategy yields more toys, given there are currently 10 workers.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying; Applying algebraic reasoning to real-world scenarios
STEPS:
- Step 1: Find hourly production rate: 240 toys / 8 hours = 30 toys/hour (total for all workers).
- Step 2: Strategy A (increase hours by 1): New daily production = 30 toys/hour × 9 hours = 270. Increase = 30 toys. (Increment rule: increase = hourly rate = 30).
- Step 3: Strategy B (add 1 worker): Current 10 workers → 11 workers. Hourly rate per worker = 30/10 = 3 toys/hour. New hourly rate = 11 × 3 = 33 toys/hour. New daily production = 33 × 8 = 264. Increase = 24 toys.
- Step 4: Compare: Strategy A increases by 30 toys, Strategy B by 24 toys. Strategy A yields more.
- Step 5: General reasoning: Increasing hours by 1 adds the current hourly rate (30). Adding 1 worker adds (hourly rate per worker × hours) = 3 × 8 = 24. Since hourly rate (30) > 24, Strategy A wins.
ANSWER: Increasing hours by 1 adds 30 toys; adding 1 worker adds 24 toys. Increasing hours yields more production because the hourly rate (30) is greater than the per-worker daily output (24).
DIFFICULTY: hard
