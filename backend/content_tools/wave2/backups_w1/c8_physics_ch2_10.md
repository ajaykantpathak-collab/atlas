## ELI5
A bulb is a factory that turns electricity into light — and unfortunately into heat as well. Efficiency asks the honest question: of all the electricity that went in, what fraction came out as useful light? The rest is warmth wasted into the room. Two bulbs of equal brightness can sip different amounts of electricity; the lighter sipper is the efficient one. Old filament bulbs are heaters that glow as a side effect; comparing their fraction with modern lamps tells you why old bulbs are fading away.

## DEFINITION
Efficiency of a light bulb is the ratio of useful light energy output to the total electrical energy input, expressed as a percentage. It measures how effectively a bulb converts electrical energy into visible light rather than waste heat.

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
Student can calculate the efficiency of a bulb given its light output power and electrical input power, and can compare two bulbs to determine which is more energy-efficient for the same brightness.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers energy efficiency comparison between incandescent bulbs and CFLs using power ratings and light output; does not cover LED technology, luminous efficacy in lumens per watt, or detailed internal working of CFLs (mercury vapor, phosphor coating).
## WALKTHROUGH
Observe two lamps at home: an old incandescent bulb and a modern CFL, both giving similar brightness. Touch the glass carefully (or feel the heat from a distance) – the incandescent bulb feels much hotter. This shows more electrical energy is wasted as heat. To begin, measure the electrical power (watts) each bulb consumes from its label. Then understand that efficiency = (light power output / electrical power input) × 100%. Since light power is not directly given, we compare bulbs of equal brightness: the one with lower wattage is more efficient. Connect this to Joule's heating effect (c8_physics_ch2_6) where electrical energy converts to heat; in incandescent bulbs, this heat is the primary mechanism producing light, making them inefficient.

Takeaway: efficiency = useful light output ÷ electrical input × 100; compare bulbs of equal brightness and the lower-wattage one wins. Exam format: expect calculations of the percentage and comparisons between two bulbs; show the ratio before the percentage. Verification beat: the efficient bulb must produce the same brightness for fewer input watts — check both numbers before declaring a winner. Why students miss it: they compare wattage without brightness — a dim bulb is not efficient, it is merely dim; equality of brightness is the control that makes the comparison fair.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the formula for calculating the energy efficiency of a light bulb as a percentage?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Efficiency (%) = (Useful light energy output / Total electrical energy input) × 100%. A wrong answer like "Efficiency = Output power / Input power" without the percentage reveals the student recalls the ratio but forgets to express it as a percentage, which is standard for efficiency comparisons.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Two bulbs produce the same brightness: a 60 W incandescent bulb and a 14 W CFL. Which bulb is more efficient and why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The 14 W CFL is more efficient because it uses less electrical power (14 W vs 60 W) to produce the same amount of light. A wrong answer choosing the 60 W bulb reveals the misconception that higher power means better performance, confusing power consumption with light output.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
An incandescent bulb consumes 100 J of electrical energy and produces 5 J of light energy. Calculate its efficiency.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Identify given: Electrical energy input = 100 J, Light energy output = 5 J.
Step 2: Formula: Efficiency = (Light energy output / Electrical energy input) × 100%.
Step 3: Substitute: (5 J / 100 J) × 100%.
Step 4: Compute: 0.05 × 100% = 5%.
Answer: 5%. A wrong answer like 20% (from 100/5) reveals the student inverted the ratio, not understanding that efficiency is output over input.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
A household replaces five 60 W incandescent bulbs with five 12 W CFLs. If all bulbs are used for 5 hours daily, how much electrical energy (in kWh) is saved per day?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Step 1: Power saved per bulb = 60 W - 12 W = 48 W.
Step 2: Total power saved for 5 bulbs = 5 × 48 W = 240 W = 0.24 kW.
Step 3: Energy saved per day = Power × Time = 0.24 kW × 5 h = 1.2 kWh.
Answer: 1.2 kWh. A wrong answer of 1200 Wh (without converting to kWh) shows the student can compute energy but misses the required unit conversion, indicating a gap in unit awareness.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student claims: "A 100 W incandescent bulb is brighter than a 20 W CFL, so the incandescent bulb is more efficient." Evaluate this statement.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is incorrect. Efficiency compares light output for the same electrical input, or electrical input for the same light output. A 100 W incandescent and a 20 W CFL may have different brightness; we cannot judge efficiency without equalizing brightness. A 20 W CFL often matches a 100 W incandescent in brightness, making the CFL more efficient. A wrong agreement reveals the student equates higher power with higher efficiency, ignoring the need for a fair comparison.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple experiment to compare the efficiency of an incandescent bulb and a CFL using equipment available in a school lab (ammeter, voltmeter, lux meter, power supply).
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Connect each bulb to the power supply with ammeter in series and voltmeter in parallel. Measure voltage (V) and current (I) to calculate electrical power input (P = VI). Use the lux meter at a fixed distance to measure illuminance (lux) as a proxy for light output. Ensure both bulbs are at the same distance and orientation. Calculate efficiency ratio = (Illuminance / Electrical Power) for each; higher ratio means more efficient. A poor answer that only measures temperature or heat shows the student confuses heating effect with light output measurement.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students think a bulb with higher wattage is always "more powerful" and therefore better, not realizing wattage indicates electrical energy consumption rate, not light output. They believe a 100 W incandescent bulb is superior to a 20 W CFL because 100 > 20, ignoring that the CFL can produce similar brightness with far less energy.
## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Take two bulbs of similar brightness (e.g., 60 W incandescent and 14 W CFL) and light them side by side.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: An incandescent bulb rated 60 W is used for 4 hours. It produces light energy equivalent to 8 W of light power. Calculate the efficiency of the bulb and the total electrical energy consumed in kWh.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given quantities: Electrical power input = 60 W, Light power output = 8 W, Time = 4 h.
- Step 2: Identify formulas: Efficiency = (Light power output / Electrical power input) × 100%; Electrical energy = Power × Time.
- Step 3: Substitute values for efficiency: (8 W / 60 W) × 100% = 13.33%. For energy: 60 W × 4 h = 240 Wh = 0.24 kWh.
- Step 4: Compute with units: Efficiency = 0.1333 × 100% = 13.33%. Energy = 60 W × 4 h = 240 Wh = 0.24 kWh.
- Step 5: Verify reasonableness: Incandescent bulbs are typically 10-15% efficient, so 13.33% is plausible. 0.24 kWh is a reasonable daily consumption for one bulb.
REASONING: Efficiency compares the useful output (light) to the total input (electrical). Since both are power values (energy per second), the time cancels out. Total energy consumed is needed for cost estimation.
ANSWER: Efficiency = 13.33%, Electrical energy consumed = 0.24 kWh.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A CFL of 14 W gives the same brightness as a 60 W incandescent bulb. If both are used for 5 hours daily, how much electrical energy (in kWh) is saved in a month (30 days) by using the CFL?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given: Incandescent power = 60 W, CFL power = 14 W, Daily usage = 5 h, Days = 30.
- Step 2: Calculate power saved per bulb: 60 W - 14 W = 46 W.
- Step 3: Calculate daily energy saved: 46 W × 5 h = 230 Wh = 0.23 kWh.
- Step 4: Calculate monthly energy saved: 0.23 kWh/day × 30 days = 6.9 kWh.
- Step 5: Verify: 46 W saved for 5 hours is 230 Wh/day; times 30 is 6900 Wh = 6.9 kWh. Reasonable for one bulb replacement.
REASONING: Energy savings come from the difference in power consumption for the same light output. Multiplying by time gives energy, and converting to kWh matches electricity billing units.
ANSWER: 6.9 kWh saved per month.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A household has 8 incandescent bulbs of 60 W each, used 6 hours daily. They replace all with 12 W CFLs. If electricity costs ₹6 per kWh, calculate the monthly (30 days) cost savings.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Total incandescent power = 8 × 60 W = 480 W = 0.48 kW. Total CFL power = 8 × 12 W = 96 W = 0.096 kW.
- Step 2: Power saved = 0.48 kW - 0.096 kW = 0.384 kW.
- Step 3: Daily energy saved = 0.384 kW × 6 h = 2.304 kWh.
- Step 4: Monthly energy saved = 2.304 kWh/day × 30 days = 69.12 kWh.
- Step 5: Cost savings = 69.12 kWh × ₹6/kWh = ₹414.72.
- Step 6: Verify: 0.384 kW × 180 h (monthly) = 69.12 kWh. At ₹6, about ₹415. Reasonable for 8 bulbs.
REASONING: This combines efficiency comparison (power difference) with energy calculation and cost analysis, linking physics to real-world economics.
ANSWER: Monthly cost savings = ₹414.72.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Two bulbs, A (incandescent) and B (CFL), are connected to identical batteries in separate circuits. Bulb A draws 0.5 A at 12 V and produces 3 W of light. Bulb B draws 0.1 A at 12 V and produces 2.5 W of light. Which bulb is more efficient? Justify with calculations.
COMPETENCY: Interpreting physical situations using formulas and unit analysis.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate electrical power input for each: P = VI.
- Step 2: For A: P_A = 12 V × 0.5 A = 6 W. For B: P_B = 12 V × 0.1 A = 1.2 W.
- Step 3: Calculate efficiency: η_A = (3 W / 6 W) × 100% = 50%. η_B = (2.5 W / 1.2 W) × 100% = 208.3%.
- Step 4: Interpret: Efficiency >100% is impossible, indicating an error in given data or measurement. Re-check: If B produces 2.5 W light with 1.2 W input, that violates energy conservation. Therefore the data for B is inconsistent. Assuming a typo and B's light output is 0.6 W, then η_B = 50%. But with given numbers, the scenario is physically impossible.
ANSWER: Bulb A efficiency = 50%. Bulb B's given data implies 208% efficiency, which is impossible. The data for Bulb B must be incorrect (light output cannot exceed electrical input).
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A 15 W CFL and a 75 W incandescent bulb produce approximately the same brightness (1100 lumens). If the CFL lasts 8000 hours and the incandescent lasts 1000 hours, compare the total electrical energy consumed and number of bulbs needed for 8000 hours of operation. Which is more economical considering only electricity cost at ₹7/kWh? (Ignore bulb purchase price.)
COMPETENCY: Predicting outcomes from changing variables and multi-step quantitative reasoning.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Energy for CFL over 8000 h: 15 W × 8000 h = 120,000 Wh = 120 kWh.
- Step 2: Energy for incandescent over 8000 h: 75 W × 8000 h = 600,000 Wh = 600 kWh.
- Step 3: Number of incandescent bulbs needed: 8000 h / 1000 h = 8 bulbs. CFL: 1 bulb.
- Step 4: Electricity cost for CFL: 120 kWh × ₹7 = ₹840. For incandescent: 600 kWh × ₹7 = ₹4200.
- Step 5: Compare: CFL saves 480 kWh and ₹3360 in electricity over 8000 hours.
ANSWER: CFL consumes 120 kWh (1 bulb), incandescent consumes 600 kWh (8 bulbs). CFL saves ₹3360 in electricity cost.
DIFFICULTY: hard
