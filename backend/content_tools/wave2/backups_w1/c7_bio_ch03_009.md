## ELI5
The sweetness in a mango or a carrot was cooked in a leaf and delivered. Phloem is the plant's food-freight tissue, carrying dissolved sugars from the leaves - the source - to wherever they are needed or stored - the sink: roots, fruits, buds. Unlike the one-way water lift in xylem, phloem traffic runs in both directions. Peel a ring of bark off a branch and you cut the food road, not the water road.

## DEFINITION
Phloem is a specialized plant vascular tissue responsible for transporting dissolved sugars and organic food synthesized during photosynthesis from the leaves to all other parts of the plant, including roots, stems, and growing buds.

## WALKTHROUGH
When you bite into a sweet mango or a juicy carrot, you are tasting the sugars that were manufactured in the plant's leaves and transported to the fruit or root. If leaves are the food factories of the plant, how does that sugary food reach the underground roots that never see the sunlight? Building on our knowledge of how water travels through xylem, we now examine the second major highway inside a plant stem. By observing how ringed barks on trees or insect feeding reveals sticky sweet sap, we can uncover how phloem distributes nourishment to every living cell.

**Takeaway:** phloem is the plant's food freight: dissolved sugars move from source leaves to sinks - fruits, roots, buds - in either direction. **In the exam:** in comparison questions, split the twins - xylem's one-way water lift against phloem's two-way food run. **Check yourself:** taste the walkthrough's sweet mango and trace the sugar back up the freight route to a leaf. **Why students miss it:** students send food only downward - the flagged error; a growing bud above the leaf is a sink too, and the bark-cut tree is a no to the food road surviving. Below the ring, the branch swells with undelivered sugar - the freight pile-up made visible on the mango tree.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the primary substance transported by the phloem tissue in plants?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The primary substance transported by phloem is dissolved sugar (glucose/food) produced during photosynthesis. If a student answers "water and minerals", they are confusing phloem with xylem, missing the distinct functions of the two vascular tissues.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why is food transport in the phloem described as moving from a "source" to a "sink"?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The "source" is where food is made or stored (like mature leaves or storage roots), and the "sink" is where food is consumed or stored for growth (like developing fruits, root tips, and young buds). A wrong answer stating that food only flows upwards from roots reveals a misunderstanding of photosynthesis and plant anatomy.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A gardener peels a ring of bark off a mango tree branch, removing the phloem while leaving the inner wood intact. After a few weeks, the part of the branch just above the ring swells up and appears thick. Why does this swelling happen?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Steps: 1. Identify that removing the bark removes the phloem tissue while xylem remains untouched. 2. Recall that phloem transports food downwards from the leaves. 3. Apply this to the scenario: food manufactured in the leaves travels down until it hits the blocked ring. 4. Conclude that sugars accumulate above the cut because they cannot pass through to the lower part of the branch, causing swelling. If a student thinks the branch swelled because of trapped water, they are ignoring the function of phloem in food transport.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
How does the direction of transport in phloem differ fundamentally from transport in xylem?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Xylem transports water and minerals almost exclusively in one direction (upwards from roots to leaves), driven by transpiration pull. In contrast, phloem can transport food in multiple directions—upwards to growing buds and downwards to roots or tubers—depending on where food is made and where it is needed. A wrong answer claiming both tissues transport substances in the same direction ignores the distinct physiological needs of different plant organs.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student claims that during winter, when deciduous trees in northern India lose all their leaves, phloem transport completely stops throughout the entire plant. Evaluate this statement.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is false. While leaves (the primary source) are absent, plants store food in roots or stems (acting as temporary sources) during warmer months. In winter, this stored food can be mobilized and transported via phloem to support underground structures and prepare buds for spring, meaning phloem transport does not entirely cease. A wrong answer agreeing with the student shows a failure to recognize storage organs as alternative sources of food.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple, non-destructive observation method to prove that sugary sap flows through the outer layers of a plant stem rather than the center.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good response suggests observing sap-sucking insects like aphids, which insert their mouthparts specifically into the outer bark (phloem) to feed on sugary sap, or examining a ringing experiment where the bark is carefully notched without cutting deep wood. A poor answer involves cutting down the entire plant or destroying the roots, which fails to isolate the vascular layers.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that plant food (glucose) travels only downwards from the leaves to the roots, much like water flowing down a drain. They forget that plants also need to send food upwards to newly sprouting buds, flowers, and developing fruits at the very top of the plant canopy.
## ON_FAILURE
To fix this, draw a tall neem tree with leaves at the top, flowers in the middle, and roots at the bottom. Ask: "How do the flowers get their food if they are above the leaves?" Guide the student to trace arrows going both upwards and downwards from the leaves to show that phloem transport is multi-directional (source to any sink).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A farmer in Maharashtra notices that a beetle is feeding exclusively on the outer bark of a grapevine stem. Over time, the grapes hanging on the vine below the bite mark continue to grow, but the new shoots above the bite mark begin to wither. Explain why this happens using biological principles.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the tissue located in the outer bark of the stem as phloem.
- step 2: Recall the function of phloem, which is to transport sugars and food from photosynthetic leaves to other plant parts.
- step 3: Analyze the position of the beetle's bite relative to the leaves and the plant parts (grapes below, shoots above).
- step 4: Determine that if the bite disrupts the phloem, food can still travel downwards to the grapes if leaves are above them, but if the bite is below the leaves and blocks upward transport to new shoots, those shoots are starved of food.
- step 5: Conclude that the upper shoots withered because the disruption of phloem stopped the upward distribution of essential nutrients.
REASONING: Phloem transport is bidirectional, but a physical blockage interrupts the continuity of the transport stream. Depending on where the damage occurs relative to the source leaves and the sink organs, specific parts of the plant will be deprived of nourishment.
ANSWER: The upper shoots withered because the beetle damaged the phloem in the bark, cutting off the supply of food needed for growth, while lower organs still received food from intermediate pathways or stored reserves.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: During winter, a deciduous tree in a Delhi garden drops all its leaves. Yet, when spring arrives, the tree produces brand-new green leaves and flowers using stored energy. Which vascular tissue facilitated the movement of this stored food from the roots to the growing buds, and in what direction did the transport occur?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify that stored food reserves in plant roots or stems act as a food "source" when leaves are absent.
- step 2: Recognize that growing buds and flowers at the branch tips act as active "sinks" requiring energy.
- step 3: Identify the tissue responsible for moving dissolved food within the plant as phloem.
- step 4: Trace the pathway of food movement: from the underground roots (source) moving upwards through the stem into the branches and buds (sink).
- step 5: Conclude that phloem transported food in an upward direction during this specific seasonal phase.
REASONING: Phloem is not restricted to downward movement. When storage organs act as sources, phloem can transport nutrients upwards to support initial spring growth before new leaves take over photosynthesis.
ANSWER: Phloem tissue facilitated the movement; the transport occurred in an upward direction from the root storage source to the growing buds.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A potted Hibiscus plant is kept in a dark room for 48 hours to exhaust its starch reserves. Then, one single leaf is covered with black paper containing a cut-out star shape, while the rest of the plant is exposed to sunlight for a day. After testing the leaf for starch, scientists find that sugars are also detected in the stem just below the petiole of that specific leaf. Explain how the sugar reached the stem and what tissue was involved.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify that photosynthesis occurred only in the exposed star-shaped area of the leaf, creating a localized high concentration of glucose (source).
- step 2: Recall that excess glucose is converted and transported out of the leaf as dissolved sugars.
- step 3: Identify the vascular tissue responsible for moving sugars out of the leaf into the stem as phloem.
- step 4: Trace the movement of sugars from the leaf cells into the veins, down through the leaf stalk (petiole), and into the main stem.
- step 5: Conclude that phloem transport successfully carried the newly synthesized food out of the leaf into adjacent stem tissues.
REASONING: Photosynthesis creates food in specific cells (source), which then loads into the phloem network of the leaf veins and moves out to nourish the rest of the plant body (sink).
ANSWER: The sugar reached the stem via the phloem tissue, which transported the photosynthetic products manufactured in the illuminated parts of the leaf downward into the stem vascular bundles.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If a plant's roots are completely surrounded by soil packed tightly in a plastic pot that prevents any water flow, but leaves are healthy and photosynthesizing, will the roots immediately stop receiving food via the phloem? Explain your reasoning.
COMPETENCY: Interpreting cause-effect in life processes
STEPS:
- step 1: Understand that photosynthesis in leaves produces food, which is transported by phloem.
- step 2: Distinguish between water transport (xylem) and food transport (phloem).
- step 3: Analyze whether a lack of water uptake by roots immediately halts food transport from leaves to roots.
- step 4: Reason that while long-term survival requires water, existing food in transit can still reach roots via phloem until photosynthesis or transport machinery shuts down due to dehydration.
ANSWER: No, the roots will not immediately stop receiving food. Phloem transport is driven by sugar concentration gradients, so existing sugars in the leaves will continue to travel down to the roots via the phloem until the plant begins to suffer severe drought stress.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A plant biologist wants to study how rapidly food travels through a plant stem. They introduce a harmless fluorescent dye into a mature leaf and track how long it takes to reach the root tips versus a flower bud at the top of the plant. They notice the dye reaches both destinations at different times. What biological factors determine this differential travel time in the phloem?
COMPETENCY: Relating structure to function and predicting biological outcomes
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify that phloem transports dissolved substances from source to sink.
- step 2: Recognize that distance from the source leaf to different sinks (roots vs. top flower bud) varies.
- step 3: Consider metabolic demand—actively growing flower buds may pull food faster than mature roots.
- step 4: Conclude that distance and sink strength (metabolic demand) determine the speed and direction of phloem transport.
ANSWER: The travel time depends on the distance between the source leaf and the respective sink (root tip vs. flower bud), as well as the "sink strength" or metabolic demand of the receiving organ, with high-demand growing tissues drawing sugars more rapidly.
DIFFICULTY: hard
