## ELI5
Two quantities are in direct proportion when they rise together and fall together, with their ratio locked at one constant value. Share mangoes among children in the same measure and both sides of the fraction stay put: cost per kilo, juice per student, pages per day. Find that constant once — any pair will do — and the relationship is written as y equals k times x forever after. On a graph it draws a straight line through the origin, the constant riding the line as its slope.

## DEFINITION
Two quantities are in direct proportion if they increase or decrease together in such a way that the ratio of their corresponding values remains constant. Mathematically, if quantity \(y\) is directly proportional to quantity \(x\), then \(\frac{y}{x} = k\) (a constant) or equivalently \(y = kx\), where \(k\) is called the constant of proportionality.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can identify whether two quantities are in direct proportion by checking for a constant ratio from a table, graph, or verbal description, and can determine the constant of proportionality \(k\) to write the relationship as \(y = kx\).
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the definition of direct proportion, identification from tables, graphs, and word problems, and finding the constant of proportionality. It does NOT cover inverse proportion, compound proportion, or applications involving percentage changes (covered in later nodes).
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you go to a fruit market where apples cost ₹60 per kilogram. If you buy 1 kg, you pay ₹60; 2 kg costs ₹120; 3 kg costs ₹180. Notice that as the weight increases, the cost increases in exactly the same proportion — the ratio of cost to weight is always 60. This is the essence of direct proportion: two quantities change together so that their ratio stays fixed. We have already learnt in Class 6 and 7 how to simplify ratios and find equivalent ratios (c8_math_ch7_3, c8_math_ch7_4). Now we formalise this: if \(\frac{\text{cost}}{\text{weight}}\) is constant for every pair of values, the quantities are in direct proportion. The key insight is that the graph of such a relationship is a straight line passing through the origin, and the constant ratio is the slope of that line.

Takeaway: direct proportion means y/x = k for every pair — test a table by dividing, test a graph by checking a straight line through the origin, and name k to write y = kx. Exam format: expect 'identify and justify' from tables, graphs or words; the quotient test written for two pairs is the justification. Verification beat: confirm the graph would pass through the origin — a straight line elsewhere is not direct proportion. Why students miss it: they accept 'both increase' as proof; only the constant ratio distinguishes direct proportion from other rising relationships.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
State the mathematical condition for two quantities \(x\) and \(y\) to be in direct proportion. Write the equation that relates them using a constant \(k\).
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The condition is that the ratio \(\frac{y}{x}\) is constant for all corresponding values of \(x\) and \(y\). The equation is \(y = kx\) (or \(\frac{y}{x} = k\)). A wrong answer like "\(x + y = \text{constant}\)" or "\(y = k + x\)" reveals the student confuses direct proportion with additive relationships or inverse proportion. They may not grasp that "proportion" implies a multiplicative constant ratio, not a constant sum or difference.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
The table below shows values of \(x\) and \(y\). Explain why this relationship is NOT a direct proportion, even though both \(x\) and \(y\) increase.

| \(x\) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| \(y\) | 3 | 5 | 7 | 9 |
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
For direct proportion, \(\frac{y}{x}\) must be constant. Here \(\frac{3}{1}=3\), \(\frac{5}{2}=2.5\), \(\frac{7}{3}\approx 2.33\), \(\frac{9}{4}=2.25\) — the ratio changes. A student who says "it is direct proportion because both increase" holds the common misconception that any increasing relationship is proportional. They are not checking the constancy of the ratio, which is the defining property.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
The cost of 5 metres of cloth is ₹375. If the cost is directly proportional to the length, find the cost of 12 metres of cloth. Show your steps using the constant of proportionality.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Find constant \(k = \frac{\text{cost}}{\text{length}} = \frac{375}{5} = 75\) ₹/metre.  
Step 2: Write relation: \(\text{cost} = 75 \times \text{length}\).  
Step 3: For 12 metres, cost \(= 75 \times 12 = 900\).  
Answer: ₹900.  
A wrong answer like ₹9000 (multiplying 375 by 12/5 incorrectly) or ₹75 (just stating the unit rate) reveals computational errors or confusion between finding the constant and applying it. The student may not be systematically using \(y = kx\).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Two relationships are given:  
A) \(y = 4x\)  
B) \(y = 4x + 2\)  
Which one represents a direct proportion? Justify your answer by analysing the ratio \(\frac{y}{x}\) for \(x = 1, 2, 3\) in both cases.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Relationship A is direct proportion because \(\frac{y}{x} = 4\) for all \(x\). For B: when \(x=1\), \(\frac{y}{x}=6\); when \(x=2\), \(\frac{y}{x}=5\); when \(x=3\), \(\frac{y}{x}=4.67\) — not constant. A student who chooses B or says both are proportional does not understand that direct proportion requires the line to pass through the origin (zero intercept). They may focus only on the multiplicative term \(4x\) and ignore the additive constant.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student claims that the relationship between the side of a square and its area is a direct proportion because "as side increases, area increases". Evaluate this claim and explain the error.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The claim is false. Area \(A = s^2\), so \(\frac{A}{s} = s\), which is not constant — it changes with \(s\). The student's error is assuming that any "increase together" relationship is direct proportion. They are not testing the constant ratio condition. This reveals a confusion between monotonic increase and proportional increase.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a real-life scenario involving two quantities that are in direct proportion. Then create a second scenario where the two quantities increase together but are NOT in direct proportion. Explain how you would verify each.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Example of direct proportion: Distance travelled at constant speed vs time (ratio = speed, constant). Verification: check \(\frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}}\) for several pairs.  
Example of non-proportional increase: Total money in a piggy bank where you add ₹10 every week but started with ₹50. After \(w\) weeks, money = \(50 + 10w\). Ratio \(\frac{\text{money}}{w}\) changes. Verification: compute ratio for \(w=1,2,3\) and see it's not constant.  
A poor answer gives two proportional scenarios or cannot explain verification, showing the student cannot distinguish constant ratio from mere co-variation.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that if two quantities both increase (or both decrease), they are in direct proportion. They do not check whether the ratio \(\frac{y}{x}\) is actually constant. For example, given \(x: 1, 2, 3, 4\) and \(y: 2, 5, 10, 17\), a student may say "yes, direct proportion" because \(y\) increases as \(x\) increases. The exact wrong step is skipping the ratio test and relying only on the direction of change. They might also incorrectly compute the ratio as \(\frac{x}{y}\) instead of \(\frac{y}{x}\) and get a different constant, not realising the constant must be the same for all pairs in the chosen order.
## ON_FAILURE
1. Ask the student to compute \(\frac{y}{x}\) for every pair in the table and write the results in a new row.  
2. Have them compare the values: "Are they all the same number? If not, it's not direct proportion."  
3. Use a visual aid: plot the points on graph paper. If they don't lie on a straight line through (0,0), it's not direct proportion.  
4. Contrast with a clear proportional example (e.g., \(y=3x\)) and a non-proportional one (e.g., \(y=3x+2\)) side by side, highlighting the ratio row and the graph.  
5. Insist they always write "Ratio = y/x = ..." for at least three pairs before concluding.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A car travels at a constant speed of 60 km/h. The distance travelled is directly proportional to the time taken. Complete the table and find the constant of proportionality.

| Time (h) | 1 | 2 | 3 | 5 | ? |
| Distance (km) | 60 | 120 | 180 | ? | 420 |

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the given pair to find the constant ratio. From the table, when time = 1 h, distance = 60 km. So ratio \(k = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}} = \frac{60}{1} = 60\) km/h.
- Step 2: Verify the constant with another pair. For time = 2 h, distance = 120 km: \(\frac{120}{2} = 60\). For time = 3 h: \(\frac{180}{3} = 60\). The ratio is constant, confirming direct proportion.
- Step 3: Write the algebraic relationship: Distance = 60 × Time, or \(d = 60t\).
- Step 4: Find missing values. For time = 5 h: \(d = 60 \times 5 = 300\) km. For distance = 420 km: \(t = \frac{420}{60} = 7\) h.
- Step 5: Complete the table and state the constant of proportionality with units: \(k = 60\) km/h.
REASONING: The constant speed (60 km/h) is exactly the constant of proportionality. By checking multiple pairs we ensure the relationship is truly proportional. The equation \(d = 60t\) allows us to compute any missing value directly, which is more efficient than repeated addition.
ANSWER: Completed table: Time 5 h → 300 km; Distance 420 km → 7 h. Constant of proportionality = 60 km/h.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: The cost of 8 notebooks is ₹240. Assuming cost is directly proportional to the number of notebooks, find the cost of 15 notebooks. Also, how many notebooks can be bought for ₹450?

STEPS:
- Step 1: Find the constant of proportionality (cost per notebook). \(k = \frac{\text{total cost}}{\text{number of notebooks}} = \frac{240}{8} = 30\) ₹/notebook.
- Step 2: Write the direct proportion equation: Cost = 30 × Number of notebooks, i.e., \(C = 30n\).
- Step 3: For 15 notebooks, substitute \(n = 15\): \(C = 30 \times 15 = 450\). So cost = ₹450.
- Step 4: For ₹450, substitute \(C = 450\): \(450 = 30 \times n \Rightarrow n = \frac{450}{30} = 15\). So 15 notebooks.
- Step 5: Interpret: The same calculation answers both questions because the relationship is symmetric; the constant 30 links the two quantities in both directions.
REASONING: Finding the unit cost (₹30) is the unitary method step. Once we have \(k = 30\), the equation \(C = 30n\) models the direct proportion. Solving for either variable uses the same constant, demonstrating the bidirectional nature of proportional relationships.
ANSWER: Cost of 15 notebooks = ₹450; Number of notebooks for ₹450 = 15.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A recipe for 6 people requires 900 g of flour. The amount of flour is directly proportional to the number of people. If you have 2.5 kg of flour, what is the maximum number of people you can serve? (Assume you can serve fractional people for calculation, but answer in whole people.)

STEPS:
- Step 1: Convert units to be consistent. 2.5 kg = 2500 g.
- Step 2: Find the constant of proportionality (flour per person). \(k = \frac{900 \text{ g}}{6 \text{ people}} = 150\) g/person.
- Step 3: Write the equation: Flour (g) = 150 × Number of people, i.e., \(F = 150p\).
- Step 4: Substitute available flour: \(2500 = 150 \times p \Rightarrow p = \frac{2500}{150} = \frac{50}{3} \approx 16.67\).
- Step 5: Since we cannot serve a fraction of a person, the maximum whole number is 16 people. (Check: 16 people need \(150 \times 16 = 2400\) g = 2.4 kg, leaving 100 g unused.)
- Step 6: Verify the ratio: For 6 people, 900 g → ratio 150. For 16 people, 2400 g → ratio 150. Constant ratio holds.
REASONING: This problem combines unit conversion (kg to g), direct proportion modelling, and real-world interpretation (rounding down). The constant of proportionality (150 g/person) is the bridge between the two quantities. The algebraic step \(F = 150p\) generalises the unitary method and allows solving for either variable.
ANSWER: Maximum 16 people can be served (using 2.4 kg flour, 100 g left over).
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: The table below shows the number of hours a water pump runs and the volume of water pumped.  
| Hours (h) | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 |
| Volume (L) | 1000 | 2000 | 3100 | 4000 |
Is the volume directly proportional to the time? Justify your answer mathematically. If not, which data point is inconsistent and what should it be for direct proportion?

COMPETENCY: Analyzing relationships and justifying with mathematical evidence

STEPS:
- Step 1: Compute the ratio Volume/Time for each pair.
- Step 2: Check if all ratios are equal.
- Step 3: Identify the pair that breaks the pattern.
- Step 4: Calculate the correct volume for that time using the constant ratio from the other pairs.
ANSWER: Ratios: 1000/2 = 500, 2000/4 = 500, 3100/6 ≈ 516.67, 4000/8 = 500. The third pair (6 h, 3100 L) is inconsistent. For direct proportion with k=500 L/h, at 6 h the volume should be 500 × 6 = 3000 L. So the table is not in direct proportion; the error is at 6 hours.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A taxi charges a fixed fare of ₹50 for the first kilometre and ₹12 for each additional kilometre. Is the total fare directly proportional to the distance travelled? Explain your reasoning. If not, what change would make it directly proportional?

COMPETENCY: Mathematical modeling and reasoning about real-world constraints

STEPS:
- Step 1: Write the fare rule as an equation: Fare = 50 + 12×(distance - 1) for distance ≥ 1 km.
- Step 2: Simplify: Fare = 12×distance + 38.
- Step 3: Check if this fits \(y = kx\). Since there is a constant term (+38), the ratio Fare/Distance is not constant.
- Step 4: Compute ratio for 1 km (50/1=50), 2 km (62/2=31), 3 km (74/3≈24.67) — not constant.
- Step 5: To make it directly proportional, the fare must be ₹0 for 0 km and a constant rate per km for all km. So remove the fixed charge and charge a single rate per km (e.g., ₹12 per km from the start).
ANSWER: No, it is not directly proportional because the fare equation has a non-zero intercept (₹38), making the ratio fare/distance vary with distance. To make it proportional, the pricing must be a constant rate per kilometre with no fixed initial charge (i.e., fare = k × distance).
DIFFICULTY: hard
