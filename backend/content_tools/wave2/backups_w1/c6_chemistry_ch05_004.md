## ELI5
Watch a candle and you are watching matter transform: solid wax seems to disappear, but nothing vanishes — it is reacting with oxygen in the air, releasing the light you read by and the heat you feel. Combustion is this rapid partnership with oxygen, and it needs a triangle complete every time: fuel to burn, like wax or wood; oxygen from the surrounding air; and enough starting heat to light the spark. Remove any side and the flame dies — which is why a jar placed over a candle snuffs it out. The payoff is everywhere: cooking food, generating electricity, powering engines — all energy set free from stored fuel.

## DEFINITION
Combustion is a chemical change where a substance reacts rapidly with oxygen, releasing energy in the form of heat and light. It is an essential process that allows us to cook food, generate electricity, and power engines.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that combustion is merely "fire," rather than a chemical reaction involving oxygen. They may think that because the wax disappears, it is destroyed, rather than changing into invisible carbon dioxide and water vapour.
## ON_FAILURE
First, explain that in chemistry, "matter is never destroyed, only transformed." Use the candle example: point out that the wax is reacting with oxygen to become gas. Ask the student: "If the wax just vanished, where did the energy come from?" Guide them to see that the change is the source of the heat.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A student has a piece of charcoal, a box of matches, and a fan. List the three requirements for combustion and explain how each is met in this scenario.
STEPS:
- 1: Identify fuel (charcoal).
- 2: Identify oxygen (the air around us).
- 3: Identify heat (the burning match).
- 4: Explain the interaction: the match provides the heat to start the reaction between charcoal and oxygen.
REASONING: Combustion requires all three parts of the "triangle." Without one, the reaction remains dormant.
ANSWER: Fuel = Charcoal; Oxygen = Air; Heat = Match. All three must be present simultaneously.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: If a candle is burning and we blow on it gently, it continues to burn. If we blow too hard, it goes out. Why?
STEPS:
- 1: Identify the role of the breath (supplying oxygen).
- 2: Identify the role of the breath (cooling the temperature).
- 3: Analyze the "too hard" scenario (excessive cooling).
- 4: Conclude that the temperature dropped below the requirement for combustion.
REASONING: Combustion is a balance. While oxygen is needed, removing heat (the ignition source) stops the reaction.
ANSWER: Gentle air feeds oxygen; strong air removes heat, breaking the combustion triangle.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A campfire produces ash, smoke, and heat. Are all of these "products" of combustion?
STEPS:
- 1: Define combustion products (heat, light, gas).
- 2: Analyze ash (solid residue of non-combustible material).
- 3: Analyze smoke (particulate matter/unburnt fuel).
- 4: Analyze heat (true energy product).
- 5: Distinguish between energy products and waste products.
REASONING: Combustion produces energy, but the process is rarely perfect, leading to residues like ash and smoke.
ANSWER: Only heat and light are the primary energy products; ash and smoke are residues of the incomplete process.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You are camping and find that your wood is damp. It is very hard to light. Explain this using the concept of the combustion triangle.
COMPETENCY: Applying concepts to real-world problems.
STEPS:
- 1: Identify the missing or inhibited requirement.
- 2: Recognize that dampness absorbs heat.
- 3: Explain that heat is used to evaporate water instead of starting the combustion reaction.
- 4: Conclude that the "heat" requirement is not being met efficiently.
ANSWER: The dampness requires extra heat to evaporate the water, meaning the wood doesn't reach the temperature needed for combustion, effectively blocking the "heat" part of the triangle.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A forest fire is spreading. Firefighters often cut down a strip of trees in the path of the fire. Why does this work?
COMPETENCY: Analyzing relationships between components.
STEPS:
- 1: Identify the component of the combustion triangle being removed.
- 2: Recognize that trees act as fuel.
- 3: Explain that removing fuel creates a gap.
- 4: Conclude that the fire cannot jump the gap because there is no fuel to sustain the reaction.
ANSWER: By creating a gap, they are removing the "fuel" component from the fire's path. Without fuel, the combustion reaction cannot continue, stopping the spread of the fire.
DIFFICULTY: hard
