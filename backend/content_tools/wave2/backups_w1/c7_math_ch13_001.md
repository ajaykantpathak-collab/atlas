## ELI5
Ask a class 'How many students are in this room?' and every counter lands on the same single number; ask 'How many hours did each of you sleep?' and the answers scatter - and that scatter is the whole idea. A statistical question anticipates variability in its data and accounts for it; a non-statistical question expects one fixed, specific answer. Capital-of-India questions never vary; heights-of-many-students questions always do. Spot which kind you are asking, and the data work that follows picks itself.

## DEFINITION
A statistical question is a question that anticipates variability in the data related to the question and accounts for it in the answers, whereas a non-statistical question expects a single, fixed, and specific answer with no variability.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you walk into your Class 7 mathematics classroom and ask two different questions: "How many students are in this room right now?" and "How many hours of sleep did each student in this room get last night?" For the first question, you will count the heads and get one single, exact number that is the same for everyone. For the second question, you will likely get a list of different numbers—some slept 7 hours, some 8, some 9. This difference between a fixed answer and a range of varied answers is the foundation of data handling. In Class 6, you learned how to organise basic data and read bar graphs, but now we must understand how to frame questions that make data collection meaningful. The key insight is that statistical questions are designed to investigate variability across a group, while non-statistical questions seek a single factual lookup.

**Takeaway:** statistical questions expect a scatter of answers; non-statistical questions expect one fixed value. **In the exam:** before collecting anything, ask: would different people give different answers? Then classify and justify. **Check yourself:** run the sleep-hours question past the class and watch the answers scatter - that scatter is the classification. **Why students miss it:** students call a question statistical because many people guess differently at one fixed fact, confusing variation in guesses with variability in data. The definition cares about the data, not the guessers.

A student who incorrectly labels this as statistical thinks that because variability exists *somewhere* in the universe of school bags, any question mentioning a bag becomes statistical.

Classify the following question as statistical or non-statistical and justify your choice: "What was the highest daily maximum temperature recorded in New Delhi in the month of May?"
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The correct answer is that it is a non-statistical question. 
Steps:
1. Examine what the question asks for: it asks for a single maximum value (the highest temperature) out of all daily recordings in May.
2. Check for variability: while individual days in May had different temperatures, the question asks for only one specific summary number (the absolute maximum).
3. Conclude: Because there is only one correct single numerical answer for that specific record, it is non-statistical.
A student who says it is statistical is confusing "temperatures changing every day" with the final output of the question, which asks for a single unique value.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that any question containing numbers, measurements, or math words is automatically a "statistical question." For example, they might classify "How many sides does a triangle have?" or "What is 45 kilograms plus 12 kilograms?" as statistical questions because numbers are involved. They fail to understand that statistics requires *variability* across a set of responses, not just numerical values.
## ON_FAILURE
To fix this, guide the student to test a question using the "Two-Answer Test." Say to the student: "Before answering, ask yourself: Will everyone in the room give the *exact same* answer, or will people give *different* answers?" Have them test "What is your age?" (different answers = statistical) versus "How many days are in a week?" (same answer = non-statistical). Emphasize that statistics is the study of variability, so if there is no variation in the expected answers, it is not statistical.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Classify the question "How many goals did the school football team score in each of their 12 matches this season?" as statistical or non-statistical, and explain your reasoning by identifying the expected data.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Read the question carefully and identify the subject being investigated (goals scored by the school football team across multiple matches).
- Step 2: Determine whether the query expects a single fixed value or a set of multiple varying values. Since there are 12 different matches, the team likely scored different numbers of goals in each match (e.g., 2 in the first, 0 in the second, 3 in the third).
- Step 3: Check if the question anticipates and accounts for this variability ("in *each* of their 12 matches").
- Step 4: Conclude that because the question expects a collection of differing values across the matches rather than one single unchanging number, it is a statistical question.
REASONING: A statistical question must account for variability. Because a football team's performance changes from match to match, asking about each match creates a set of diverse data points rather than a single fixed fact.
ANSWER: Statistical question, because it anticipates and studies the variability in goal counts across 12 different matches.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Examine the question "What is the boiling point of pure water at standard atmospheric pressure?" Determine if it is statistical or non-statistical and justify your classification through step-by-step analysis.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Analyze the core subject of the question (the physical boiling point of pure water under standard conditions).
- Step 2: Recall or investigate scientific properties: pure water always boils at 100 degrees Celsius at sea level.
- Step 3: Check for potential variability among different samples: under identical standard conditions, every single drop of pure water will boil at exactly the same temperature.
- Step 4: Conclude that since there is only one fixed, universal correct answer with zero variation across observations, the question is non-statistical.
REASONING: Non-statistical questions yield a single deterministic value. Scientific constants and fixed historical facts do not produce varying data sets when measured under identical conditions.
ANSWER: Non-statistical question, because pure water has a fixed, invariant boiling point of 100 degrees Celsius under standard conditions.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A teacher asks her students: "On average, how many minutes do 7th-grade students spend walking to school every morning?" Is this a statistical question? Break down the components to prove why or why not, and explain what data would need to be collected.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the target population (7th-grade students) and the attribute being measured (minutes spent walking to school every morning).
- Step 2: Analyze individual student experiences: Does every 7th-grade student live the exact same distance from school and walk at the exact same speed? No, student A might live 5 minutes away, student B 20 minutes away, and student C might come by bus (0 minutes).
- Step 3: Recognize variability: The answers will vary greatly across the student group, producing a spread of numerical data.
- Step 4: Evaluate the phrasing: Although the question asks for an "average" (which is a single summary number), the underlying question requires collecting a varied set of data from many individuals to compute that average.
- Step 5: Conclude that any question requiring the collection of variable data from a group to analyze—even if it results in a summary statistic—is fundamentally rooted in a statistical question.
REASONING: Questions that necessitate gathering a distribution of different values from a group before performing calculations are statistical questions, as they acknowledge and rely on data variability.
ANSWER: Yes, it is a statistical question because it requires gathering varied travel times from multiple individual students to understand the group's behavior.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Your school cricket team played 10 matches this season. Consider these two questions:
1. "How many runs did the captain score in the 5th match?"
2. "What was the average number of runs scored by the team across all 10 matches?"
One of these questions requires statistical thinking while the other does not. Identify which is which, rewrite the non-statistical one so that it becomes statistical, and explain the mathematical transformation.
COMPETENCY: Transforming non-statistical inquiries into statistical inquiries by altering scope and variability
STEPS:
- Step 1: Evaluate Question 1 ("How many runs did the captain score in the 5th match?"). It asks about a specific single event for a specific individual, yielding one fixed number. This is non-statistical.
- Step 2: Evaluate Question 2 ("What was the average number of runs scored by the team across all 10 matches?"). It requires compiling data from 10 different matches where scores varied, making it statistical.
- Step 3: Take Question 1 and modify its scope to introduce variability across multiple matches or players. For example: "How many runs did the captain score in *each* of the 10 matches this season?"
- Step 4: Explain the transformation: By changing "the 5th match" to "each of the 10 matches," we shift from looking for a single isolated fact to investigating a distribution of varying scores across multiple games.
ANSWER: Question 1 is non-statistical (single fixed value), while Question 2 is statistical (requires analyzing varied match scores). To make Question 1 statistical, rewrite it as: "How many runs did the captain score in each of the 10 matches this season?" This shifts the focus from a single isolated data point to a complete data set showing performance variability.
DIFFICULTY: hard
