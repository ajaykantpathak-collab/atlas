## ELI5
Choosing an opening batsman from ten past scores is real data analysis in miniature: organise the runs into a frequency table, compute the mean for the central story, spot the mode for the habit, and study the spread to tell a steady forty from a flashy one. Then write the verdict in sentences - a number alone never answers 'whom do we send in?'. Organisation, representative values and graphs are the toolkit; the conclusion is the product.

## DEFINITION
Analysing real-world data sets involves examining collected numbers, measurements, or observations to uncover underlying trends, patterns, and insights that help answer practical questions. It requires combining data organisation, representative values (mean, median, mode), and visual representations to draw meaningful, evidence-based conclusions.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are the captain of your school cricket team, and you need to decide which opening batsman to send out for a crucial match based on their last 10 scores. Where do you begin? You start by organising their run counts into a frequency table, calculating their average (mean) runs, finding their most frequent score (mode), and checking the middle score (median). By connecting these different statistical tools, you stop looking at isolated numbers and start seeing a complete picture of player consistency. The key insight is that no single representative value tells the whole story; a true data detective looks at the spread, the average, and the peaks together to solve real-world puzzles.

**Takeaway:** analysing data is a pipeline: organise, summarise with representative values, display, then conclude in sentences. **In the exam:** compute the statistic the question needs, inspect the spread for consistency, and write the verdict in full sentences. **Check yourself:** run the batsman's ten scores through table, mean and spread, and name the opener with one reason. **Why students miss it:** students stop at the number - the average - and never ask what the spread says about bad days, or whom the number crowns. Two players can share a mean while one is steady and the other a lottery; analysis exists to tell them apart.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If the mean test score of a class of 30 students is 45, and another class of 20 students has a mean test score of 50, why is it incorrect to say that the overall mean score for all 50 students is simply (45 + 50) / 2?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The correct answer is that the classes have different numbers of students, so their weights in the overall average are unequal. ## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A school canteen sold the following numbers of samosas over 5 days: 35, 42, 35, 50, 38. Calculate the mean daily sale of samosas.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The correct answer is 40 samosas. 
Steps: 
1. Sum all values: 35 + 42 + 35 + 50 + 38 = 200. 
2. Count the number of days: n = 5. 
3. Divide the sum by the count: Mean = 200 / 5 = 40. 
Students who miscalculate often miscount the number of data points or make an arithmetic error in addition, yielding values like 38 or 41.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Two different cricket players have the exact same mean score of 40 runs across 5 innings. Player A's scores are [38, 40, 42, 39, 41] while Player B's scores are [5, 10, 40, 65, 80]. How can an analyst determine which player is more reliable despite identical means?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The correct answer is by examining the spread or range of the data. Students who focus only on the mean will incorrectly conclude that both players are equally good choices.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students frequently look at an isolated data point or a single statistic like the mode when analysing a real-world data set, completely ignoring sample size and data spread. For example, if a weather station records temperatures of 42°C, 42°C, 42°C, 42°C, and 20°C, a struggling student might state that 42°C is the only important temperature because it is the mode, ignoring how the outlier (20°C) drastically shifts the mean and median.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A local fruit vendor recorded his daily mango sales (in kilograms) over 6 days as follows: 25, 30, 25, 45, 60, 25. As a data detective, analyse this data by calculating the mean, median, and mode, and explain which measure best represents his typical daily sale.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify all given data points and total number of days ($n = 6$). Data set: {25, 30, 25, 45, 60, 25}.
- step 2: Calculate the mean by summing all values and dividing by 6. Sum = 25 + 30 + 25 + 45 + 60 + 25 = 210. Mean = 210 / 6 = 35 kg.
- step 3: Find the median by first arranging the data in ascending order: {25, 25, 25, 30, 45, 60}. Since $n=6$ (even), the median is the average of the 3rd and 4th terms. 3rd term = 25, 4th term = 30. Median = (25 + 30) / 2 = 27.5 kg.
- step 4: Find the mode by identifying the most frequently occurring value. The number 25 appears 3 times, so Mode = 25 kg.
- step 5: Interpret the findings. The mode (25 kg) and median (27.5 kg) reflect the vendor's everyday baseline sales better than the mean (35 kg), which is pulled upward by the single high sale of 60 kg.
REASONING: Calculating all three central tendencies allows an analyst to look beyond a single distorted average. Because there is an outlier (60 kg), the mean is higher than most daily sales, making the median or mode a more honest representation of a normal day.
ANSWER: Mean = 35 kg, Median = 27.5 kg, Mode = 25 kg; Mode or Median best represents typical daily sales.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Two sections of Class 7 at a school in Delhi recorded their weekly electricity consumption (in kilowatt-hours, kWh) for 5 weeks. Section A: {45, 50, 48, 52, 55}, Section B: {30, 40, 50, 60, 70}. Both sections have a mean consumption of 50 kWh. Compare their consistency as a data detective.
STEPS:
- step 1: Note the given data for Section A {45, 50, 48, 52, 55} and Section B {30, 40, 50, 60, 70}, noting that both have a pre-calculated mean of 50 kWh.
- step 2: Calculate the range for Section A to measure spread: Maximum value (55) - Minimum value (45) = 10 kWh.
- step 3: Calculate the range for Section B to measure spread: Maximum value (70) - Minimum value (30) = 40 kWh.
- step 4: Compare the medians. Section A arranged: {45, 48, 50, 52, 55} -> Median = 50 kWh. Section B arranged: {30, 40, 50, 60, 70} -> Median = 50 kWh.
- step 5: Conclude based on analysis. Although both sections share the same mean and median (50 kWh), Section A is much more consistent because its range is only 10 kWh, whereas Section B has wide fluctuations with a range of 40 kWh.
REASONING: When two data sets share the same mean and median, an analyst must look at the range and spread. A smaller range indicates stable, predictable patterns, while a larger range reveals high volatility.
ANSWER: Section A is more consistent (Range = 10 kWh) compared to Section B (Range = 40 kWh), despite identical means of 50 kWh.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A NGO surveyed monthly household incomes (in thousands of rupees) in a small colony: {12, 15, 14, 13, 15, 95}. The NGO director claims the average income of the colony is 27.5 thousand rupees. As a data detective, investigate if this claim is misleading and justify your reasoning using statistical measures.
STEPS:
- step 1: List the data set and count the observations: {12, 15, 14, 13, 15, 95}, $n = 6$.
- step 2: Compute the mean income as claimed: Sum = 12 + 15 + 14 + 13 + 15 + 95 = 165. Mean = 165 / 6 = 27.5 thousand rupees.
- step 3: Arrange the data in ascending order to find the median: {12, 13, 14, 15, 15, 95}. The 3rd and 4th terms are 14 and 15. Median = (14 + 15) / 2 = 14.5 thousand rupees.
- step 4: Identify the mode of the data set: The number 15 appears twice, so Mode = 15 thousand rupees.
- step 5: Analyse the discrepancy and evaluate the director's claim. The value 95 is an extreme outlier (a wealthy household), which pulls the mean up to 27.5 thousand rupees. However, 5 out of 6 families earn 15 thousand rupees or less. Therefore, the director's claim using the mean is misleading; the median (14.5 thousand) or mode (15 thousand) accurately reflects the typical household income.
REASONING: Outliers severely distort the arithmetic mean in skewed real-world data sets. A thorough data analysis requires checking the median and mode to ensure that conclusions are not hijacked by extreme values.
ANSWER: The director's claim is misleading because the outlier (95) inflates the mean to 27.5; the true typical income is better represented by the median of 14.5 thousand rupees.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A local store tracked the daily number of umbrellas sold during the monsoon season: {2, 3, 2, 15, 3, 2, 4}. The store manager wants to stock inventory for next month based on this data. (a) Calculate the mean, median, and mode. (b) If the manager uses the mean to decide how many umbrellas to stock every day, explain why this will lead to a poor business decision.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world data sets into practical business decisions and evaluating statistical measures.
STEPS:
- step 1: Organise the data in ascending order: {2, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4, 15}, $n = 7$.
- step 2: Calculate the mean: Sum = 2 + 2 + 2 + 3 + 3 + 4 + 15 = 31. Mean = 31 / 7 $\approx$ 4.43 umbrellas.
- step 3: Find the median (4th term in sorted list) = 3 umbrellas. Find the mode (most frequent) = 2 umbrellas.
- step 4: Evaluate business impact. The mean is 4.43 due to one heavy rain day (15 umbrellas). On 6 out of 7 days, sales were 4 or fewer, and on most days it was 2. Stocking 4-5 umbrellas every day will cause overstocking on normal days and tie up capital unnecessarily.
ANSWER: (a) Mean $\approx$ 4.43, Median = 3, Mode = 2. (b) Using the mean is poor because the outlier (15) skews the average upward; the mode (2) or median (3) shows that daily demand is typically much lower.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Two student runners, Amit and Rahul, recorded their 100-metre sprint times (in seconds) over 6 trials. Amit: {14.2, 14.5, 14.3, 14.6, 14.4, 14.2}. Rahul: {12.0, 16.5, 13.0, 17.0, 13.5, 18.0}. Both boys have the exact same mean time of 14.375 seconds. The school sports coach must select one runner for the inter-school finals where consistency under pressure wins races. As lead data analyst, construct a recommendation with statistical justification.
COMPETENCY: Comparative data analysis, evaluating consistency, and handling data sets with identical means but differing spreads.
STEPS:
- step 1: Review given means for Amit and Rahul (both equal to 14.375 seconds).
- step 2: Calculate the range and spread for Amit: Max (14.6) - Min (14.2) = 0.4 seconds. Observe that Amit's times cluster tightly around his mean.
- step 3: Calculate the range and spread for Rahul: Max (18.0) - Min (12.0) = 6.0 seconds. Observe that Rahul's times fluctuate wildly between very fast and very slow trials.
- step 4: Evaluate performance stability in a competitive real-world context. Even though Rahul occasionally runs a brilliant 12.0s, his extreme unreliability (range of 6 seconds) makes him a risky choice for a final race. Amit guarantees a predictable performance around 14.3 seconds every time.
- step 5: Formulate final recommendation. Recommend Amit because his extremely low range (0.4s) proves high consistency, whereas Rahul's data shows erratic performance despite having the same average.
ANSWER: Recommend Amit. Although both have an average time of 14.375s, Amit's range is only 0.4s (highly consistent), whereas Rahul's range is 6.0s (unreliable and unpredictable).
