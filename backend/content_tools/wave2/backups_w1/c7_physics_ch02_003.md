## ELI5
The quiet hero of the TV remote: an electric cell, a sealed can of chemistry. Inside, reactions push charge to the terminals, holding a small potential difference - measured in volts - ready to drive current the moment a circuit invites it. Chemical energy in, electrical energy out, at your wall clock's pace. When the clock slows and stops, the chemistry is spent; fresh cells restore the push.

## DEFINITION
An electric cell is a device that converts chemical energy stored within it into electrical energy, serving as a portable source of electric current for various circuits. It maintains a potential difference between its two terminals (positive and negative), which drives electric charge through a conductor.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
Can identify the positive and negative terminals of a dry cell, explain how chemical energy transforms into electrical energy inside it, and predict the correct orientation of cells needed to power a simple device like a torchlight.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers the internal structure, energy conversion mechanism, and terminal identification of a standard dry electric cell. It does not cover electrochemical equations, advanced battery chemistry, or alternating current generation.
## WALKTHROUGH
When you look at the TV remote control or a wall clock at home, it stops working when the batteries run out, and inserting new cells brings it back to life. To understand how this happens, we examine a standard dry cell, which has a metal cap representing the positive terminal and a flat metal base representing the negative terminal. Inside this sealed cylinder, chemical reactions take place silently without any moving parts. These reactions create a continuous push that drives electric current through any connected circuit path. Building on the basic ideas of electric circuits learned earlier, the cell acts as the "heart" that pumps electricity through the wires. We measure its output using electrical parameters, but fundamentally, it is an energy converter that turns chemicals into electric current.

**Takeaway:** an electric cell is portable chemistry: reactions maintain a potential difference that drives current when a circuit closes. **In the exam:** name the energy conversion and the unit (volt) in the same breath when defining a cell. **Check yourself:** check the walkthrough's wall clock: what does a slowing hand say about the chemistry inside? **Why students miss it:** students call the cell a storehouse of electricity - the flagged metaphor. It stores chemical energy and generates electrical flow on demand. Say 'converts', and the definition - and the voltmeter - both agree with you.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A science student connects a small bulb across a single 1.5 V electric cell using copper wires. If the total resistance of the circuit results in a current of 0.3 A flowing for 10 seconds, what is the total charge that passes through the circuit?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Steps:
1. Identify given quantities: Current ($I$) = 0.3 A, Time ($t$) = 10 s.
2. Recall the relationship between charge ($Q$), current ($I$), and time ($t$): $Q = I \times t$.
3. Substitute the values with units into the formula: $Q = 0.3 \text{ A} \times 10 \text{ s}$.
4. Compute the product and track unit cancellation (A $\times$ s = C): $Q = 3.0 \text{ C}$.
5. Verify the answer: A charge of 3.0 Coulombs is physically reasonable for a small circuit operating over 10 seconds.
Answer: 3.0 Coulombs (C). A wrong answer usually stems from multiplying time by voltage or dividing current by time, showing confusion among fundamental electrical quantities.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think that an electric cell stores ready-made "electricity" or "electrons" inside it as a fluid, rather than understanding that it stores chemical potential energy which is converted into electrical energy when a complete circuit allows charges to move. For instance, they believe shaking a dead battery can generate more electric current by rearranging trapped electricity, confusing mechanical motion with chemical exhaustion.
## ON_FAILURE
To fix this misconception, take a discharged dry cell and have a teacher carefully cut it open (or show a safe cross-section diagram) to reveal the zinc container, carbon rod, and moist chemical paste inside. Explain that these active chemicals react with each other to produce the push on charges, and once those chemicals are fully transformed into different substances, the chemical energy is gone. Emphasize that no amount of physical shaking can recreate those original active chemicals.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A science kit circuit uses a fresh 1.5 V electric cell that drives a constant current of 0.25 A through a small LED indicator for 2 minutes. Calculate the total electric charge that flows through the circuit during this time.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given values with units: Current ($I$) = 0.25 A, Time ($t$) = 2 minutes. Convert time into SI units (seconds): $2 \times 60 \text{ s} = 120 \text{ s}$.
- Step 2: Identify the required formula relating charge, current, and time: $Q = I \times t$.
- Step 3: Substitute the numerical values and units into the formula: $Q = 0.25 \text{ A} \times 120 \text{ s}$.
- Step 4: Compute the product and track unit cancellation (A $\times$ s = C): $Q = 30 \text{ C}$.
- Step 5: Verify that 30 Coulombs is a physically sensible charge for a small LED circuit running for 2 minutes. State the final answer with units: 30 C.
REASONING: Electric charge is defined as the product of steady current and the duration of time that current flows. Converting time from minutes to seconds is essential because the SI unit of time is the second, ensuring correct unit cancellation when multiplying by Amperes (Coulombs per second).
ANSWER: 30 C
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: In a classroom experiment at a school in Delhi, students measure that a total charge of 45 C passes through a wire connected to an electric cell over a time span of 3 minutes. Determine the magnitude of the electric current flowing through the circuit.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given values with units: Charge ($Q$) = 45 C, Time ($t$) = 3 minutes. Convert time into seconds: $3 \times 60 \text{ s} = 180 \text{ s}$.
- Step 2: Rearrange the charge formula $Q = I \times t$ to solve for current ($I$): $I = \frac{Q}{t}$.
- Step 3: Substitute the numerical values with units into the rearranged equation: $I = \frac{45 \text{ C}}{180 \text{ s}}$.
- Step 4: Perform the division and track unit cancellation ($\text{C/s} = \text{A}$): $I = 0.25 \text{ A}$.
- Step 5: Verify that 0.25 A is a reasonable current for a school cell circuit. State the final answer with units: 0.25 A.
REASONING: Current is the rate of flow of electric charge. By dividing the total charge by the time taken in seconds, we find how many Coulombs pass per second, which gives the current in Amperes.
ANSWER: 0.25 A
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Two identical electric cells are tested in an experimental setup. Cell A operates a circuit with a current of 0.4 A for 50 seconds, while Cell B operates a circuit with a current of 0.5 A for 40 seconds. Which cell transfers a greater total electric charge, and by how much?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate the charge transferred by Cell A using $Q_A = I_A \times t_A$. Substitute values: $Q_A = 0.4 \text{ A} \times 50 \text{ s} = 20 \text{ C}$.
- Step 2: Calculate the charge transferred by Cell B using $Q_B = I_B \times t_B$. Substitute values: $Q_B = 0.5 \text{ A} \times 40 \text{ s} = 20 \text{ C}$.
- Step 3: Compare the two calculated charges: $Q_A = 20 \text{ C}$ and $Q_B = 20 \text{ C}$.
- Step 4: Compute the difference between the two charges: $\Delta Q = 20 \text{ C} - 20 \text{ C} = 0 \text{ C}$.
- Step 5: Verify the computations and state the final comparison clearly with units.
REASONING: Although Cell A and Cell B operate with different current magnitudes and time durations, their products of current and time ($0.4 \times 50$ and $0.5 \times 40$) are both equal to 20. Thus, both cells transfer the exact same total amount of electric charge.
ANSWER: Both cells transfer the same amount of charge (20 C each); the difference is 0 C.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A student reverses the orientation of one of the two electric cells inside a torchlight so that both positive terminals face each other. Explain what happens to the electric current and why the torch fails to glow.
COMPETENCY: Predicting outcomes from changing physical arrangements and understanding terminal polarity.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Analyze the normal orientation required for cells in series (positive of one connects to negative of the next).
- Step 2: Identify the conflict caused by facing two positive terminals together (opposing potential differences cancel each other out).
- Step 3: Conclude that the net voltage across the circuit drops to zero.
- Step 4: Explain that without a net potential difference, no electric current flows through the lamp filament, so it does not glow.
ANSWER: The torch fails to glow because facing the positive terminals toward each other creates opposing potential differences that cancel out the driving force. With zero net voltage, no electric current flows through the lamp circuit.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A household device requires an energy source that maintains a constant unidirectional push of electric charge. A student proposes using a mechanical hand-crank generator or a chemical electric cell interchangeably. Compare how both devices function as sources of electric current in terms of their initial energy storage and conversion mechanism.

- Step 1: Identify the primary energy storage of an electric cell (stored chemical energy inside chemical pastes) versus a hand-crank generator (mechanical work input from human muscles).
- Step 2: Explain the conversion process for the cell: chemical energy changes directly into electrical energy via internal reactions.
- Step 3: Explain the conversion process for the generator: mechanical energy changes into electrical energy via electromagnetic induction when rotated.
