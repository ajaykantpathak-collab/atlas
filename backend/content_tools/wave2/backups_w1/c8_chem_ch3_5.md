## ELI5
In the salt pans of Gujarat, shallow ponds of seawater sit in the sun until the water quietly slips away as vapour and piles of white salt stay behind. That is evaporation — a liquid turning to vapour below its boiling point, leaving dissolved solids stranded, because the salt's own boiling point is far too high to follow. Catch that vapour on a cool surface and it loses heat, slows down and turns back into liquid — condensation, evaporation's reverse twin.

## DEFINITION
Evaporation is the physical process by which a liquid solvent transitions into its vapor state (at any temperature below its boiling point), leaving behind dissolved, non-volatile solid solutes. Condensation is the reverse physical process in which a gaseous vapor loses thermal energy and transitions back into its liquid state.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the separation of homogeneous mixtures containing a non-volatile soluble solid (such as salt or sugar) and a volatile liquid solvent (such as water) using evaporation and simple condensation. It does not cover fractional distillation of miscible liquids, crystallization of heat-sensitive compounds (such as copper sulfate decomposition), or separation of immiscible liquids.
## WALKTHROUGH
Think about how salt is harvested from seawater in the salt pans of Gujarat or Tamil Nadu. The clear seawater is trapped in shallow clay ponds, and over time, the liquid water disappears, leaving behind piles of white, solid salt. To understand this process, we must first observe that when salt dissolves in water, it forms a homogeneous mixture where the salt particles are broken down to a molecular level and cannot be filtered out. When heated by the sun (or a burner in a lab), the liquid water molecules gain kinetic energy and escape into the air as water vapor—a process called evaporation. Because the salt has an extremely high boiling point, it remains behind as a solid residue. If we want to capture the escaping water vapor, we must direct it to a cooler surface, where the vapor molecules lose thermal energy, slow down, and turn back into liquid water—a process called condensation.

Takeaway: evaporation and condensation are a round trip — heat the salt solution and water leaves as vapour while salt stays; cool the vapour on a surface and it returns as pure liquid — separation by boiling-point differences, nothing chemical. Exam format: expect the procedure designed step by step and state transitions predicted. Verification beat: say which component is volatile and which is left behind. Why students miss it: they lose the recovered water — condensation is evaporation's partner, and naming both products completes the design.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Name the physical change of state that occurs when water vapor turns back into liquid water droplets on a cold glass plate.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Condensation. 

*What a wrong answer reveals:* If a student writes "evaporation" or "melting," they are confusing the direction of the phase change (gas-to-liquid vs. liquid-to-gas or solid-to-liquid). If they write "precipitation," they are using meteorological terms instead of the specific chemical physical change term.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why is simple filtration ineffective at separating dissolved salt from water, whereas evaporation works perfectly? Explain in terms of particle size.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
When salt dissolves in water, it breaks down into individual ions ($Na^+$ and $Cl^-$) which are extremely small (less than 1 nanometer in size) and easily pass through the microscopic pores of filter paper. Evaporation works because it relies on the difference in volatility: water easily transitions into a gas at boiling temperatures, while salt remains a solid due to its extremely high boiling point.

*What a wrong answer reveals:* If the student says "filtration doesn't work because salt is liquid when dissolved," they misunderstand solubility, falsely believing that dissolving changes the state of the solute to a liquid rather than dispersing it as microscopic particles in a solvent.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A student is given $100\text{ mL}$ of a muddy salt-water mixture. Outline the sequence of two different separation methods required to obtain dry, clean salt.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
1. **Step 1 (Filtration):** Pass the muddy salt-water mixture through filter paper. The insoluble mud particles will be trapped on the filter paper as residue, while the clear salt-water solution passes through as the filtrate.
2. **Step 2 (Evaporation):** Heat the clear salt-water filtrate in an evaporating dish. The water will evaporate into the air as steam, leaving behind dry, white crystals of common salt ($NaCl$) in the dish.

*What a wrong answer reveals:* If a student suggests evaporating the muddy salt-water directly, they fail to realize that both mud and salt are non-volatile, meaning they would end up with a dirty, unusable mixture of salt and mud. This shows a lack of systematic planning in separation schemes.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the boiling of a salt solution to the natural evaporation of seawater in a salt pan. Identify one similarity and one key difference between these two processes.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
*   **Similarity:** In both processes, liquid water undergoes a physical change to become water vapor, leaving the non-volatile dissolved salt behind.
*   **Difference:** Boiling is a rapid process that occurs throughout the entire volume of the liquid at a specific temperature ($100^\circ\text{C}$ for pure water) using artificial heating, whereas natural evaporation is a slow surface phenomenon that occurs at any temperature below the boiling point, driven by solar energy and wind.

*What a wrong answer reveals:* If a student states that boiling produces salt but natural evaporation does not, they do not understand that the chemical mechanism of water loss is identical in both cases, differing only in rate and temperature.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student wants to recover both pure water and dry salt from a salt-water mixture. They set up an open beaker of salt-water and boil it until only dry salt is left. Have they successfully achieved their goal? Evaluate their method and suggest a correction if needed.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, they have only partially succeeded. While they recovered dry salt, they lost all the water because it escaped into the atmosphere as water vapor. To recover the water as well, they must perform the evaporation in a closed system (like a distillation flask) connected to a condenser. The condenser will cool the escaping water vapor, causing it to undergo condensation and drip into a separate collection flask as pure liquid water.

*What a wrong answer reveals:* If the student says "Yes, the method is correct," they are overlooking the requirement to recover the *liquid solvent* (water), indicating they do not realize that once vapor escapes into the open air, it cannot be collected.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design an experiment using everyday household items to demonstrate both evaporation and condensation to a younger sibling.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
1. **Materials:** A glass mug, hot water, a spoonful of salt, a small metal plate, and ice cubes.
2. **Procedure:** Dissolve the salt in the hot water inside the mug. Place the metal plate over the top of the mug as a lid, and place a few ice cubes on top of the plate.
3. **Observation of Evaporation:** The hot salt-water will produce invisible water vapor rising up inside the mug.
4. **Observation of Condensation:** The rising water vapor will hit the cold underside of the metal plate (cooled by the ice), lose heat, and form clear liquid water droplets that cling to the plate. Tasting these droplets will reveal they are fresh, not salty, proving the salt stayed behind.

*What a poor answer reveals:* A poor answer might lack a cooling mechanism (like ice) to force condensation, or fail to explain how the experiment proves that the salt was separated from the water, showing a lack of understanding of the role of temperature differentials in condensation.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
A very common misconception is that when a solution is heated, the dissolved solute (like salt) evaporates along with the solvent, or that the salt "disappears" or is destroyed by the heat. Students often think that the steam coming off a boiling salt-water pot must taste salty. In reality, ionic compounds like sodium chloride ($NaCl$) have extremely strong ionic bonds and require temperatures over $1400^\circ\text{C}$ to vaporize, whereas water ($H_2O$) only requires $100^\circ\text{C}$ to boil. Thus, only the water transitions into the gas phase.
## ON_FAILURE
To correct this misconception, perform a simple "steam taste-test" demonstration. Boil a highly concentrated salt-water solution in a beaker. Place a clean, cold glass lid diagonally over the beaker to collect the condensed steam. Collect the dripping condensate in a clean spoon and let the student taste it once cooled. They will observe that the condensed water is completely fresh and tasteless. This direct sensory feedback proves that the salt remains entirely in the boiling beaker and does not travel with the water vapor.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A student in Chennai accidentally spills $10\text{ g}$ of table salt ($NaCl$) into a beaker containing $200\text{ mL}$ of water. They need to recover all $10\text{ g}$ of the dry salt. Design a step-by-step laboratory procedure to achieve this, explaining the physical changes taking place at each stage.
STEPS:
- **Step 1: Analyze the mixture.** Identify the components: Salt ($NaCl$) is a non-volatile soluble solid, and water ($H_2O$) is a volatile liquid solvent. They form a homogeneous solution.
- **Step 2: Set up the apparatus.** Pour the salt solution into an evaporating dish placed on a wire gauze over a tripod stand. Position a Bunsen burner beneath it.
- **Step 3: Apply heat (Evaporation).** Light the burner. Heat the solution to its boiling point. As the water absorbs heat energy, the liquid water molecules overcome their intermolecular forces and escape as water vapor:
  $$H_2O(l) \xrightarrow{\text{heat}} H_2O(g)$$
- **Step 4: Reduce heat near dryness.** As the water volume decreases drastically, reduce the flame to prevent the salt crystals from "spitting" or popping out of the dish due to trapped steam.
- **Step 5: Cool and weigh.** Allow the dish to cool down. Weigh the remaining dry, white solid. Verify that $10\text{ g}$ of solid $NaCl$ residue remains.
REASONING: Water has a boiling point of $100^\circ\text{C}$, whereas sodium chloride has a boiling point of $1413^\circ\text{C}$. Heating the solution easily vaporizes the water while leaving the salt completely unaffected chemically and physically, allowing $100\%$ recovery of the non-volatile solute.
ANSWER: Place the salt solution in an evaporating dish, heat it until all the water vaporizes as steam, and collect the dry $NaCl$ solid remaining in the dish.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A survivalist on a beach near Puri needs to obtain clean drinking water from brackish pond water containing dissolved salts and suspended sand particles. Design a multi-step separation process to obtain pure liquid water.
STEPS:
- **Step 1: Identify impurities.** The mixture contains suspended sand (insoluble solid), dissolved salts (soluble solids), and water (liquid solvent).
- **Step 2: Remove insoluble impurities (Filtration).** Pass the mixture through a clean cloth filter or sand-and-charcoal filter column. The suspended sand is trapped as residue, leaving a clear salt-water filtrate.
- **Step 3: Vaporize the water (Evaporation).** Pour the clear filtrate into a kettle or covered pot with a spout. Heat the pot over a fire. The water turns to steam ($H_2O(g)$), leaving the dissolved salts behind in the pot.
- **Step 4: Collect and cool the steam (Condensation).** Direct the steam coming out of the spout toward a cold surface, such as a slanted metal plate held over a clean cup.
- **Step 5: Retrieve fresh water.** As the hot steam hits the cold metal plate, it loses heat energy, condenses back into liquid water droplets ($H_2O(l)$), and slides down into the cup.
REASONING: Suspended sand is separated mechanically via filtration based on particle size. The dissolved salt and water are separated based on volatility: water evaporates at a low temperature, and its vapor is recaptured and turned back into pure liquid via condensation, leaving the salt behind.
ANSWER: Filter the mixture to remove sand, boil the clear filtrate in a closed kettle, and condense the escaping steam on a cold plate to collect pure drinking water in a separate cup.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A chemist has a $250\text{ mL}$ solution of copper(II) sulfate ($CuSO_4$) in water. They want to separate this homogeneous mixture to recover both dry blue copper sulfate crystals and pure water. However, if copper sulfate is heated too strongly, it decomposes into black copper oxide gas. Explain how to safely separate this mixture using controlled evaporation and condensation.
STEPS:
- **Step 1: Identify properties and risks.** $CuSO_4$ is highly soluble and heat-sensitive. Water is volatile. Strong heating will chemically destroy the solute ($CuSO_4(s) \xrightarrow{\text{strong heat}} CuO(s) + SO_3(g)$), so direct boiling to dryness must be avoided.
- **Step 2: Set up a water bath (Controlled Evaporation).** Instead of heating the copper sulfate solution directly over a flame, place the evaporating dish over a beaker of boiling water (a water bath or double boiler). This limits the maximum temperature to $100^\circ\text{C}$.
- **Step 3: Concentrate the solution.** Heat until about two-thirds of the water evaporates, leaving a highly concentrated, saturated solution.
- **Step 4: Collect the water vapor (Condensation).** Position a glass dome or a water-cooled condensing cover over the evaporating setup to trap the rising steam, funneling the condensed pure water into a side beaker.
- **Step 5: Allow slow crystallization.** Turn off the heat and let the remaining concentrated solution cool slowly. Beautiful blue crystals of copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate ($CuSO_4 \cdot 5H_2O$) will form safely without thermal decomposition.
REASONING: Direct strong heating causes chemical decomposition of copper sulfate. Using a water bath restricts the temperature to a safe ceiling of $100^\circ\text{C}$, allowing the solvent (water) to evaporate and condense safely while preserving the chemical structure of the delicate salt.
ANSWER: Evaporate the water slowly using a gentle water bath to prevent chemical decomposition of $CuSO_4$, while condensing the vapor onto a cooled surface to collect pure water, leaving blue copper sulfate crystals behind.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: In many rural coastal areas of India, drinking water is scarce. A family sets up a "solar still" by digging a hole in the wet, salty sand, placing a clean steel bowl in the center, covering the hole with a clear plastic sheet, placing a small stone in the center of the sheet directly above the bowl, and leaving it in the hot sun. Explain the science of how this simple setup provides fresh drinking water, identifying exactly where evaporation and condensation take place and how thermal energy drives the system.
COMPETENCY: Relating properties to structure
STEPS:
- **Step 1: Understand the thermal input.** Solar radiation (heat energy from the sun) passes through the clear plastic sheet and heats the wet, salty sand below.
- **Step 2: Identify the evaporation phase.** The water in the damp sand absorbs this thermal energy, causing its molecules to move faster and escape as water vapor (evaporation). The non-volatile salts remain trapped in the sand.
- **Step 3: Identify the condensation phase.** The warm water vapor rises and hits the cooler underside of the plastic sheet (which is cooled by the outside air). The vapor loses thermal energy and changes back into liquid water droplets (condensation).
- **Step 4: Analyze the mechanical collection.** The stone in the center weighs down the plastic sheet, creating a downward cone. Gravity pulls the condensed water droplets along this slope to the lowest point, where they drip directly into the clean steel bowl as salt-free drinking water.
ANSWER: Solar energy heats the wet sand, causing water to undergo **evaporation** into water vapor, leaving salts behind. The vapor rises, touches the cooler plastic sheet, and undergoes **condensation** into liquid water droplets. The slope created by the stone guides these pure water droplets to drip into the collection bowl.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A factory accidentally mixes a large quantity of common salt ($NaCl$) with ammonium chloride ($NH_4Cl$). Both are white, water-soluble crystalline solids, but ammonium chloride undergoes sublimation (turns directly from solid to gas when heated) at $338^\circ\text{C}$ without melting. Explain why dissolving this mixture in water and performing simple evaporation to dryness will NOT separate these two solids, and design an alternative heating-based method to separate them in their dry state.
COMPETENCY: Predicting reaction products
STEPS:
- **Step 1: Analyze the solubility behavior.** Both salt ($NaCl$) and ammonium chloride ($NH_4Cl$) are highly soluble in water. If dissolved, they form a uniform homogeneous solution.
- **Step 2: Evaluate simple evaporation.** If you heat this solution, water will evaporate. However, as the water disappears, both $NaCl$ and $NH_4Cl$ will crystallize out together, leaving you with the exact same solid mixture you started with. Thus, evaporation of the solution fails to separate the two solids.
- **Step 3: Leverage sublimation (Alternative dry method).** Instead of dissolving them, take the dry solid mixture and place it in a deep porcelain dish.
- **Step 4: Set up sublimation apparatus.** Place an inverted glass funnel over the dish, plugging the stem of the funnel with cotton wool.
- **Step 5: Heat the dry mixture.** Heat the dish. At $338^\circ\text{C}$, the dry ammonium chloride ($NH_4Cl$) sublimates directly into gas, leaving solid $NaCl$ behind in the dish. The ammonium chloride gas rises, hits the cool upper walls of the glass funnel, and condenses directly back into a pure solid (sublimid) on the glass walls.
ANSWER: Dissolving and evaporating fails because both soluble solids precipitate out together when water is lost. To separate them, heat the dry mixture directly: the ammonium chloride will sublimate into gas and condense as a pure solid on a cool inverted funnel, leaving the non-sublimable common salt behind in the dish.
DIFFICULTY: hard
