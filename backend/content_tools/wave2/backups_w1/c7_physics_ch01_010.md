## ELI5
Science never graduates; it keeps studying. The ceiling fan blurs at high speed until you switch it off and the blades reappear - today's camera catches what yesterday's eye could not, and understanding sharpens. Scientific temper is the habit underneath: question, test against evidence, accept corrections. Facts get refined, sometimes overturned - not because science fails but because it works. An ongoing process, proudly unfinished.

## DEFINITION
Science is an ongoing, evolving process of discovery where observations, experiments, and reasoning continuously refine our understanding of the natural world, measured through the accumulation of validated knowledge and empirical evidence. There is no single SI unit for this philosophical and methodological concept, but the process relies on reproducible measurements of physical quantities using standard SI units (meters, seconds, kilograms).

Observe how a simple household ceiling fan running at high speed creates a blur, making individual blades invisible until you turn it off and inspect its structure up close. This everyday experience teaches us that our initial perception is often incomplete and requires closer investigation, testing, and sometimes the revision of our ideas. Connect this to what you learned in Class 6 about the scientific method, where an observation leads to a question and a tentative hypothesis. Now, we take that understanding further to see that science never truly "stops" with one answer; every conclusion opens up new questions and deeper layers of reality. By combining systematic observation, experimentation, and peer review, our understanding of the universe continually evolves and grows more precise.

**Takeaway:** science is permanently in progress: new tools and evidence keep refining understanding, and questioning is the engine. **In the exam:** in 'nature of science' questions, show an idea changing with better evidence - the fan blur caught by better instruments. **Check yourself:** run the walkthrough's fan: what the eye misses at speed, the slowed frame reveals - who was wrong, who unfinished? **Why students miss it:** students want final answers - the flagged discomfort. ## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think science is a static collection of absolute "facts" memorized from a textbook, rather than an active, ongoing method of asking questions, testing hypotheses, and revising models when new data appears. For example, they might believe that once we learned about electric circuits in Class 6, every single truth about electricity was fully discovered and closed forever, ignoring how modern research into superconductors and renewable energy constantly updates our understanding.
## ON_FAILURE
To fix this misconception, guide the student to compare how a specific topic (like light or heat) was understood historically versus how it is understood today. Have them perform a simple experiment where their first measurement of a moving toy car's speed differs slightly from a second trial, and ask: "Is your first number the 'only' truth, or is science about finding a more reliable pattern through multiple tests?" Emphasize that uncertainty and revision are strengths of science, not weaknesses.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A student records the temperature of boiling water in a school science lab over three consecutive days. Day 1: 99.0 °C, Day 2: 99.2 °C, Day 3: 98.9 °C. Calculate the average temperature and explain why the values differ slightly, reflecting the ongoing nature of measurement in science.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the given values with units: $T_1 = 99.0\text{ °C}$, $T_2 = 99.2\text{ °C}$, $T_3 = 98.9\text{ °C}$.
- Step 2: Set up the formula for average temperature: $\text{Average } T = \frac{T_1 + T_2 + T_3}{n}$, where $n = 3$.
- Step 3: Substitute the numerical values and units into the equation: $\text{Average } T = \frac{99.0\text{ °C} + 99.2\text{ °C} + 98.9\text{ °C}}{3}$.
- Step 4: Compute the sum and divide, tracking units: Sum $= 297.1\text{ °C}$; $\text{Average } T = \frac{297.1\text{ °C}}{3} = 99.033\text{ °C}$.
- Step 5: Verify if the answer is physically reasonable (water boils near 100 °C depending on atmospheric pressure) and state with correct SI/derived units.
REASONING: Variations in repeated measurements occur due to minor environmental fluctuations and instrument precision. Calculating an average and recognizing experimental spread is a core part of scientific discovery.
ANSWER: 99.03 °C
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A bicycle travels a distance of 150 meters in 10 seconds during trial 1, and 150 meters in 12 seconds during trial 2. Calculate the speed for both trials (m/s) and explain what the difference tells us about real-world experimentation.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given values for Trial 1 ($d_1 = 150\text{ m}$, $t_1 = 10\text{ s}$) and Trial 2 ($d_2 = 150\text{ m}$, $t_2 = 12\text{ s}$).
- Step 2: Recall the formula for speed: $v = \frac{d}{t}$ with units meters per second (m/s).
- Step 3: Substitute values for Trial 1: $v_1 = \frac{150\text{ m}}{10\text{ s}}$.
- Step 4: Compute Trial 1 ($15\text{ m/s}$) and Trial 2 ($v_2 = \frac{150\text{ m}}{12\text{ s}} = 12.5\text{ m/s}$).
- Step 5: Verify reasonableness and track unit cancellation (meters divided by seconds gives m/s).
REASONING: Real-world physical measurements rarely yield identical results every single time because external factors like friction or pedaling force change. Comparing multiple trials is essential for scientific validity.
ANSWER: Trial 1: 15 m/s, Trial 2: 12.5 m/s
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A solar heating experiment measures water temperature rise over time. In trial A, a container exposed to direct sunlight heats by 5 °C in 300 seconds. In trial B, with a glass cover added (simulating a greenhouse effect, connecting to Class 6 heat concepts), it heats by 8 °C in 300 seconds. Calculate the rate of temperature increase (°C/s) for both trials and determine the percentage increase in heating rate.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given data: Trial A ($\Delta T_A = 5\text{ °C}$, $t = 300\text{ s}$), Trial B ($\Delta T_B = 8\text{ °C}$, $t = 300\text{ s}$).
- Step 2: Define rate of heating formula: $\text{Rate} = \frac{\Delta T}{t}$ with units (°C/s).
- Step 3: Calculate Rate A: $\text{Rate}_A = \frac{5\text{ °C}}{300\text{ s}} = 0.0167\text{ °C/s}$.
- Step 4: Calculate Rate B: $\text{Rate}_B = \frac{8\text{ °C}}{300\text{ s}} = 0.0267\text{ °C/s}$.
- Step 5: Calculate percentage increase: $\frac{\text{Rate}_B - \text{Rate}_A}{\text{Rate}_A} \times 100 = \frac{0.0267 - 0.0167}{0.0167} \times 100 \approx 60\%$.
- Step 6: Verify physical consistency: Adding a glass cover traps radiation (convection/radiation trapping), accelerating heat gain, which validates why scientists modify experimental setups to test new variables.
REASONING: Modifying an experimental condition and comparing quantitative rates allows scientists to discover new principles and refine existing technological applications.
ANSWER: Trial A: 0.0167 °C/s, Trial B: 0.0267 °C/s, Increase: 60%
