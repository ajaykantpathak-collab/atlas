## ELI5
Squeeze lemon on sliced brinjal and the white flesh browns; drop an iron gate in the rain and it blushes orange - chemistry leaving notes. Indicators of chemical change are the observable evidence - permanent colour change, bubbles of gas, heat given out or taken in - that signal a new substance has formed. No single note proves the case alone, but together they are chemistry's signature.

## DEFINITION
Indicators of chemical change are observable physical evidence—such as a permanent change in colour, evolution of a gas, or release/absorption of heat—that signal a new chemical substance has been formed during a reaction. For example, when magnesium ribbon burns in air, it produces a blinding white light and forms a new white powder (magnesium oxide, $\text{2Mg} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{2MgO}$).

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can identify whether a given everyday observation (like tarnishing, fizzing, or warming) represents a chemical change by correctly pointing to at least one indicator (colour change, gas evolution, or heat change) and explaining why a new substance is formed.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the identification of chemical changes through three primary indicators: colour change, gas evolution, and heat/energy changes within standard laboratory and daily life conditions. It does NOT cover complex thermodynamic calculations, reaction kinetics, or advanced spectroscopy.
## WALKTHROUGH
When you squeeze lemon juice over sliced raw brinjals or watch an iron gate turn reddish-brown during the monsoon in India, you are witnessing nature's silent chemical laboratory at work. To begin investigating these changes, we must observe what happens right in front of our eyes before, during, and after the process. We first notice if the original material alters its appearance, gives off bubbles of gas, or becomes noticeably hot or cold to the touch. Building on our Class 6 understanding that chemical changes create entirely new substances, we now look for the visible 'clues' or indicators that announce a chemical reaction has taken place. By systematically checking for colour shifts, gas fizzing, and temperature changes, we can prove beyond doubt that a chemical transformation occurred.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What type of change is indicated when baking soda reacts with vinegar and produces bubbles of carbon dioxide gas?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
This is a chemical change. A wrong answer—such as classifying it as a physical change because the fizzing eventually stops—reveals a fundamental misunderstanding that the disappearance of a gas means no new substance was made, ignoring the fact that a completely different gas ($\text{CO}_2$) was generated.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why does the appearance of a new colour in a reaction mixture strongly suggest a chemical change rather than a physical change?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
A new colour indicates that the chemical bonds of the original substances have rearranged to form an entirely different chemical compound with different light-absorbing properties. A wrong answer claiming that colour changes can happen by simply mixing paints ignores that mixing paints is a physical combination of pigments, whereas a chemical reaction creates new microscopic chemical species.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
When a strip of zinc metal is dropped into dilute hydrochloric acid ($\text{HCl}$), vigorous bubbling occurs and the test tube becomes warm. State two indicators of chemical change observed here and write the chemical formula for the gas typically released in such metal-acid reactions.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The two indicators are the evolution of a gas (bubbling) and the release of heat (test tube becomes warm). The gas released is hydrogen ($\text{H}_2$). Equation: $\text{Zn} + \text{2HCl} \rightarrow \text{ZnCl}_2 + \text{H}_2\uparrow$. A wrong answer missing either indicator or misidentifying the gas as oxygen shows confusion regarding metal-acid reactivity properties learned in previous units.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Analyze the following scenario: Solid copper sulfate crystals are dissolved in water to form a blue solution. Then, an iron nail is dipped into it, and after some time, a brown coating of copper deposits on the iron while the blue solution turns green. Which part of this sequence is purely a physical change, and which part is a chemical change accompanied by a colour indicator?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Dissolving copper sulfate in water is a physical change (the blue solid simply dissolves, and crystals can be recovered by evaporation). Dipping the iron nail and getting a brown copper coating accompanied by a colour shift from blue to green is a chemical change, indicated by a distinct colour change and the formation of a new substance (iron sulfate and copper metal). A wrong answer grouping both into chemical changes fails to recognize that dissolving a soluble salt without a chemical reaction is a reversible physical process.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate the statement: "Any time a liquid gets warm, a chemical change must have occurred." Is this statement scientifically valid? Justify your reasoning with examples.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is invalid. While many chemical changes release heat (exothermic), physical processes like dissolving concentrated sulfuric acid or even friction can generate heat without creating a new chemical substance in the case of friction, or involve solution processes that are physical. A wrong answer accepting the statement blindly fails to distinguish between thermal energy changes caused by physical dissolution/friction versus actual bond-breaking/bond-forming chemical reactions.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple kitchen-based experiment using household items found in India (such as turmeric powder, lemon juice, soap solution, and water) to demonstrate a chemical change indicated by a colour shift. Describe the steps and explain the chemical indicator principle.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good response describes mixing turmeric paste with water (yellow), adding a basic soap solution to turn it reddish-brown (indicating a chemical reaction between turmeric's natural dye and a base), and then adding acidic lemon juice to turn it back to yellow. A poor answer fails to name specific everyday items or mistakes a physical dilution for a chemical reaction.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that if a solid completely dissolves in a liquid (like sugar dissolving in water or copper sulfate dissolving in water), the resulting colour change or solution formation is a chemical change because the original solid "disappeared" and cannot be seen separately.
## ON_FAILURE
Remind the student to check for reversibility and the creation of new chemical bonds. Have them perform a physical separation technique (like evaporation of sugar water to recover sugar crystals) to prove that no new substance was created, contrasting it with a true chemical reaction where original properties vanish permanently.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: When a clean iron nail is placed inside a blue aqueous solution of copper sulfate ($\text{CuSO}_4$) in a beaker at room temperature, a chemical change occurs. Over 30 minutes, the blue colour fades to light green, and a brown coating appears on the iron nail. Identify the reactants, products, and the indicators of this chemical change.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the initial reactants and their properties—solid iron nail (grey metal) and copper sulfate solution (blue liquid).
- Step 2: Observe the visible changes during the reaction—the blue colour changes to green, and a reddish-brown solid deposits on the nail.
- Step 3: Interpret the chemical indicators—a permanent colour change occurs, signaling that new chemical substances (iron sulfate and copper metal) are formed.
- Step 4: Write the word equation and chemical equation for the reaction: $\text{Iron} + \text{Copper sulfate} \rightarrow \text{Iron sulfate} + \text{Copper}; \text{Fe} + \text{CuSO}_4 \rightarrow \text{FeSO}_4 + \text{Cu}$.
- Step 5: Verify that the change is irreversible and involves both a colour change and formation of a precipitate/deposit, confirming it is a chemical change.
REASONING: The transition from a blue solution to a green solution along with a brown metallic deposit proves that the original copper ions have been displaced by iron, creating entirely new chemical species with distinct optical and physical properties.
ANSWER: Indicators observed: Colour change (blue to green) and deposit formation (brown copper coating on iron). Equation: $\text{Fe} + \text{CuSO}_4 \rightarrow \text{FeSO}_4 + \text{Cu}$.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A pinch of baking soda ($\text{NaHCO}_3$) is added to a test tube containing clear vinegar (acetic acid, $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$). Vigorous fizzing with bubbles is observed, and a burning splinter brought near the mouth of the tube is extinguished. Determine the indicators of chemical change and write the chemical reaction.
STEPS:
- Step 1: List the reactants—solid sodium hydrogen carbonate (baking soda) and liquid acetic acid (vinegar).
- Step 2: Observe the changes—rapid effervescence (bubbling) indicating the release of a gas, and the extinguishing of a flame showing the gas is carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$).
- Step 3: Identify the primary indicator—evolution of gas with effervescence, accompanied by a slight temperature drop (absorption of heat).
- Step 4: Write the balanced chemical equation: $\text{NaHCO}_3 + \text{CH}_3\text{COOH} \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COONa} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2\uparrow$.
- Step 5: Conclude that the rapid bubbling and gas generation prove a chemical change has taken place because baking soda and vinegar cannot be easily separated back into their original forms.
REASONING: The sudden appearance of gas bubbles where no gas existed before is a classic indicator of a chemical change, driven by the chemical reaction between an acid and a carbonate.
ANSWER: Indicator: Gas evolution (effervescence of $\text{CO}_2$). Equation: $\text{NaHCO}_3 + \text{CH}_3\text{COOH} \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COONa} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2\uparrow$.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A magnesium ribbon ($\text{Mg}$) of mass 2.4 g is ignited with a spirit lamp in the presence of atmospheric oxygen ($\text{O}_2$). It burns with a dazzling white flame, releasing intense heat and leaving behind a white powdery ash of magnesium oxide ($\text{MgO}$). Identify all three major indicators of chemical change (colour/light, gas/solid product, heat) and verify mass relationships in the balanced equation.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify reactants and their initial states—solid magnesium ribbon (lustrous grey) and oxygen gas (colourless).
- Step 2: Note the energy indicator—intense heat and blinding white light are released during combustion.
- Step 3: Note the appearance indicator—the lustrous grey metal transforms into a brittle white powder (magnesium oxide).
- Step 4: Write and balance the chemical equation for the reaction: $\text{2Mg} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{2MgO}$.
- Step 5: Check atomic balance—2 magnesium atoms and 2 oxygen atoms on both reactant and product sides.
- Step 6: Conclude that heat/light emission, dramatic appearance change, and a new chemical substance formation confirm a permanent chemical change.
REASONING: Combustion reactions combine fuel with oxygen, releasing large amounts of heat and light while forming metal oxides with completely different properties than the starting elements.
ANSWER: Indicators: Intense heat/light, complete transformation of grey metal into white powder ($\text{MgO}$). Equation: $\text{2Mg} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{2MgO}$.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A student drops a piece of limestone (calcium carbonate, $\text{CaCO}_3$) into hydrochloric acid. Fizzing occurs, and the beaker feels slightly warm. The student claims this is only a physical change because the limestone 'dissolves' into the liquid. Evaluate this student's claim using indicators of chemical change.
COMPETENCY: Evaluating and correcting misconceptions about physical vs. chemical changes using observable indicators.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Analyze the student's observation of limestone 'dissolving' in acid.
- Step 2: Identify the actual indicators present—vigorous gas evolution (effervescence of $\text{CO}_2$) and a temperature change (warmth).
- Step 3: Contrast these indicators with simple physical dissolution (like salt in water), noting that gas evolution and heat generation signal bond breaking and new substance formation ($\text{CaCl}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2$).
- Step 4: Formulate the final conclusion refuting the student's claim.
ANSWER: The student is incorrect. Although the limestone piece disappears, the vigorous bubbling (evolution of carbon dioxide gas) and temperature change are definitive indicators of a chemical change, proving a new substance has been created.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: In a science laboratory, a teacher mixes two clear, colourless solutions: lead(II) nitrate and potassium iodide. Immediately, a bright yellow solid settles at the bottom of the beaker. Explain how this observation proves a chemical change has occurred, and identify which specific indicator is responsible for this conclusion.
COMPETENCY: Predicting and interpreting precipitation reactions as colour/substance-based indicators of chemical change.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Examine the initial state of the reactants—two clear, colourless, transparent liquids.
- Step 2: Examine the final state—a bright yellow solid (lead iodide precipitate) suspended/settled in a clear liquid.
- Step 3: Connect the sudden appearance of a distinct solid colour to chemical bond rearrangement and precipitation.
- Step 4: Identify the primary indicator—dramatic colour change and formation of a new insoluble solid substance (precipitate).
ANSWER: This proves a chemical change has occurred because mixing two colourless liquids spontaneously produces a bright yellow solid (lead iodide) along with potassium nitrate. The indicator is a striking colour change and formation of a new solid substance (precipitate), which cannot happen in a mere physical mixing of stable solutions.
DIFFICULTY: hard
