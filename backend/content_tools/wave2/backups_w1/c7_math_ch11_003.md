## ELI5
Cutting 24 red and 36 blue ribbons into the longest identical pieces is a listing job: write every factor of each number in tidy pairs - one times the number, two times its partner, inward until the pairs meet - then circle the names both lists share and pick the tallest. Factor pairs guarantee nothing is missed, and composites always list more than the naked eye expects. Beware the classic confusion: a number that divides into both is a factor; a number both grow into is a multiple.

## DEFINITION
Finding the Highest Common Factor (HCF) by the listing factors method involves writing down all the individual factors of two or more given numbers, identifying which factors appear on all the lists (common factors), and selecting the largest one among them. This method directly translates the foundational concept of factors into finding the greatest shared divisor for quantities like grouping items or simplifying ratios.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are organizing a school cultural event at your school in Delhi, and you have 24 red ribbons and 36 blue ribbons. You want to cut them into identical pieces of maximum possible length without any ribbons left over so that each stage decoration looks uniform. To figure out the maximum possible length, you need to find the factors of 24 and the factors of 36. We begin by recalling what a factor is: a whole number that divides another number completely without leaving a remainder. By writing down every single factor for both numbers in systematic pairs, we can spot which factors are shared by both. Finally, we look at those shared factors and pick the highest one, which gives us our maximum ribbon length.

**Takeaway:** listing for HCF means complete factor pairs per number, an intersection, then the tallest survivor. **In the exam:** build factor lists in pairs so nothing is missed, mark common entries, and box the largest. **Check yourself:** list the ribbons' factors in pairs and check the pair method against a quick divisibility scan. **Why students miss it:** they stop the list early, missing the middle pairs where common factors like to hide. The paired columns catch the middle factors where careless lists lose the HCF.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the exact definition of a "factor" of a number when listing factors to find an HCF?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
A factor of a number is an integer that divides that number completely without leaving any remainder. A wrong answer usually reveals that the student is confusing factors with multiples, such as thinking factors are numbers obtained by multiplying by 2, 3, etc., rather than numbers that divide evenly into the given number.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
When listing factors of 18, a student writes: $1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18$. Are all these numbers factors, and how do we know we haven't missed any?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Yes, these are all the correct factors because they divide 18 evenly, and they are found in pairs ($1 \times 18 = 18, 2 \times 9 = 18, 3 \times 6 = 18$). A wrong answer or missing factor indicates that the student does not check factor pairs systematically or forgets to test numbers using division or divisibility rules.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Find the HCF of 24 and 30 by listing all their factors. Show the common factors and state the highest one.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
First, list the factors of 24: $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24$. Next, list the factors of 30: $1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 15, 30$. The common factors are $1, 2, 3, 6$. The highest common factor is $6$. A wrong answer often stems from arithmetic errors during division or missing a factor pair like $4 \times 6$ for 24.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
If two numbers are prime numbers, such as 7 and 11, what will always be their HCF when found by listing factors, and why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Their HCF will always be 1 because prime numbers only have two distinct factors: 1 and themselves, meaning the only factor they share is 1. A wrong answer suggests the student confuses prime numbers with composite numbers or incorrectly assumes prime numbers can share other common factors.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student claims that the HCF of 12 and 36 is 36 because 36 appears in the factor list of both numbers. Evaluate this claim and correct the error.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The claim is incorrect because 36 is a multiple of 12, not a factor of 12 (you cannot divide 12 by 36 to get a whole number). The correct factors of 12 are $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$, and the factors of 36 include those, making the HCF equal to 12. This error shows the student is confusing the concepts of factors (numbers that divide into the value) with multiples (numbers that contain the value).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a pair of composite numbers less than 50 whose HCF by listing factors is exactly 12. Show your factor lists to prove it.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
An example pair is 36 and 48. Factors of 36: $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36$. Factors of 48: $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24, 48$. The common factors are $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$, and the highest is 12. A poor answer would list numbers that do not share 12 as a factor or fail to verify all factor pairs.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students frequently confuse factors with multiples, listing numbers that are larger than the given number (e.g., listing $12, 24, 36$ as factors of 12 instead of $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$). Another common error is stopping the listing process too early before checking all integer pairs up to the square root of the number, thereby missing middle factors like 6 in the number 36.
## ON_FAILURE
Remind the student that factors are building blocks that go *into* a number (so they are smaller than or equal to the number), whereas multiples are products that grow *larger* than the number. Guide them to write factor pairs multiplication-style ($1 \times n, 2 \times \text{something} = n$) in ascending order until the numbers meet in the middle.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A teacher in Jaipur has 36 notebooks and 48 pens to distribute equally among a group of struggling students such that no notebooks or pens are left over. What is the maximum number of students who can receive these items?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the numbers to analyze: 36 (notebooks) and 48 (pens).
- Step 2: List all factors of 36 by checking pairs: $1 \times 36$, $2 \times 18$, $3 \times 12$, $4 \times 9$, $6 \times 6$. Factor list for 36: $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36$.
- Step 3: List all factors of 48 by checking pairs: $1 \times 48$, $2 \times 24$, $3 \times 16$, $4 \times 12$, $6 \times 8$. Factor list for 48: $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24, 48$.
- Step 4: Identify the common factors shared by both lists: $1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12$.
- Step 5: Select the highest number from the common factors list, which is 12, representing the maximum number of students.
REASONING: Dividing items equally without leftovers requires finding a number that divides both 36 and 48 completely (a common factor). To find the *maximum* possible group size, we need the *highest* common factor (HCF).
ANSWER: 12 students
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Two alarm clocks ring together at intervals that share factor properties with 40 and 60 minutes. Find the HCF of 40 and 60 using the listing factors method to determine the base time unit interval.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Write down the target numbers: 40 and 60.
- Step 2: Find all factors of 40 systematically: $1 \times 40, 2 \times 20, 4 \times 10, 5 \times 8$. Factors of 40: $1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 10, 20, 40$.
- Step 3: Find all factors of 60 systematically: $1 \times 60, 2 \times 30, 3 \times 20, 4 \times 15, 5 \times 12, 6 \times 10$. Factors of 60: $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30, 60$.
- Step 4: Compare both lists to find the common factors: $1, 2, 4, 5, 10, 20$.
- Step 5: Determine the largest value among the common factors, which is 20.
REASONING: By exhaustively writing out factor pairs in order, we ensure no intermediate factors (like 5 or 8 for 40) are omitted. Matching the two sets allows us to isolate the shared divisors and pinpoint the maximum common divisor.
ANSWER: 20
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A florist in Kolkata has 45 red roses and 75 yellow marigolds. She wants to make identical flower bouquets using all the flowers without mixing them into incomplete sets, ensuring each bouquet has the maximum possible number of flowers. Find the HCF of 45 and 75 by listing factors, and calculate how many bouquets of red roses can be made.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the quantities: 45 red roses and 75 yellow marigolds.
- Step 2: List all factors of 45: $1 \times 45, 3 \times 15, 5 \times 9$. Factors of 45: $1, 3, 5, 9, 15, 45$.
- Step 3: List all factors of 75: $1 \times 75, 3 \times 25, 5 \times 15$. Factors of 75: $1, 3, 5, 15, 25, 75$.
- Step 4: Find the common factors of 45 and 75: $1, 3, 5, 15$.
- Step 5: Identify the HCF, which is 15 (representing the maximum number of identical bouquets).
- Step 6: Divide the number of red roses by the HCF to find bouquets made of red roses: $45 \div 15 = 3$ bouquets.
REASONING: The HCF represents the greatest number of identical groupings we can create from both flower types. Using this HCF as the divisor per bouquet type gives us the exact count of bouquets for each flower variety.
ANSWER: HCF is 15; 3 bouquets of red roses
