## ELI5
Probability measures hope on a leash: a number from impossible to certain, with the whole walk in between. It is the fraction of favourable outcomes over every possible outcome - and the denominator must count the misses too, not just the hits. Tails on a fair coin, an off-table dice face, a dark chocolate in a mixed box: every hope gets its honest share of the whole.

## DEFINITION
Probability is a numerical measure of the likelihood that a specific event will occur, ranging from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). It represents the ratio of successful outcomes to the total number of equally likely possible outcomes.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can calculate the probability of simple events (like coin tosses or die rolls) and classify them as impossible, possible, or certain. They must be able to express the result as a fraction or a number between 0 and 1.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the basic calculation of probability for simple, independent events. It excludes conditional probability, combinatorics, and probability distributions.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you have a bag of 5 different coloured marbles. If you reach in without looking, how do you know if you are "likely" to pick a blue one? To find out, we look at the total number of marbles (the total outcomes) and count how many are blue (the favourable outcomes). We use the formula: Probability = (Number of favourable outcomes) / (Total number of outcomes). By turning this into a fraction, we can compare the chance of picking a blue marble to the chance of picking any other colour. The key insight is that probability is just a way to put a number on "luck" or "chance."

**Takeaway:** probability is favourable outcomes over total outcomes, living between impossible and certain. **In the exam:** in probability questions, write the fraction explicitly - favourites on top, all possibilities below - before simplifying. **Check yourself:** count the walkthrough's marble bag - every colour, not just the hoped one, fills the denominator. **Why students miss it:** students count only the winners - the mixed bag is a no to that; the misses own their share of the bottom. ## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often mistake the "total outcomes" by only counting the outcomes that are different from the one they want. For example, in the chocolate problem, they might use 7 (milk) as the denominator because it's the "other" number. They forget that the denominator must always be the grand total of all possibilities.
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to physically list all possible outcomes on a piece of paper. For the marble bag, have them write: "Red, Blue, Green, Yellow, White." Then, have them count the total list (5). Finally, ask them to circle the ones that match their goal (the "favourable" ones). Always emphasize "Total = Everything that could possibly happen."

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A box contains 4 red pens, 6 blue pens, and 2 black pens. What is the probability of picking a blue pen?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the number of blue pens (favourable = 6).
- Step 2: Calculate the total number of pens (4 + 6 + 2 = 12).
- Step 3: Apply the formula: P = 6 / 12.
- Step 4: Simplify the fraction: 6/12 = 1/2.
- Step 5: State the result: The probability is 1/2 or 0.5.
REASONING: We must find the "part" (blue) relative to the "whole" (all pens combined). Dividing the specific colour by the total gives us the statistical chance of that specific outcome.
ANSWER: 1/2
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A spinner has 8 equal sections numbered 1 through 8. What is the probability of landing on a number greater than 6?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the total sections (total = 8).
- Step 2: Identify the favourable numbers (numbers greater than 6 are 7 and 8).
- Step 3: Count the favourable outcomes (2).
- Step 4: Apply formula: P = 2 / 8.
- Step 5: Simplify: 1/4 or 0.25.
REASONING: By listing the possibilities (7 and 8), we isolate the outcomes that meet our criteria, then divide by the total possible spaces on the spinner.
ANSWER: 1/4
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A bag has 5 apples, 3 oranges, and 2 mangoes. If you pick one fruit, what is the probability of picking a fruit that is NOT a mango?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Find total fruits (5 + 3 + 2 = 10).
- Step 2: Identify favourable fruits (Apples and Oranges).
- Step 3: Count favourable: 5 + 3 = 8.
- Step 4: Apply formula: P = 8 / 10.
- Step 5: Simplify: 4/5 or 0.8.
REASONING: "Not a mango" means any fruit except the 2 mangoes. Subtracting the mangoes from the total (10 - 2 = 8) gives us the number of favourable outcomes for the "not mango" condition.
ANSWER: 4/5
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You have a bag with 10 marbles: 5 red, 3 green, and 2 white. A friend claims that because there are more red marbles, the probability of picking a red one is 50%. Is this correct? Explain your reasoning.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- Step 1: Define total outcomes (10).
- Step 2: Identify favourable outcomes for red (5).
- Step 3: Calculate probability (5/10).
- Step 4: Compare fraction to 50% (5/10 = 1/2 = 50%).
ANSWER: Yes, the friend is correct because 5 out of 10 is exactly half, which is 50%.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: You are rolling a standard 6-sided die. If you roll it 100 times, will you get a "6" exactly 16 or 17 times? Why or why not?
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions
STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate the theoretical probability of a "6" (1/6).
- Step 2: Recognize that probability predicts "likelihood" over many trials, not exact results per trial.
- Step 3: Explain the difference between expected theoretical outcome and actual random chance.
ANSWER: Not necessarily. While the probability is 1/6, each roll is independent. 1/6 of 100 is about 16.6, but random chance means you could get more or fewer than that in reality.
DIFFICULTY: hard
