## ELI5
Bounce a pocket mirror's bright patch onto a classroom wall and you are running reflection's two laws: the bounce angle equals the incoming angle, and all three lines - incoming ray, bounce ray, and the upright normal between them - share one flat plane. The normal is the referee's vertical, the line angles are measured from. Miss the normal and every angle reads wrong; keep it, and mirrors behave like geometry.

## DEFINITION
The laws of reflection state that when light falls on a smooth, reflective surface, the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence, and the incident ray, reflected ray, and normal all lie in the same plane. These angular measurements are expressed in degrees (°) relative to an imaginary perpendicular line called the normal.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
Can measure or calculate angles of incidence and reflection using the normal line, and can sketch the path of a light ray reflecting off a plane surface following both geometric laws.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers regular (specular) reflection on flat plane mirrors and surfaces, angle calculations, and the coplanar relationship of light rays. Does not cover diffuse reflection on rough surfaces or complex curved mirrors.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine holding a pocket mirror in the sunlight and bouncing a bright patch of light onto a classroom wall. If you tilt the mirror slightly, the light patch on the wall moves quickly across the room in a very predictable way. To understand why light behaves this way, scientists use a ray of light, an imaginary perpendicular line called a normal, and geometric angles. Building on our understanding of how light travels in straight lines from earlier studies, we measure how incoming rays strike surfaces. By measuring the angle between the incoming ray and the normal, and comparing it to the angle of the bouncing ray, we discover the exact rules governing reflection.

**Takeaway:** reflection is measured from the normal: equal angles in, equal angles out, all three rays agreeing to one plane. **In the exam:** sketch the normal first in every ray diagram; only then read or compute the angles. **Check yourself:** aim the walkthrough's mirror patch and mark where the normal would stand on the wall. **Why students miss it:** students measure angles off the mirror's face - the flagged slip that turns equality into nonsense. The normal is the reference, always. Draw it as a dashed upright in every diagram, and the second law keeps the drawing honest too.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If a light ray strikes a plane mirror at an angle of 30° to the surface of the mirror, what is the angle of incidence?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The angle of incidence is 60°. A wrong answer (like 30°) reveals the common misconception that the angle of incidence is measured from the mirror's surface instead of the normal line, which is perpendicular (90°) to the surface.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A light ray strikes a plane mirror such that the angle between the incident ray and the reflected ray is 80°. Calculate the angle of reflection in degrees.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Identify that the total angle between the incident ray and reflected ray is the sum of the angle of incidence ($\angle i$) and the angle of reflection ($\angle r$). Step 2: Recall the first law of reflection which states that the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection ($\angle i = \angle r$). Step 3: Set up the equation: $\angle i + \angle r = 80° \Rightarrow 2\angle r = 80°$. Step 4: Divide by 2 to solve for the angle of reflection: $\angle r = 80° / 2 = 40°$. Step 5: Verify that both angles equal 40°, making the total 80°, which is physically reasonable. Answer: 40°. A wrong answer indicates the student forgot that the total angle is split equally between the incident and reflected rays relative to the normal.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
How does changing the angle of incidence from 25° to 50° affect the angle of reflection, and what fundamental law explains this change?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The angle of reflection will also double, increasing from 25° to 50°, as explained by the first law of reflection ($\angle i = \angle r$). A wrong answer shows a failure to recognize the direct proportionality between the two angles governed by the laws of reflection.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student claims that if an incident ray is perpendicular (along the normal) to a plane mirror, the reflected ray will bounce off at an angle of 90° to the mirror. Is this correct, and why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
This is incorrect because a ray striking along the normal has an angle of incidence of 0°. According to the first law of reflection, the angle of reflection must also be 0°, meaning the light ray retraces its path straight back along the normal. A wrong answer shows a mix-up between the angle measured from the mirror surface and the angle measured from the normal line.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students frequently measure the angle of incidence and angle of reflection between the light ray and the reflecting surface itself, rather than measuring it from the normal line (the perpendicular drawn to the surface). For example, if a ray hits a table mirror at 40° to the glass, students incorrectly state the angle of incidence is 40° instead of calculating $90° - 40° = 50°$.
## ON_FAILURE

Step 2: Draw a dashed line straight up at a 90° angle from the middle of the mirror line; label this dashed line as the 'Normal'. Step 3: Emphasize by drawing an arc that all angle measurements in optics must start from this dashed normal line, never from the flat mirror line itself.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A light ray strikes a plane glass mirror in a physics lab in Delhi at an angle of incidence of 35°. What is the angle of reflection, and what is the total angle between the incident ray and the reflected ray?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the given value: Angle of incidence ($\angle i$) = 35°. Identify what needs to be found: Angle of reflection ($\angle r$) and total angle between incident and reflected rays ($\angle i + \angle r$).
- Step 2: Recall the first law of reflection: Angle of incidence equals angle of reflection ($\angle i = \angle r$).
- Step 3: Substitute the value for the angle of reflection: $\angle r = 35°$.
- Step 4: Compute the total angle by adding the two angles: Total angle = $\angle i + \angle r = 35° + 35° = 70°$.
- Step 5: Verify the answer physically: Both angles are equal relative to the normal, and 70° is a reasonable geometric sum for an acute angle of incidence.
REASONING: The first law of reflection dictates complete symmetry across the normal line. Whatever angle the incoming ray makes with the normal, the outgoing ray must match it.
ANSWER: Angle of reflection = 35°, Total angle between rays = 70°
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A beam of sunlight hits a smooth water surface in a pond at a certain angle. If the angle between the reflected ray and the surface of the water is 55°, calculate the angle of incidence in degrees.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the given value: Angle between reflected ray and mirror surface = 55°. Identify what to find: Angle of incidence ($\angle i$).
- Step 2: Recall that the normal line is perpendicular (90°) to the water surface.
- Step 3: Calculate the angle of reflection ($\angle r$) from the normal: $\angle r = 90° - 55° = 35°$.
- Step 4: Apply the first law of reflection, which states that the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection ($\angle i = \angle r$).
- Step 5: State the final calculated value for the angle of incidence with units: $\angle i = 35°$.
REASONING: Students must remember that angles in reflection problems are always measured from the normal line, not the surface. Subtracting the surface angle from 90° gives the correct angle relative to the normal.
ANSWER: 35°
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A ray of light strikes a plane mirror with an angle of incidence of 42°. If the mirror is rotated slightly such that the angle of incidence increases by 8°, what will be the new angle between the incident ray and the new reflected ray?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the initial angle of incidence ($\angle i_1$ = 42°) and the increase in the angle of incidence ($\Delta i$ = 8°).
- Step 2: Calculate the new angle of incidence ($\angle i_2$): $\angle i_2 = 42° + 8° = 50°$.
- Step 3: Apply the first law of reflection to find the new angle of reflection ($\angle r_2$): Since $\angle i_2 = \angle r_2$, the new angle of reflection is also 50°.
- Step 4: Compute the total angle between the incident ray and the reflected ray for this new position: Total angle = $\angle i_2 + \angle r_2 = 50° + 50° = 100°$.
- Step 5: Verify that the math follows linear addition and satisfies the laws of reflection.
REASONING: Changing the tilt of the mirror alters the normal's position relative to the incoming ray. Once the new angle of incidence is found, the law of reflection allows us to sum both sides to find the total spread between the incoming and outgoing light paths.
ANSWER: 100°
