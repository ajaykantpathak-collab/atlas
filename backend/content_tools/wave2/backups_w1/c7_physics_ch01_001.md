## ELI5
Watch water boiling on the kitchen stove - bubbles rise, steam escapes, the level drops - and you are watching science happen. Science is not the shelf of textbook answers; it is the process: notice something, ask a curious question, explore with observations and experiments, reason to a conclusion. Every chapter you study is somebody's question that got answered carefully. The method is the subject; the facts are just the souvenirs.

## DEFINITION
Science as a process is a systematic method of inquiry used to investigate natural phenomena, starting from asking curious questions and exploring through observations, experiments, and logical reasoning. There are no specific physical units for this overarching process as it is a cognitive and investigative framework rather than a measured physical quantity.

## MASTERY_CRITERIA

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers the foundational process of formulating questions, designing qualitative and simple quantitative explorations, and recognizing patterns from data. Does not cover advanced multi-variable statistical analysis or complex laboratory instrumentation beyond middle school scope.
## WALKTHROUGH
Observe water boiling in a kitchen vessel on a stove: bubbles rise, steam escapes, and the water level gradually drops. To begin understanding this scientifically, we must ask "Why does steam rise?" and "Where does the water go?" rather than just accepting it as magic. We connect this to our prior understanding of heat transfer and states of matter from Class 6, where we learned how heat flows through liquids and gases. We then form a tentative explanation or hypothesis and design a simple, safe exploration to test it. The core principle governing this process is that every scientific discovery begins with organized curiosity followed by systematic testing.

**Takeaway:** science is a verb before it is a noun: question, explore, record, conclude - in that order. **In the exam:** in process questions, name each step with an example - the boiling pot gives you observation and question free. **Check yourself:** run the walkthrough's pot: what do you see, what would you ask, what would you measure? **Why students miss it:** students recite facts and call it science - the flagged mix-up. The examiner asks for the method: what was observed, what was asked, what was controlled. Answer in verbs and the process definition stops being a formality.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A student wants to test if a toy car rolls faster on a smooth marble floor or a rough carpet. If the car travels 3 meters on the marble floor in 6 seconds, what is its average speed?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Steps: 1. Identify given values: Distance ($d$) = 3 m, Time ($t$) = 6 s. 2. Recall speed formula: Speed = Distance / Time. 3. Substitute values: Speed = 3 m / 6 s. 4. Compute: Speed = 0.5 m/s. 5. Verify: 0.5 m/s is a reasonable speed for a toy car rolling across a room. A wrong answer like 2 m/s usually stems from dividing time by distance instead of distance by time.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6

A poor answer lacks a controlled variable (like using different amounts of water or different containers) or fails to state how measurement is done.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think that science is just a collection of memorized facts found in textbooks rather than an ongoing, active process of questioning and exploring. For example, when studying how a shadow changes length throughout the day, a student might just memorize "shadows are long in the morning" without understanding how to observe, measure, and question *why* the sun's angle causes this change.
## ON_FAILURE
To fix this, guide the student to perform a hands-on activity where they observe a moving object or changing shadow in the schoolyard. Ask them: "What do you see happening right now?" and "What question can you ask about why it changes?" Help them write down their own question, make a simple prediction, and test it by taking measurements over a 15-minute interval.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A student notices that a bicycle tyre left in the hot afternoon sun feels much harder than it did in the cool morning. Formulate a scientific question, a possible reason (hypothesis), and a 4-step exploration plan to investigate it.
STEPS:
- [step 1: State the initial observation of the tyre hardening in the heat.]
- [step 2: Formulate the scientific question: "Does an increase in temperature increase the pressure of air inside a sealed container?"]
- [step 3: Propose a hypothesis: "Higher temperature makes air particles move faster, increasing pressure."]
- [step 4: Design the exploration steps: (1) Connect a bicycle pump with a pressure gauge to a tyre, (2) Record initial pressure in shade, (3) Leave the setup in sunlight for 30 minutes, (4) Record the final pressure and compare.]
- [step 5: Verify physical reasonableness: Air expands or exerts higher pressure when heated, which matches everyday experience with tyre pressure.]
REASONING: Scientific exploration requires moving from a casual observation to a structured question, framing a testable hypothesis, and setting up a controlled procedure to gather comparative data.
ANSWER: Question: Does temperature affect enclosed air pressure? Exploration: Measure pressure before and after sun exposure using a gauge.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A student observes that a puddle of water on a concrete courtyard disappears faster on a windy day than on a calm day. Outline the 4-step scientific process used to explore this phenomenon.
STEPS:
- [step 1: Document the observation—puddles evaporate quicker when wind blows.]
- [step 2: Ask the specific scientific question—"How does wind speed affect the rate of water evaporation?"]
- [step 3: Design a controlled test: Take two plates, put exactly 20 ml of water in each, place one in still air and blow a gentle fan over the other.]
- [step 4: Measure the time taken for all water to vanish in both plates and record the data.]
- [step 5: Draw a conclusion: Moving air increases the rate of evaporation by sweeping away water vapor molecules.]
REASONING: Breaking down an everyday phenomenon into a controlled test isolates the variable of interest (wind speed) while keeping others (temperature, surface area) constant.
ANSWER: Wind speed increases evaporation rate by removing saturated air above the liquid surface.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A student building a simple electric circuit notices that the bulb glows dimmer when a long wire is used compared to a short wire. Structure a complete scientific inquiry plan with 5 steps to explore this issue.
STEPS:
- [step 1: State the observation—bulb brightness decreases when wire length increases in a circuit.]
- [step 2: Pose the research question—"How does the length of a connecting wire affect the brightness of a bulb in a simple circuit?"]
- [step 3: Formulate a hypothesis—"Longer wires offer more resistance to electric current, causing the bulb to glow dimmer."]
- [step 4: Set up the exploration: Use a fixed battery and bulb, then test three different wire lengths (10 cm, 50 cm, 100 cm), recording observations of brightness for each.]
- [step 5: Analyze the data: Compare brightness levels across the wire lengths to see if a clear pattern emerges.]
- [step 6: Formulate final conclusion: Wire length is inversely related to current flow due to increased electrical resistance.]
REASONING: This multi-step inquiry bridges prior electrical circuit knowledge from Class 6 with scientific questioning, ensuring variables are systematically altered and observed.
ANSWER: Increasing wire length decreases bulb brightness due to greater resistance in longer conductors.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You drop two identical sheets of paper from the same height at the same time—one is flat, and the other is crumpled into a tight ball. The crumpled ball hits the floor first. A friend says, "Heavy things always fall faster." Using the scientific process of questioning and exploring, how would you evaluate and correct your friend's statement?
COMPETENCY: Evaluating hypotheses and identifying confounding variables in physical phenomena
STEPS:
- [step 1: Analyze the friend's claim against the observation.]
- [step 2: Identify the actual variable causing the difference (air resistance acting on surface area, not mass, since both papers have the same mass).]
- [step 3: Design a quick mental or physical counter-test (drop two heavy objects of different masses through a vacuum or shape them identically).]
- [step 4: Formulate the corrected scientific conclusion.]
ANSWER: The friend's statement is incorrect because both paper sheets have the exact same mass. The flat sheet falls slower due to greater air resistance (surface area interaction), not because it is lighter. When air resistance is minimized (as in the crumpled ball), both fall at similar rates.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A farmer notices that crops near a metal shed grow slightly differently than crops in the open field. Instead of guessing that "metal attracts bad luck," propose a scientific inquiry pathway consisting of a question, a hypothesis, and two specific measurements the farmer should make to explore the cause.
COMPETENCY: Designing scientific investigations and replacing unverified assumptions with empirical data
STEPS:
- [step 1: Replace superstitious assumption with an objective observation.]
- [step 2: Formulate a testable scientific question regarding environmental factors near sheds (e.g., shade, heat reflection, soil moisture).]
- [step 3: Propose a logical hypothesis based on physical principles (e.g., metal sheds reflect heat or block sunlight, altering local temperature).]
- [step 4: Define two specific measurable parameters (soil temperature and daily sunlight hours).]
- [step 5: Outline how comparing these measurements between the shed area and open field will test the hypothesis.]
ANSWER: Question: "Does the metal shed alter soil temperature and sunlight duration for nearby crops?" Hypothesis: "The metal shed reflects solar radiation, raising local ambient temperature." Measurements: 1. Measure daily maximum soil temperature near the shed versus the open field. 2. Measure hours of direct sunlight received in both locations.
DIFFICULTY: hard
