## ELI5
During a power cut, the torchlight is the government of your study table: cell, switch, lamp in one tube. Slide the switch, and chemical energy stored in the cell converts to electrical energy, marches through the circuit, and gives itself up as light in the bulb. Three parts, one handshake. A torch that fails is just a broken handshake - dead cell, loose contact, or a switch that never closes the loop.

## DEFINITION
A torchlight is a portable handheld lighting device that uses an electric cell, a switch, and a small electric lamp housed in a casing to convert chemical energy into electrical energy and then into light energy. It operates by forming a complete, continuous closed loop for electric current to flow through when the switch is closed.

## WALKTHROUGH
When you slide the switch of a household torchlight in your room during a power cut, the bulb instantly lights up your study table. To understand how this happens, we must open the torch casing and observe its internal anatomy: an electric cell (or battery), a metallic spring, a sliding metal strip (switch), and a small bulb resting in a reflector. Building upon our earlier understanding of simple circuits from Class 6, the torch is simply a cleverly packaged circuit where the metal casing and strips act as conducting wires. When you push the switch, you bridge a physical gap, closing the loop so that electric current flows continuously from the positive terminal of the cell, through the lamp filament, and back to the negative terminal. Let us analyze how these components work together systematically.

**Takeaway:** a torchlight is a complete circuit in your palm: cell (chemical to electrical), switch (control), lamp (electrical to light). **In the exam:** trace the current's path aloud - cell to switch to lamp and back - when asked why a torch fails. **Check yourself:** slide the walkthrough's switch during a power cut and narrate the two energy conversions it triggers. **Why students miss it:** students blame the bulb first - the flagged reflex. The circuit fails as a loop, so test each link: cell's charge, contacts, switch, then lamp. Answer with the path, and troubleshooting becomes a route map instead of a guess.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why a torchlight will not glow if you insert the electric cell with its negative terminal facing the bulb and the positive terminal touching the bottom spring, assuming all other connections are intact.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Electric current requires a specific direction of flow from the positive terminal through the circuit components to the negative terminal; however, as long as a complete metallic path exists, current can still flow. The actual reason a reversed cell might fail in a torch is if the spring or contact plate is specifically shaped or insulated to only touch the protruding positive terminal correctly, or if the circuit design relies on the casing polarity. A wrong answer claiming "electricity stops instantly because cells only work one way" shows a misunderstanding of current flow direction versus physical contact geometry.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A torchlight bulb has a resistance of $5\,\Omega$ and draws a current of $0.6\,\text{A}$ from two cells connected in series. What is the total voltage supplied across the bulb? (Use $V = I \times R$, where $V$ is voltage in volts, $I$ is current in amperes, and $R$ is resistance in ohms).
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Identify given values: Current $I = 0.6\,\text{A}$, Resistance $R = 5\,\Omega$.
Step 2: Identify the formula: $V = I \times R$.
Step 3: Substitute values with units: $V = 0.6\,\text{A} \times 5\,\Omega$.
Step 4: Compute and track unit cancellation: $V = 3\,\text{V}$ (since amperes $\times$ ohms equals volts).
Step 5: Verify the answer is physically reasonable: $3\,\text{V}$ matches the typical voltage of two dry cells ($1.5\,\text{V} + 1.5\,\text{V}$).
Answer: $3\,\text{V}$. A wrong answer like $11\,\text{V}$ or $0.12\,\text{V}$ reveals an error in multiplying decimals or confusing addition with multiplication.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Analyze what happens inside a torchlight circuit when the sliding switch is moved to the "OFF" position. How does this affect the flow of electric charge?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Moving the switch to the "OFF" position creates an air gap in the metallic conduction path, breaking the closed loop into an open circuit. Because electric current requires an unbroken conducting path to flow, the movement of charges stops instantly, causing the bulb to go dark. A wrong answer stating "charges are stored up inside the switch when turned off" reveals a misconception that electric current behaves like stagnant water dammed in a pipe rather than flowing charges in a complete loop.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate the following statement: "A torchlight can work perfectly even if the metal spring at the bottom of the casing is replaced by a thick dry wooden stick." Is this statement correct or incorrect, and why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is incorrect. Wood is an electrical insulator that blocks the flow of electric current. The metal spring is essential because it is a conductor that completes the circuit path from the cell's terminal to the metal casing. A wrong answer saying "it will work because wood still touches the cell" indicates a failure to distinguish between physical contact and electrical conductivity.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a troubleshooting checklist for a student whose torchlight fails to light up when the switch is pushed. List at least three specific physical checks they should perform.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Criteria for a good answer: 1. Check if the electric cells are inserted with correct polarity orientation. 2. Inspect the thin wire filament inside the bulb to see if it is broken (fused). 3. Check the metal contacts and switch for corrosion, dirt, or loose connections that break the circuit. A poor answer gives vague advice like "shake the torch hard until it works" without identifying scientific components.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that a torchlight bulb lights up because electricity is "consumed" or "used up" inside the bulb without needing a return path back to the cell. They think a single wire connected from the cell's positive terminal to the bulb is enough, ignoring the need for a complete closed loop through the casing or a second wire to the negative terminal.
## ON_FAILURE
Remediate by taking a torchlight apart in class. First, connect a wire only from the positive terminal of a cell to the side of a bulb and observe that it does not glow. Next, complete the loop by adding a second wire from the negative terminal to the bottom contact of the bulb, showing that a continuous closed loop is mandatory for the bulb to light up.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A student examines a torchlight where the metal reflector touches the side terminal of the bulb. If the bulb operates at $2.5\,\text{V}$ and draws $0.4\,\text{A}$ of current when the switch is closed for $10\,\text{seconds}$, calculate the total electrical charge that flows through the torch circuit. (Use $Q = I \times t$, where $Q$ is charge in coulombs, $I$ is current in amperes, and $t$ is time in seconds).
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given values with units: Current $I = 0.4\,\text{A}$, Time $t = 10\,\text{s}$.
- Step 2: Identify the correct formula relating charge, current, and time: $Q = I \times t$.
- Step 3: Substitute the given values into the formula with their units: $Q = 0.4\,\text{A} \times 10\,\text{s}$.
- Step 4: Compute the numerical value and track unit cancellation: $0.4 \times 10 = 4$, and amperes $\times$ seconds ($\text{A} \cdot \text{s}$) equals coulombs ($\text{C}$).
- Step 5: Verify the answer is physically reasonable: $4\,\text{C}$ of charge flowing in $10\,\text{seconds}$ for a small torch bulb is a realistic magnitude.
REASONING: Electric charge flowing through a circuit is defined as the product of steady electric current and the time duration for which the current flows. Tracking units ensures that amperes multiplied by seconds correctly yields coulombs.
ANSWER: $4\,\text{C}$
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A torchlight uses two dry cells in series, each providing $1.5\,\text{V}$. If the total resistance of the torch circuit including the bulb is $6\,\Omega$, calculate the current flowing through the circuit. (Use $I = \frac{V}{R}$, where $I$ is current in amperes, $V$ is total voltage in volts, and $R$ is resistance in ohms).
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given values with units: Cell voltage $V_1 = 1.5\,\text{V}$ each, total cells = 2, Resistance $R = 6\,\Omega$.
- Step 2: Calculate total voltage by adding cells in series: $V_{\text{total}} = 1.5\,\text{V} + 1.5\,\text{V} = 3.0\,\text{V}$.
- Step 3: Identify the formula relating current, voltage, and resistance: $I = \frac{V}{R}$.
- Step 4: Substitute values with units: $I = \frac{3.0\,\text{V}}{6\,\Omega}$.
- Step 5: Compute and track unit cancellation: $3.0 / 6 = 0.5$, and volts divided by ohms ($\text{V}/\Omega$) yields amperes ($\text{A}$). Verify reasonableness: $0.5\,\text{A}$ is a standard current for a small torch bulb.
REASONING: When cells are placed end-to-end in series inside a torch, their voltages add up. Ohm's Law allows us to determine the resulting current by dividing this total voltage by the total resistance of the circuit.
ANSWER: $0.5\,\text{A}$
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A torchlight runs continuously for $2\,\text{minutes}$ ($120\,\text{seconds}$). The current flowing through its filament is $0.5\,\text{A}$, and the voltage across the lamp is $3\,\text{V}$. Calculate the total electrical energy consumed by the torch bulb during this time. (Use $E = V \times I \times t$, where $E$ is energy in joules, $V$ is voltage in volts, $I$ is current in amperes, and $t$ is time in seconds).
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given values with units: Voltage $V = 3\,\text{V}$, Current $I = 0.5\,\text{A}$, Time $t = 120\,\text{s}$.
- Step 2: Identify the formula for electrical energy: $E = V \times I \times t$.
- Step 3: Substitute the given numerical values and units into the formula: $E = 3\,\text{V} \times 0.5\,\text{A} \times 120\,\text{s}$.
- Step 4: Multiply the terms step-by-step: $3 \times 0.5 = 1.5$, then $1.5 \times 120 = 180$.
- Step 5: Track unit cancellation: volts $\times$ amperes $\times$ seconds ($\text{V} \cdot \text{A} \cdot \text{s}$) equals joules ($\text{J}$).
- Step 6: Verify the answer is physically reasonable: $180\,\text{J}$ of energy consumed over two minutes for a handheld torch is a realistic value.
REASONING: Electrical energy is the product of potential difference, current, and time. Multiplying voltage, current, and time correctly accounts for the total power delivered over the given duration.
ANSWER: $180\,\text{J}$

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: In a faulty torchlight, the switch is closed and the cell is fresh, but the bulb filament is broken (fused). If you connect a voltmeter across the terminals of the fused bulb while the switch is closed, what voltage will it read compared to the cell voltage, and why does this happen?
COMPETENCY: Predicting outcomes from changing variables and analyzing circuit breaks
STEPS:
- Step 1: Examine the condition of the circuit: switch is closed, but the bulb filament has a physical break (open circuit).
- Step 2: Recall that in an open circuit with a break, no current flows through the circuit components.
- Step 3: Apply circuit rules to determine potential difference across an open break.
- Step 4: Conclude that the full voltage of the cell appears across the break (the fused bulb terminals) because no voltage is dropped across other functional parts when current is zero.
ANSWER: The voltmeter will read the full voltage of the cell (e.g., $3\,\text{V}$). This happens because a fused bulb creates an open gap in the circuit where current cannot pass. Since no current flows through the rest of the circuit, there is no voltage drop across the conducting wires, meaning the full potential difference of the power source appears directly across the break in the filament.
DIFFICULTY: hard
