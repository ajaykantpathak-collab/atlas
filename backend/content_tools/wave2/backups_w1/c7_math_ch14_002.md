## ELI5
You and Priya stand at opposite ends of a school corridor and a plant pot must sit exactly halfway, squared to the wall - that spot is the perpendicular bisector's work. From each endpoint, swing arcs with a compass opened more than half the corridor's length; the arc crossings line up above and below, and the line through them cuts the segment into equal halves at a perfect right angle. Wider or narrower settings only move the arcs, never the final line.

## DEFINITION
A perpendicular bisector of a line segment is a line, ray, or line segment that divides the given line segment into two equal halves (bisects it) and intersects it at a right angle ($90^\circ$). Using a compass and ruler, it is constructed by drawing intersecting arcs from both endpoints of the segment with a radius greater than half its length.

## MASTERY_CRITERIA

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you and your friend Priya are standing at opposite ends of a long school corridor, and you want to place a plant pot on the floor exactly halfway between you, but positioned in a way that forms a straight T-junction with the corridor wall. How do you find that exact spot without a measuring tape? Just like drawing a neat symmetry line across a paper fold, we can use a compass to swing two pairs of matching arcs from each endpoint of our line segment. When we connect the points where these arcs cross above and below the line, we create a dividing line that cuts our original segment squarely in half at $90^\circ$. Building on our previous work with basic line segments and angles from Class 6 geometry, we now use our compass as a measuring tool to guarantee both equal distance and exact perpendicularity. The key insight is that any point on the perpendicular bisector is equidistant from the two endpoints of the line segment.

**Takeaway:** a perpendicular bisector is two promises at once: equal halves and a square crossing. **In the exam:** open the compass past half the segment, cut matching arcs from both ends, and join the crossings. **Check yourself:** fold-test your drawing: measure both halves and the corner angle to certify equal and square. **Why students miss it:** students open the compass short of half the length and the arcs never meet - no crossing, no bisector. Any radius past half works; the crossings simply land higher or lower while the final line stays put.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the primary geometric definition of a perpendicular bisector of a line segment?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
It is a line that divides the segment into two equal parts and forms a $90^\circ$ angle with it. A student who answers that it only cuts the line in half without mentioning the right angle confuses a regular bisector with a perpendicular bisector, missing the key $90^\circ$ condition.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
When constructing a perpendicular bisector using a compass, why must the opening (radius) of the compass be set to more than half the length of the line segment?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The radius must exceed half the length so that the arcs drawn from both endpoints intersect each other both above and below the line segment. A student who thinks any random compass opening works will find that the arcs fail to cross each other, making it impossible to locate the two necessary intersection points for the dividing line.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If you construct the perpendicular bisector of line segment $AB$ of length $8\text{ cm}$ and it intersects $AB$ at point $M$, what is the exact length of segment $AM$?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The length of $AM$ is $4\text{ cm}$. Steps: 1) Identify that a perpendicular bisector splits a line segment into two equal halves. 2) Divide the total length by $2$: $8\text{ cm} \div 2 = 4\text{ cm}$. 3) Since $M$ is the midpoint, $AM = MB = 4\text{ cm}$. A student who answers $8\text{ cm}$ or $2\text{ cm}$ has forgotten that "bisect" means to divide into two equal parts.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
How does changing the compass radius (while keeping it greater than half the segment length) affect the position of the final perpendicular bisector line?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
It does not change the position of the final line at all; it only changes where the intersection arcs are drawn higher or lower on the page. A student who believes a larger radius shifts the bisector off-center does not understand that two points determine a unique straight line, and any valid pair of intersection points yields the exact same perpendicular line.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student constructs a line through the midpoint of $AB$, but measures the angle with a protractor and finds it is $85^\circ$. Is this line a valid perpendicular bisector? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, it is not a valid perpendicular bisector because the angle must be exactly $90^\circ$, whereas $85^\circ$ is acute and not perpendicular. A student who says yes ignores the strict perpendicular requirement in favor of only checking the bisecting property.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a construction challenge where a carpenter needs to find the exact center of a wooden plank of length $12\text{ cm}$ without using a ruler marked with numbers, relying solely on a compass and an unmarked straightedge. Describe the steps.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Steps: 1) Place the compass pointer on one end of the $12\text{ cm}$ plank and open it to roughly $8\text{ cm}$ (more than half). 2) Draw arcs above and below the plank. 3) Keep the exact same compass width, place the pointer on the other end of the plank, and draw intersecting arcs above and below. 4) Use the straightedge to connect the top and bottom intersection points. 5) Mark the spot where this connecting line crosses the plank; that point is the exact center ($6\text{ cm}$). A poor answer suggests guessing or folding the wood, which fails the geometric construction requirement.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often change the compass radius halfway through the construction when moving from one endpoint to the other (e.g., using a radius of $6\text{ cm}$ from point $A$ and a different radius of $5\text{ cm}$ from point $B$). This results in asymmetric arcs that do not line up correctly, causing the drawn bisector to either miss the true midpoint or fail to form a $90^\circ$ angle.
## ON_FAILURE
Remind the student that the compass width represents a fixed distance. Say: "When you swing the arc from point $A$, lock your compass tight. Do not touch the hinge! Move the exact same locked compass to point $B$ to draw the second set of arcs. If the radius changes, the distance is no longer equal, and our symmetry is broken." Have them restart by redrawing both arcs with a single fixed compass setting.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Rahul wants to divide a metal wire of length $10\text{ cm}$ into two equal halves for a craft project using a compass and ruler. Construct the perpendicular bisector and state the length of each resulting half-segment.
STEPS:
- step 1: Draw a line segment $AB = 10\text{ cm}$ horizontally using a ruler in your notebook.
- step 2: Place the compass pointer on point $A$ and adjust its width to more than half of $10\text{ cm}$ (e.g., set it to $7\text{ cm}$).
- step 3: Draw two arcs, one above the segment $AB$ and one below the segment $AB$.
- step 4: Without changing the compass width, place the compass pointer on point $B$ and draw two intersecting arcs that cut the previous arcs above and below the line at points $P$ and $Q$.
- step 5: Use a ruler to join points $P$ and $Q$ with a straight line that intersects $AB$ at midpoint $M$, and verify that $AM = MB = 5\text{ cm}$ and $\angle PMA = 90^\circ$.
REASONING: By opening the compass to a radius greater than half the length, we ensure the arcs drawn from $A$ and $B$ overlap. Connecting the two intersection points creates a line of symmetry where every point is equidistant from $A$ and $B$, guaranteeing both a $5\text{ cm}$ split and a $90^\circ$ angle.
ANSWER: Each half-segment is $5\text{ cm}$ long, intersected at $90^\circ$.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: An architect is designing a courtyard partition and needs to find the exact midpoint of a stone boundary wall represented by segment $XY = 12.4\text{ cm}$ on a blueprint. Perform the construction steps and calculate the distance from either end to the midpoint.
STEPS:
- step 1: Draw segment $XY = 12.4\text{ cm}$ on the blueprint paper.
- step 2: Choose a compass radius greater than half of $12.4\text{ cm}$ (half is $6.2\text{ cm}$, so choose $7.5\text{ cm}$).
- step 3: Place the compass tip at $X$ and swing arcs above and below segment $XY$.
- step 4: Keeping the compass radius at $7.5\text{ cm}$, place the tip at $Y$ and swing arcs to intersect the first set of arcs at points $R$ and $S$.
- step 5: Draw line segment $RS$ intersecting $XY$ at point $N$, and compute the length: $12.4\text{ cm} \div 2 = 6.2\text{ cm}$.
REASONING: The construction method works for any decimal length as long as the radius exceeds half the total measurement. Dividing the total length in half gives the exact position of the midpoint $N$ along $XY$.
ANSWER: The distance from $X$ to $N$ is $6.2\text{ cm}$, and line $RS$ is perpendicular to $XY$.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A school playground has a straight running track represented by line segment $CD$. Maintenance staff need to mark a penalty spot $P$ that is exactly halfway between $C$ and $D$, and also build a perpendicular drainage ditch along line $PQ$. If $CD = 16\text{ cm}$, and the drainage ditch extends $4\text{ cm}$ on either side of the track, find the total length of the drainage ditch segment within those bounds and verify the geometric properties.
STEPS:
- step 1: Draw line segment $CD = 16\text{ cm}$ representing the track.
- step 2: Set the compass width to $10\text{ cm}$ (which is greater than half of $16\text{ cm}$, i.e., $8\text{ cm}$).
- step 3: With center $C$ and radius $10\text{ cm}$, draw arcs above and below segment $CD$.
- step 4: With center $D$ and radius $10\text{ cm}$, draw intersecting arcs cutting the previous arcs at $E$ and $F$.
- step 5: Draw line $EF$ cutting $CD$ at midpoint $P$, such that $CP = PD = 8\text{ cm}$.
- step 6: Measure $4\text{ cm}$ above $P$ to point $Q_1$ and $4\text{ cm}$ below $P$ to point $Q_2$ along the perpendicular line $EF$, making the total drainage ditch length $Q_1Q_2 = 4\text{ cm} + 4\text{ cm} = 8\text{ cm}$.
REASONING: The perpendicular bisector provides the precise midpoint $P$ and the $90^\circ$ line of orientation. Extending equal lengths ($4\text{ cm}$) outward along this perpendicular maintains symmetry relative to the track.
ANSWER: Midpoint $P$ divides $CD$ into two $8\text{ cm}$ segments, and the drainage ditch segment $Q_1Q_2$ has a total length of $8\text{ cm}$ perpendicular to the track.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Meera constructed a perpendicular bisector to line segment $AB$, but her compass slipped while drawing the second arc from point $B$. Explain how she can determine whether her current intersection points still form a valid perpendicular bisector, and what specific tests she should perform on her drawing.
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction
STEPS:
- step 1: Understand that a valid perpendicular bisector must satisfy two conditions: it must split the segment into two equal halves, and the intersection angle must be $90^\circ$.
- step 2: Test the bisecting property by measuring lengths $AM$ and $MB$ with a ruler to check if $AM = MB$.
- step 3: Test the perpendicular property by placing a protractor at the intersection point $M$ to verify that the angle is exactly $90^\circ$.
- step 4: If either test fails due to the compass slip, erase the incorrect arcs and repeat the construction with a steady hand.
ANSWER: Meera must check two things: first, measure both halves ($AM$ and $MB$) to ensure they are equal; second, measure the angle at the intersection with a protractor to confirm it is $90^\circ$. If either check fails, the slip affected the construction and she must redo it.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Two students, Amit and Sneha, are given a line segment $EF = 14\text{ cm}$. Amit uses a compass radius of $8\text{ cm}$ from both ends, while Sneha uses a compass radius of $11\text{ cm}$ from both ends. Both successfully draw intersecting arcs and connect them. Will their final perpendicular bisector lines be in different positions on the paper? Justify your mathematical reasoning.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying geometric methods
STEPS:
- step 1: Analyze Amit's method: radius $8\text{ cm}$ is greater than half of $14\text{ cm}$ ($7\text{ cm}$), so his arcs intersect and form a valid perpendicular bisector.
- step 2: Analyze Sneha's method: radius $11\text{ cm}$ is also greater than half of $14\text{ cm}$ ($7\text{ cm}$), so her arcs intersect and form a valid perpendicular bisector.
- step 3: Recognize that any point on a perpendicular bisector is equidistant from the endpoints $E$ and $F$.
- step 4: Conclude that regardless of whether the radius is $8\text{ cm}$ or $11\text{ cm}$, the set of all points equidistant from $E$ and $F$ forms one unique straight line.
ANSWER: No, their final perpendicular bisector lines will be in the exact same position. Any radius greater than half the segment length creates intersection points that lie on the unique line of symmetry equidistant from the endpoints, so the choice of radius does not change the final line's location.
