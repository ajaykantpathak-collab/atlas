## ELI5
A convex mirror scatters parallel rays outward, but the scatter is not chaotic — the escaping rays behave as if all of them had sprung from a point hidden behind the mirror: its principal focus. Since no light actually passes through that spot, the focus is virtual, and the focal length measuring it still equals half the radius of curvature. The mirror thus sees wide angles at once — which is why vehicles trust this bent sentinel for their rear view.

## DEFINITION
The principal focus of a convex mirror is the point on the principal axis where incident rays parallel to the principal axis appear to diverge from after reflection. The focal length (f) is the distance between the pole (P) and the principal focus (F); for a convex mirror, the focus lies behind the mirror, so the focal length is taken as positive in the Cartesian sign convention and is equal to half the radius of curvature (f = R/2).

## WALKTHROUGH
Observe the outer curved surface of a stainless steel spoon or a car's side-view mirror — you see a diminished, upright image of the surroundings. This is a convex mirror. Recall from c8_physics_ch6_2 that the centre of curvature (C) is behind the reflecting surface. Shine a laser pointer or torch beam parallel to the principal axis onto the convex surface; the reflected ray diverges. Extend the reflected ray backward behind the mirror — it meets the principal axis at a point F. This virtual point is the principal focus. Measure the distance PF; it is exactly half of PC (the radius of curvature). Thus, f = R/2.

Takeaway: the convex mirror's principal focus is virtual, behind the mirror, where reflected diverging rays appear to originate — with focal length still half the radius of curvature. Exam format: expect ray diagrams with backward dashed extensions locating F behind the mirror, the half-of-radius calculation, and the wide-field-of-view justification for vehicle mirrors. Verification beat: solid lines for real rays, dashes for the backward extensions — the virtual focus must never be drawn as a real crossing. Why students miss it: they put the focus in front — a convex mirror sends rays apart; only their backwards imagination meets, and that is exactly where F lives. Erect, diminished, wide-view: the driver's trinity, all from one virtual point.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
A student says, "The focal length of a convex mirror is negative because the focus is behind the mirror." Using the Cartesian sign convention, explain why this statement is incorrect.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
In the Cartesian sign convention, distances measured opposite to the direction of incident light are positive. For a convex mirror, the incident light travels from the object towards the mirror; the focus is behind the mirror, i.e., opposite to the incident light direction, so the focal length is positive. A wrong answer indicates the student has memorised "convex = negative" from a different sign convention (like the old real-is-positive convention) without understanding the Cartesian rule.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
The radius of curvature of a convex mirror used as a rear-view mirror in a car is 2.0 m. Calculate its focal length. Give the answer with proper sign and unit.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Given: R = +2.0 m (centre of curvature behind mirror → positive in Cartesian convention). Formula: f = R/2. Substitution: f = (+2.0 m)/2 = +1.0 m. The focal length is +1.0 m. A wrong answer with a negative sign shows sign convention error; missing unit or using cm without conversion shows unit negligence.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Two convex mirrors A and B have radii of curvature 30 cm and 50 cm respectively. Which mirror has a longer focal length? How does the focal length affect the field of view?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Mirror B (R = 50 cm) has focal length f = 25 cm; Mirror A has f = 15 cm. Mirror B has longer focal length. A longer focal length (less curved mirror) gives a narrower field of view but larger image size; a shorter focal length (more curved) gives a wider field of view but smaller images. A wrong choice reveals inability to connect focal length with curvature and practical consequence.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A diagram shows a convex mirror with pole P, centre of curvature C, and a point F marked on the principal axis in front of the mirror (between P and C). A student labels F as the principal focus. Evaluate this diagram and correct the error.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The diagram is incorrect. For a convex mirror, the principal focus must be behind the mirror (on the opposite side of the reflecting surface), not in front. The point F should be located behind the mirror such that PF = PC/2. The student has confused convex with concave mirror focus position. This error shows lack of visualisation of virtual focus.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple activity using a convex mirror (e.g., a shiny metal bowl), a laser pointer, and a screen to demonstrate that the principal focus of a convex mirror is virtual. Describe the steps and what you would observe.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Steps: (1) Fix the convex mirror on a stand. (2) Direct a laser beam parallel to the principal axis onto the mirror. (3) Observe the reflected beam diverges. (4) Place a screen in front of the mirror — no sharp spot forms. (5) Extend the reflected ray backward using a ruler or string behind the mirror; the extensions meet at a point F behind the mirror. Observation: The focus cannot be captured on a screen in front; it is virtual. A poor answer that tries to catch the focus on a screen in front shows misunderstanding of virtual vs real focus.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think the principal focus of a convex mirror is a real point in front of the mirror where light actually converges, just like a concave mirror. They draw reflected rays meeting in front of the mirror. This happens because they over-generalise the concave mirror behaviour and do not visualise that convex mirrors cause divergence; the focus is formed by extending diverging rays backward behind the mirror.
## ON_FAILURE
Use a shiny stainless steel bowl (convex surface) and a laser pointer in a darkened room. Shine the laser parallel to the principal axis; mark the incident and reflected ray directions on paper. Extend the reflected ray backward with a dashed line behind the bowl — the intersection is the virtual focus. Repeat for 3-4 parallel rays; all extensions meet at the same point behind the mirror. This concrete tracing forces the student to see divergence and backward extension.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A convex mirror used as a security mirror in a shop has a radius of curvature of 4.0 m. Calculate its focal length. Express the answer in metres with correct sign according to the Cartesian sign convention.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given quantities: R = +4.0 m (positive because centre of curvature is behind the mirror, opposite to incident light direction). Find: f = ?
- Step 2: Recall the formula relating focal length and radius of curvature for spherical mirrors: f = R/2. Check units: both in metres, so answer will be in metres.
- Step 3: Substitute the value: f = (+4.0 m) / 2.
- Step 4: Compute: f = +2.0 m. The positive sign indicates the focus is behind the mirror (virtual focus).
- Step 5: Verify: A security mirror needs a wide field of view, which corresponds to a shorter focal length (more curvature). Here f = 2.0 m is reasonable for a large mirror. Answer: +2.0 m.

REASONING: The formula f = R/2 comes from the geometry of a spherical mirror: the focal point is the midpoint of the radius of curvature. For a convex mirror, both R and f are positive in the Cartesian convention because they are measured opposite to the incident light direction.

ANSWER: +2.0 m
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: The focal length of a convex mirror fitted on a scooter's side-view mirror is +0.25 m. What is its radius of curvature? Give the answer in centimetres.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Given: f = +0.25 m (positive, virtual focus behind mirror). Find: R in cm.
- Step 2: Use the formula f = R/2. Rearrange to R = 2f. Ensure units: convert metres to centimetres at the end (1 m = 100 cm).
- Step 3: Substitute f in metres: R = 2 × (+0.25 m) = +0.50 m.
- Step 4: Convert to centimetres: R = 0.50 m × 100 cm/m = +50 cm.
- Step 5: Check reasonableness: A side-view mirror is small; a radius of curvature of 50 cm (diameter 1 m) is plausible for a curved mirror surface. The positive sign confirms the centre of curvature is behind the mirror.

REASONING: The relationship R = 2f is the inverse of f = R/2. Converting to centimetres is practical because small mirrors are usually measured in cm. The sign remains positive throughout, consistent with the Cartesian convention for convex mirrors.

ANSWER: +50 cm
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A student has two convex mirrors: Mirror X with focal length +15 cm and Mirror Y with focal length +30 cm. Which mirror will give a wider field of view? If the radius of curvature of Mirror X is R_X, what is the radius of curvature of Mirror Y in terms of R_X?

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given: f_X = +15 cm, f_Y = +30 cm. Both positive (convex mirrors).
- Step 2: Recall: For convex mirrors, shorter focal length (more curved) → wider field of view. Compare f_X and f_Y: 15 cm < 30 cm, so Mirror X has shorter focal length.
- Step 3: Therefore, Mirror X gives a wider field of view.
- Step 4: Use f = R/2 to relate radii. For Mirror X: f_X = R_X / 2 → R_X = 2 f_X.
- Step 5: For Mirror Y: f_Y = R_Y / 2 → R_Y = 2 f_Y = 2 × (+30 cm) = +60 cm.
- Step 6: Express R_Y in terms of R_X: Since f_Y = 2 f_X (30 cm = 2 × 15 cm), then R_Y = 2 f_Y = 2 × (2 f_X) = 4 f_X = 2 R_X. So R_Y = 2 R_X.

REASONING: Field of view is inversely related to focal length magnitude for convex mirrors. The algebraic relation R = 2f allows expressing one radius in terms of the other. This combines the geometric relationship with a practical consequence (field of view).

ANSWER: Mirror X gives a wider field of view. Radius of curvature of Mirror Y is 2 R_X (or +60 cm if R_X = +30 cm).
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A convex mirror forms a virtual focus. Explain why you cannot project the principal focus of a convex mirror onto a screen placed in front of the mirror, but you can locate it by extending reflected rays backward. Use the concept of divergence of light rays.

COMPETENCY: Interpreting physical situations using ray behaviour

STEPS:
- Step 1: Recognise that a convex mirror causes parallel incident rays to diverge after reflection.
- Step 2: Diverging rays never actually meet in front of the mirror; they spread apart.
- Step 3: A screen captures light only where rays actually converge (real focus). Since rays diverge, no real focus forms in front.
- Step 4: By extending the reflected rays backward (behind the mirror), they appear to meet at a point — this virtual point is the principal focus.

ANSWER: The convex mirror diverges parallel rays; they do not actually meet in front of the mirror, so no real image/focus can be caught on a screen there. The focus is virtual, located behind the mirror where the extensions of the diverging rays intersect.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A car's side-view mirror (convex) has a radius of curvature of 2.4 m. A truck is approaching from behind. The driver notices that the image of the truck in the mirror appears smaller than in a plane mirror. Explain how the focal length of the convex mirror relates to this diminished image size and wider field of view. Calculate the focal length.

COMPETENCY: Predicting outcomes from changing variables and unit analysis

STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate focal length: f = R/2 = 2.4 m / 2 = 1.2 m (positive).
- Step 2: Relate focal length to curvature: Smaller f (more curved) → stronger divergence → smaller virtual image and wider field of view.
- Step 3: Here f = 1.2 m is relatively large (less curved) for a side-view mirror? Actually typical side-view mirrors have f around 0.3-0.5 m. But the principle holds: convex mirror always gives diminished image and wider field compared to plane mirror (which has f = infinity).
- Step 4: The diminished size allows more of the scene to fit in the mirror (wider field of view). The focal length quantifies the curvature: shorter f would give even wider view but smaller images.
- Step 5: Conclude: The positive focal length of 1.2 m indicates a virtual focus behind the mirror; the divergence caused by this curvature produces a diminished, upright virtual image, enabling the driver to see a wider traffic scene.

ANSWER: Focal length = +1.2 m. The convex mirror's curvature (finite positive f) causes light rays to diverge, forming a diminished virtual image. This diminution allows a wider angular field of view compared to a plane mirror (infinite f), helping the driver see more traffic.

DIFFICULTY: hard
