## ELI5
Squaring and square-rooting are mirror twins: multiply a number by itself and you square it; ask 'which number, times itself, gives this?' and you have taken the square root. Perfect squares are the tidy cases — numbers built by self-multiplication, so their roots come out whole, like unwrapping a parcel that folds back flat. Prime factorisation is the trusty method for big ones: break the number into prime pieces, pair the identical pieces, and one of each pair multiplies back to the root.

## DEFINITION
The square root of a perfect square number is the positive number which, when multiplied by itself, gives the original number. For a perfect square \( n = m^2 \) where \( m \) is a natural number, the square root is denoted by \( \sqrt{n} = m \).

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you have a square garden with an area of 225 square metres. You want to put a fence around it, so you need to know the length of one side. Since area of a square = side × side, you are looking for a number that when multiplied by itself gives 225. This is exactly what a square root does – it reverses the squaring operation. We already know from earlier nodes that 225 is a perfect square (15²). To find the square root systematically for larger numbers like 1764, we use prime factorisation: break the number into prime factors, pair them up, and take one from each pair. This connects directly to the prime factorisation skills learned in Class 6 (c6_math_ch01_015) and the perfect square properties from c8_math_ch1_2.

Takeaway: the square root of a perfect square is the positive number whose self-multiplication rebuilds it — find it by prime factorisation: factor completely, pair identical primes, multiply one from each pair; always verify by squaring the answer. Exam format: expect perfect squares up to five digits and the 'why is a non-perfect square's root not whole?' reasoning — an unpaired prime factor is the proof. Verification beat: square your root; you must land exactly on the original number. Why students miss it: they pair factors carelessly or leave a pair unaccounted — every prime must sit in a pair for the root to come out whole.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the square root of 144? Write the answer and explain what the symbol \( \sqrt{144} \) means.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The square root of 144 is 12 because \( 12 \times 12 = 144 \). The symbol \( \sqrt{144} \) means "the positive number which when squared gives 144". A wrong answer like -12 reveals the misconception that square root gives both positive and negative values; at this level, \( \sqrt{} \) denotes only the principal (positive) square root.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
A student says \( \sqrt{100} = 50 \) because "you divide by 2 to find the square root". Why is this reasoning incorrect? Use the definition of square root to explain.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The reasoning is incorrect because square root is the inverse of squaring, not dividing by 2. \( \sqrt{100} \) asks: what number multiplied by itself gives 100? \( 50 \times 50 = 2500 \), not 100. The correct answer is 10 because \( 10 \times 10 = 100 \). A student who agrees with the wrong reasoning confuses "half of a number" with "a number that squares to the original", showing they don't understand the inverse relationship between squaring and square root.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Find the square root of 1764 using the prime factorisation method. Show all steps.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Prime factorise 1764. \( 1764 = 2 \times 882 = 2 \times 2 \times 441 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 147 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 3 \times 49 = 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 7^2 \).
Step 2: Pair the prime factors: \( (2 \times 2) \times (3 \times 3) \times (7 \times 7) \).
Step 3: Take one factor from each pair: \( 2 \times 3 \times 7 = 42 \).
Step 4: Verify: \( 42^2 = 1764 \). So \( \sqrt{1764} = 42 \).
A wrong answer like 21 (taking only one factor from each pair but missing a pair) or 84 (not pairing correctly) reveals errors in pairing or in prime factorisation itself.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the square roots of 256 and 625. Without calculating the exact square roots, how can you tell which one is larger? Explain using the properties of perfect squares.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Since both are perfect squares and the square root function is increasing for positive numbers, the larger perfect square has the larger square root. 625 > 256, so \( \sqrt{625} > \sqrt{256} \). Alternatively, note the number of digits: 256 is a 3-digit perfect square, its square root is 2-digit (since \( 10^2=100, 31^2=961 \)). 625 is also 3-digit, but closer to 900 (\( 30^2 \)) so its root is larger. A student who cannot compare without calculating lacks understanding of the monotonic nature of the squaring function.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student solved \( \sqrt{441} \) as follows: \( 441 = 3 \times 147 = 3 \times 3 \times 49 = 3^2 \times 7^2 \). Then they wrote \( \sqrt{441} = 3 \times 7 = 21 \). Is this solution correct? If not, identify the error.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The solution is correct in result (21) but the factorisation shown is incomplete: \( 441 = 3 \times 147 = 3 \times 3 \times 49 = 3^2 \times 7^2 \) is correct. However, the student missed showing that 49 = 7 × 7 explicitly, but the final pairing is correct. If a student says it's incorrect because "49 is not prime", they misunderstand that factorisation can stop at perfect square factors. The error would be if they wrote \( 441 = 3 \times 147 = 3 \times 3 \times 49 = 3^2 \times 7 \) (missing the second 7). A wrong judgment here reveals the student thinks every step must break down to primes immediately, not recognizing that 49 is a perfect square.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think the square root of a number is obtained by dividing the number by 2 (e.g., \( \sqrt{64} = 32 \)) or by halving the number of zeros (e.g., \( \sqrt{10000} = 100 \) is correct but for wrong reason). The exact wrong step: they confuse the inverse operation of squaring (which is square root) with the inverse of multiplying by 2 (which is dividing by 2). For example, they see \( 8^2 = 64 \) and think \( \sqrt{64} = 64 \div 2 = 32 \), not checking that \( 32 \times 32 \neq 64 \).
## ON_FAILURE
If a student makes this error, immediately ask: "If \( \sqrt{64} = 32 \), then what is \( 32 \times 32 \)?" Let them compute 1024. Then ask: "Does that equal 64?" Then remind: "Square root asks: what number times itself gives 64? We know \( 8 \times 8 = 64 \), so \( \sqrt{64} = 8 \)." Next, give a set of 5 perfect squares (36, 81, 100, 144, 225) and have them find roots by asking "what number squared gives this?" and verify by multiplication. This builds the inverse-operation concept concretely.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A square tile has an area of 1296 square centimetres. What is the length of one side of the tile?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify that the side length of a square is the square root of its area. We need to find \( \sqrt{1296} \).
- Step 2: Use prime factorisation method. Divide 1296 by smallest primes: \( 1296 \div 2 = 648 \), \( 648 \div 2 = 324 \), \( 324 \div 2 = 162 \), \( 162 \div 2 = 81 \), \( 81 \div 3 = 27 \), \( 27 \div 3 = 9 \), \( 9 \div 3 = 3 \), \( 3 \div 3 = 1 \).
- Step 3: Write in exponent form: \( 1296 = 2^4 \times 3^4 \). Group into pairs: \( (2^2)^2 \times (3^2)^2 = (2^2 \times 3^2)^2 = (4 \times 9)^2 = 36^2 \).
- Step 4: Take one factor from each pair: \( 2^2 \times 3^2 = 4 \times 9 = 36 \). So \( \sqrt{1296} = 36 \).
- Step 5: Verify: \( 36 \times 36 = 1296 \). The side length is 36 cm.
REASONING: The area of a square is side × side, so side = √area. Prime factorisation breaks the number into prime factors; since it's a perfect square, every prime appears an even number of times. Pairing them and taking one from each pair reconstructs the number that was squared.
ANSWER: 36 cm
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Find the smallest number by which 1800 must be multiplied to make it a perfect square. Then find the square root of the resulting perfect square.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Prime factorise 1800: \( 1800 = 18 \times 100 = 2 \times 3^2 \times 2^2 \times 5^2 = 2^3 \times 3^2 \times 5^2 \).
- Step 2: For a perfect square, all prime exponents must be even. Here, 2 has exponent 3 (odd). We need one more factor of 2 to make it \( 2^4 \).
- Step 3: The smallest multiplier is 2. New number = \( 1800 \times 2 = 3600 \).
- Step 4: Factorise 3600: \( 3600 = 2^4 \times 3^2 \times 5^2 \). Pair them: \( (2^2)^2 \times (3^1)^2 \times (5^1)^2 \).
- Step 5: Take one from each pair: \( 2^2 \times 3 \times 5 = 4 \times 3 \times 5 = 60 \). So \( \sqrt{3600} = 60 \).
- Step 6: Verify: \( 60^2 = 3600 \). The multiplier is 2, square root is 60.
REASONING: This combines perfect square properties (even exponents) with square root finding. The multiplier supplies the missing factors to complete pairs. Then the square root is found by taking one from each completed pair.
ANSWER: Multiply by 2; square root = 60
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A school has 2025 students. They want to arrange them in a square formation (equal rows and columns) for a drill. How many students will be in each row? If 4 more students join, can they still form a perfect square? Explain.
STEPS:
- Step 1: The number of students per row is the square root of total students if it's a perfect square. Find \( \sqrt{2025} \).
- Step 2: Prime factorise 2025: ends with 25, divisible by 25. \( 2025 \div 25 = 81 \). So \( 2025 = 25 \times 81 = 5^2 \times 9^2 = 5^2 \times (3^2)^2 = 5^2 \times 3^4 \).
- Step 3: Pair factors: \( (5 \times 3^2)^2 = (5 \times 9)^2 = 45^2 \). So \( \sqrt{2025} = 45 \). Each row has 45 students.
- Step 4: With 4 more students, total = 2029. Check if 2029 is a perfect square. Perfect squares end in 0,1,4,5,6,9. 2029 ends in 9, so possible.
- Step 5: Check between which squares: \( 45^2 = 2025 \), \( 46^2 = 2116 \). 2029 is between them, not a perfect square. Or note \( 45^2 = 2025 \), next square is \( 46^2 = 2025 + 45 + 46 = 2116 \) (using \( (n+1)^2 = n^2 + n + (n+1) \)). 2029 ≠ 2116.
- Step 6: Conclusion: 45 students per row originally. With 4 more, they cannot form a perfect square.
REASONING: This applies square root to a real arrangement problem and uses the property that perfect squares are not consecutive integers (except 0 and 1). The step using \( (n+1)^2 = n^2 + 2n + 1 \) connects to algebraic patterns from Class 7.
ANSWER: 45 students per row; with 4 more, cannot form a perfect square.

QUESTION: A square field has an area of 9216 m². A farmer wants to divide it into 4 equal square plots. What will be the side length of each small square plot? Explain your reasoning.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions
STEPS:

- Step 2: Prime factorise 9216: \( 9216 = 96^2 \) (since \( 100^2=10000 \), \( 96^2 = (100-4)^2 = 10000 - 800 + 16 = 9216 \)). Or by factorisation: \( 9216 = 2^{10} \times 3^2 \). Pairs: \( (2^5 \times 3)^2 = (32 \times 3)^2 = 96^2 \). So side = 96 m.

- Step 4: Side of small square = \( \sqrt{2304} \). Since \( 2304 = 9216 \div 4 = (96^2) \div (2^2) = (96 \div 2)^2 = 48^2 \). So side = 48 m.
- Step 5: Verify: 4 small squares of side 48 m have total area \( 4 \times 48^2 = 4 \times 2304 = 9216 \) m².
ANSWER: Each small square plot has side length 48 metres.
