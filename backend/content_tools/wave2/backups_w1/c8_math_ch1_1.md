## ELI5
A square number is a number multiplied by itself — four by four gives sixteen, so sixteen is four's square. Why 'square'? Picture tiles: a square with side four is tiled by sixteen little unit squares, and that tiling is the multiplication sitting in a shape. Every natural number owns one; the tidy ones that are themselves squares of whole numbers — one, four, nine, sixteen — are called perfect squares. Squaring is just the special multiplication where both factors agree to be the same.

## DEFINITION
A square number is the product obtained when a natural number is multiplied by itself. For any natural number \( n \), its square is denoted by \( n^2 \) and calculated as \( n \times n \). The result is called a perfect square if it can be expressed as the square of a natural number.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can correctly identify square numbers, write the notation \( n^2 \) for any given natural number \( n \), compute squares of numbers up to 20, and explain why the product of a number with itself is called its square using the area model of a square.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the definition of square numbers, the notation \( n^2 \), and computing squares of natural numbers up to 20. It does NOT cover properties of perfect squares, patterns in units digits, square roots, or cubes — those are addressed in subsequent nodes.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are tiling a square floor in your classroom using 1 m × 1 m tiles. If the floor is 4 m on each side, how many tiles do you need? You multiply 4 by 4 to get 16 tiles. This is exactly what a square number represents: the total number of unit squares that fit inside a larger square. We write this as \( 4^2 = 4 \times 4 = 16 \). The exponent 2 tells us the number is multiplied by itself once. Connect this to the multiplication you already know from Class 6 and 7 — squaring is just a special case of multiplication where both factors are the same. The key insight is that \( n^2 \) always gives the area of a square with side length \( n \) units.

Takeaway: n² = n × n, and the name is literal — n² counts the unit squares tiling a square of side n. Exam format: expect 'compute squares up to twenty' and 'explain the area meaning'; a quick tile sketch justifies the notation better than words. Verification beat: sanity-check by pattern — squares end only in certain digits and grow by successive odd numbers, so an answer violating either is suspect. Why students miss it: they treat n² as n × 2 — doubling instead of squaring; the area picture (a square of side n) makes the difference visible instantly.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Write the square of 7 using the correct notation and calculate its value.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The square of 7 is written as \( 7^2 \) and its value is \( 7 \times 7 = 49 \).  
A wrong answer like \( 7 \times 2 = 14 \) or \( 2^7 = 128 \) reveals the student confuses the exponent 2 with multiplication by 2 or with the base and exponent swapped. They have not grasped that the superscript 2 means "multiply the base by itself once."
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why \( 5^2 \) is not the same as \( 2 \times 5 \). Use the area of a square in your explanation.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
\( 5^2 \) means \( 5 \times 5 = 25 \), which is the area of a square of side 5 units. \( 2 \times 5 = 10 \) is the area of a rectangle 2 by 5, not a square. The exponent 2 indicates two equal factors, not multiplication by 2.  
A student who says "both give 10" or "the 2 just means multiply by 2" does not understand the geometric meaning of squaring and treats the exponent as a factor.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Calculate \( 12^2 \) and \( 15^2 \) by showing the multiplication step by step.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
\( 12^2 = 12 \times 12 \):  
 \( 12 \times 10 = 120 \)  
 \( 12 \times 2 = 24 \)  
 \( 120 + 24 = 144 \)  
\( 15^2 = 15 \times 15 \):  
 \( 15 \times 10 = 150 \)  
 \( 15 \times 5 = 75 \)  
 \( 150 + 75 = 225 \)  
A student who writes \( 12^2 = 24 \) or \( 15^2 = 30 \) is multiplying by 2 instead of by itself. A student who gets \( 12 \times 12 = 134 \) likely made an error in partial products or addition.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Observe the sequence: \( 1^2 = 1 \), \( 2^2 = 4 \), \( 3^2 = 9 \), \( 4^2 = 16 \), \( 5^2 = 25 \). What is the difference between consecutive squares? Describe the pattern and predict the difference between \( 10^2 \) and \( 9^2 \) without calculating the squares.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The differences are: \( 4-1=3 \), \( 9-4=5 \), \( 16-9=7 \), \( 25-16=9 \). The differences are consecutive odd numbers: 3, 5, 7, 9. The difference between \( n^2 \) and \( (n-1)^2 \) is \( 2n-1 \). So between \( 10^2 \) and \( 9^2 \), the difference is \( 2 \times 10 - 1 = 19 \).  
A student who says the difference is constant or gives an even number does not recognise the pattern of odd numbers, which is foundational for later nodes on sum of odd numbers.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Ravi says \( (-6)^2 = -36 \) because "a negative times a positive is negative". Is he correct? Justify your answer.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Ravi is incorrect. \( (-6)^2 = (-6) \times (-6) = 36 \). The product of two negative integers is positive (Class 7 integer multiplication rule). He mistakenly treated \( (-6)^2 \) as \( - (6 \times 6) \) instead of \( (-6) \times (-6) \).  
A student who agrees with Ravi has not connected integer sign rules to squaring. They may also confuse \( (-6)^2 \) with \( -6^2 \) (which would be -36), but the parentheses make the base -6.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a real-life problem (using rupees, metres, or kilograms) that requires finding the square of a number between 10 and 20. Write the problem, the mathematical expression, and the solution.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Example: "A square garden has a side length of 13 metres. What is its area?"  
Expression: \( 13^2 = 13 \times 13 \)  
Solution: \( 13 \times 10 = 130 \), \( 13 \times 3 = 39 \), \( 130 + 39 = 169 \) m².  
A poor answer uses addition instead of multiplication, chooses a non-square context (e.g., rectangle), or cannot set up the expression \( n^2 \). This reveals inability to connect the notation to a meaningful situation.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often interpret \( n^2 \) as \( n \times 2 \) (e.g., \( 8^2 = 16 \)) because they see the "2" and think "multiply by 2". Another error is writing \( -5^2 = 25 \) instead of \( -25 \), confusing \( (-5)^2 \) with \( -5^2 \). In \( -5^2 \), the exponent applies only to 5, so it is \( -(5 \times 5) = -25 \), whereas \( (-5)^2 = (-5) \times (-5) = 25 \). This confusion between the base being -5 versus 5 with a negative sign outside is very common.
## ON_FAILURE
If a student writes \( 8^2 = 16 \), ask: "What does the little 2 mean? Let's read it: '8 squared' means 8 multiplied by itself. Show me 8 times 8 using repeated addition or the area model." Draw a 8×8 grid and count. For the negative sign error, write both \( (-5)^2 \) and \( -5^2 \) side by side. Ask: "In the first, what is the base? In the second, what is the base?" Emphasise parentheses change the base. Practice 5 examples of each type until they distinguish them fluently.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A square classroom has a side length of 12 metres. The school wants to cover the floor with 1 m × 1 m tiles. How many tiles are needed? Write the mathematical expression using square notation and solve.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the side length of the square floor: 12 m.
- Step 2: Recall that the area of a square = side × side, which is written as \( (\text{side})^2 \). So the expression is \( 12^2 \).
- Step 3: Compute \( 12^2 = 12 \times 12 \). Break 12 into 10 + 2: \( 12 \times 12 = 12 \times (10 + 2) = (12 \times 10) + (12 \times 2) \).
- Step 4: Calculate: \( 12 \times 10 = 120 \); \( 12 \times 2 = 24 \); \( 120 + 24 = 144 \).
- Step 5: Interpret the result: 144 tiles are needed. The unit is square metres (m²) because we multiplied metres by metres.
REASONING: The area model connects the geometric meaning of a square to the arithmetic operation of squaring. Using the distributive property (splitting 12 into 10 and 2) makes the multiplication manageable and reinforces a key algebraic skill.
ANSWER: \( 12^2 = 144 \) tiles (or 144 m²)
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Evaluate \( (-9)^2 \) and \( -9^2 \). Explain why the answers are different.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the base in each expression. In \( (-9)^2 \), the base is -9 (including the negative sign) because of the parentheses. In \( -9^2 \), the base is 9, and the negative sign is applied after squaring.
- Step 2: Apply the definition: \( (-9)^2 = (-9) \times (-9) \). \( -9^2 = -(9 \times 9) \).
- Step 3: Compute \( (-9) \times (-9) \). Using integer sign rule: negative × negative = positive. \( 9 \times 9 = 81 \), so \( (-9)^2 = 81 \).
- Step 4: Compute \( -(9 \times 9) = -81 \).
- Step 5: Compare: \( (-9)^2 = 81 \), \( -9^2 = -81 \). The parentheses change the base from 9 to -9, which changes the sign of the result.
REASONING: This example directly addresses the common misconception about parentheses. It forces the student to identify the base correctly before applying the squaring operation, linking back to Class 7 integer multiplication rules.
ANSWER: \( (-9)^2 = 81 \); \( -9^2 = -81 \)
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A farmer has a square field of side 15 m. He wants to increase each side by 5 m to make a larger square field. By how much does the area increase? Express the increase using square notation and compute.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Original side = 15 m. Original area = \( 15^2 \).
- Step 2: New side = 15 + 5 = 20 m. New area = \( 20^2 \).
- Step 3: Increase in area = \( 20^2 - 15^2 \).
- Step 4: Compute \( 20^2 = 20 \times 20 = 400 \). Compute \( 15^2 = 15 \times 15 = 225 \) (using \( 15 \times 10 = 150 \), \( 15 \times 5 = 75 \), sum = 225).
- Step 5: Subtract: \( 400 - 225 = 175 \).
- Step 6: The area increases by 175 m².
REASONING: This problem combines squaring with a real-world change scenario. It requires computing two different squares and subtracting, reinforcing that \( n^2 \) represents area. The step of finding the new side length first connects to algebraic thinking (expressing new side as \( n+5 \)).
ANSWER: The area increases by 175 m².
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Priya says, "If I double a number, its square also doubles." For example, 3 doubled is 6. \( 3^2 = 9 \) and \( 6^2 = 36 \). She claims 36 is double of 9? Is she correct? Test her claim with another number and explain the actual relationship between \( n^2 \) and \( (2n)^2 \).
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying mathematical statements using algebraic reasoning.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Check Priya's example: \( 3^2 = 9 \), \( 6^2 = 36 \). Is 36 double of 9? No, \( 2 \times 9 = 18 \neq 36 \).
- Step 2: Test with another number, say 4. \( 4^2 = 16 \). Double of 4 is 8. \( 8^2 = 64 \). Is 64 double of 16? No, \( 2 \times 16 = 32 \neq 64 \).
- Step 3: Find the relationship: \( (2n)^2 = (2n) \times (2n) = 4 \times (n \times n) = 4n^2 \). So the square becomes 4 times, not 2 times.
- Step 4: Conclude: Priya is incorrect. Doubling the number quadruples its square.
ANSWER: Priya is incorrect. \( (2n)^2 = 4n^2 \), so the square becomes four times the original, not double.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A square park of side 18 m has a 2 m wide path running along its inside perimeter. Find the area of the path. (Hint: Find area of outer square and inner square, then subtract.)
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions and multi-step problem solving.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Outer square side = 18 m. Outer area = \( 18^2 \).
- Step 2: Path is 2 m wide inside, so inner square side = 18 - 2 - 2 = 14 m (path on both sides). Inner area = \( 14^2 \).
- Step 3: Area of path = Outer area - Inner area = \( 18^2 - 14^2 \).
- Step 4: Compute \( 18^2 = 18 \times 18 = 324 \) (18×10=180, 18×8=144, 180+144=324). Compute \( 14^2 = 14 \times 14 = 196 \) (14×10=140, 14×4=56, 140+56=196).
- Step 5: Subtract: 324 - 196 = 128.
- Step 6: The area of the path is 128 m².
ANSWER: 128 m²
DIFFICULTY: hard
