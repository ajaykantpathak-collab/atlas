## ELI5
A ruler's centimetre is sliced into 10 tiny tick marks called millimetres - and each tick is a tenth: one part out of ten equal parts. Written as a fraction it is one over ten; written as a decimal it is 0.1, the first seat right of the decimal point. Every step right of the point makes a digit ten times smaller, the mirror of the ladder you climb leftwards. So a pencil's price and a tick's length both live comfortably in tenths.

## DEFINITION
The tenth part represents one of ten equal parts of a whole unit, expressed as the fraction $\frac{1}{10}$ or as the decimal number $0.1$. It is the first position to the right of the decimal point, representing a value ten times smaller than a whole number one.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
Can convert a fraction with a denominator of 10 into its decimal form ($0.1$ to $0.9$), represent tenths using base-ten place value grids or number lines, and explain that moving one place to the right of the decimal point means dividing by 10.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers the understanding, representation, and conversion of tenths ($\frac{1}{10}$ and decimals up to $0.9$) and their place value meaning. It does NOT cover hundredths, thousandths, or operations on decimals yet.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you go to a stationery shop to buy a pencil, and the shopkeeper tells you it costs one rupee, but you also notice small rulers where a centimetre is divided into 10 tiny equal tick marks called millimetres. How do we write a length that is just 1 tiny tick mark out of those 10 equal parts of a centimetre? Just like we use fractions like $\frac{1}{10}$ to show sharing a whole into 10 equal pieces, mathematicians invented decimals to write these parts without fraction bars by using a dot called a decimal point. To begin, think of a long strip of paper divided into 10 equal segments; shading 1 segment gives us $\frac{1}{10}$ of the whole strip. Connecting this to what we learned in fractions, any whole divided into 10 equal parts gives tenths. The key insight is that the first position immediately to the right of the decimal point is the "tenths" place, where $0.1$ is exactly the same value as $\frac{1}{10}$.

**Takeaway:** a tenth is one part of ten equal parts: the fraction one-tenth, the decimal 0.1, the first seat right of the point. **In the exam:** convert tenths by matching the denominator of ten; on grids and rulers, count equal parts and shade or tick. **Check yourself:** cut a ten-part strip, take a few parts, and say the fraction and the decimal for what you hold. **Why students miss it:** they read 0.3 as 'three' or as one-third, missing the parts-out-of-ten meaning entirely.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What fraction is represented by the decimal number $0.3$?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The correct answer is $\frac{3}{10}$. A student who answers $3$ or $\frac{1}{3}$ does not understand that the first digit after the decimal point represents tenths (parts out of 10), confusing the place value position with a whole number or a numerator-denominator relationship.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why does shifting a digit one place to the right of the decimal point make its value 10 times smaller instead of 10 times larger?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The correct answer is that each place value position to the right is obtained by dividing the previous place value by 10 (since a whole is divided into 10 parts to make tenths). A student who says it gets larger is confusing the whole number place value system (where moving left increases value) with decimal fractions, failing to recognize that moving right means splitting into smaller fractional parts.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A ribbon is 1 metre long and is divided into 10 equal parts. If Riya cuts off 7 of those parts, what decimal fraction of a metre does she have?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The correct answer is $0.7$ metres. 
Steps:
1. Identify total equal parts: 10 parts make 1 whole metre.
2. Identify parts taken: Riya takes 7 parts, which as a fraction is $\frac{7}{10}$.
3. Convert the fraction to a decimal: $\frac{7}{10}$ written in decimal form is $0.7$.
4. Verify with units: $0.7$ metres means 7 tenths of a metre.
A student who answers $7.0$ or $0.07$ has misplaced the decimal point or confused tenths with units or hundredths.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the fraction $\frac{4}{10}$ and the decimal $0.4$. Are they equal, and how do you know based on their place value meaning?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
They are equal. $\frac{4}{10}$ means 4 parts out of 10 equal divisions of a whole, and $0.4$ places the digit 4 in the first position to the right of the decimal point, which is explicitly named the "tenths" place. A student who thinks they are different does not realize that decimals are simply an alternative notation for fractions with denominators that are powers of 10.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student writes that $1.5$ metres is equal to 1 metre and 5 centimeters because there are 5 digits. Is this reasoning correct? Explain why or why not.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The reasoning is incorrect. $1.5$ metres means 1 whole metre and $5$ tenths of a metre ($\frac{5}{10}$ of a metre), which is equal to $50$ centimetres, not $5$ centimetres. A student who agrees with the statement is confusing the tenths place ($0.1$ or $\frac{1}{10}$) with the hundredths place or direct unit conversion units like centimetres ($\frac{1}{100}$ of a metre).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a real-life scenario using Indian currency (Rupees and 10-paisa coins) to explain why the number $0.6$ represents six parts out of ten.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good answer should state that 1 Rupee can be thought of as divided into 10 equal 10-paisa coins. Taking 6 of those 10-paisa coins represents $\frac{6}{10}$ of a Rupee, which is written as $0.6$ rupees. A poor answer fails to connect the denominator of 10 to a physical grouping of 10 equal items in real life.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often write the fraction $\frac{1}{10}$ as decimal $0.01$ instead of $0.1$. This happens because they confuse the tenths place (the first position after the decimal point) with the hundredths place, thinking that the number of zeros in the denominator ($10$ has one zero) does not correspond directly to the decimal place value structure.
## ON_FAILURE
Remind the student to count the number of zeros in the denominator of the fraction $\frac{1}{10}$. Show them a place value chart where the column immediately to the right of the decimal point is labelled "Tenths" and has a value of $\frac{1}{10}$ or $0.1$, while the next column to the right is "Hundredths" ($\frac{1}{100}$ or $0.01$). Have them practice writing fractions like $\frac{1}{10}, \frac{2}{10}, \frac{9}{10}$ directly as $0.1, 0.2, 0.9$.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Amit measures a wooden stick and finds it is 4 centimetres and 3 millimetres long. If 1 millimetre is $\frac{1}{10}$ of a centimetre, express the total length of the stick in centimetres using a decimal number.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the whole centimetres and the fractional part. Whole part = $4\text{ cm}$, fractional part = $3\text{ mm} = \frac{3}{10}\text{ cm}$.
- step 2: Write the combined measurement as a mixed number: $4\frac{3}{10}\text{ cm}$.
- step 3: Convert the fraction part $\frac{3}{10}$ into its decimal equivalent using place value: $\frac{3}{10} = 0.3$.
- step 4: Combine the whole number and the decimal part: $4 + 0.3 = 4.3$.
- step 5: Verify the result with proper units: The total length is $4.3\text{ cm}$, where 4 is in the units place and 3 is in the tenths place.
REASONING: Since 1 millimetre is one-tenth of a centimetre, 3 millimetres represent 3 parts out of 10, which translates to $0.3$ when placed in the tenths column to the right of the decimal point.
ANSWER: $4.3\text{ cm}$
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A water bottle contains 1 litre of water. Priya pours out some water until exactly $\frac{8}{10}$ of a litre remains in the bottle. Express this remaining quantity as a decimal and explain its place value meaning.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the given fraction of the total volume: $\frac{8}{10}\text{ litres}$.
- step 2: Recognize that the denominator is 10, which means the whole litre is divided into 10 equal parts and we have 8 of those parts.
- step 3: Apply the decimal conversion rule for tenths: $\frac{8}{10}$ is written as $0.8$.
- step 4: Interpret the place value: In $0.8$, the digit 8 is in the tenths place, indicating 8 tenths of a litre.
- step 5: Verify the expression with units: The remaining volume is $0.8\text{ litres}$.
REASONING: Any fraction with 10 in the denominator can be written directly as a decimal by placing the numerator in the first position to the right of the decimal point.
ANSWER: $0.8\text{ litres}$
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Rahul has a ribbon of length $2$ metres. He cuts off a piece measuring $1\frac{6}{10}$ metres. Express the length of the remaining ribbon as a decimal number of metres.
STEPS:
- step 1: Write down the initial length and the cut length: Initial length = $2\text{ m}$, cut length = $1\frac{6}{10}\text{ m}$.
- step 2: Convert the mixed fraction $1\frac{6}{10}$ into decimal form: $1\frac{6}{10} = 1 + \frac{6}{10} = 1 + 0.6 = 1.6\text{ m}$.
- step 3: Set up the subtraction expression using decimals: Remaining length = $2.0 - 1.6$.
- step 4: Perform the subtraction by aligning decimal points: $2.0 - 1.6 = 0.4$.
- step 5: Verify with units and context: The remaining ribbon length is $0.4\text{ metres}$, which is equivalent to $\frac{4}{10}\text{ m}$ or $40\text{ cm}$.
REASONING: By converting the mixed fraction into decimal form first, we align the whole numbers and tenths columns correctly, allowing us to find the difference efficiently.
ANSWER: $0.4\text{ metres}$
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1

COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying fractions and decimals using place value
STEPS:
- step 1: Convert both fractions and decimals into a common format (all as decimals or all as tenths).
- step 2: Evaluate Rahul's first claim by comparing $0.2$ and $\frac{3}{10}$ (which is $0.3$).
- step 3: Evaluate Rahul's second claim by comparing $0.2$ and $\frac{1}{10}$ (which is $0.1$).
- step 4: State whether Rahul's claims are correct and provide mathematical justification.
ANSWER: Rahul's first claim ($0.2 < \frac{3}{10}$) is correct because $0.2$ is equal to $\frac{2}{10}$, and 2 tenths is less than 3 tenths. His second claim ($0.2 > \frac{1}{10}$) is also correct because 2 tenths is greater than 1 tenth. Both claims are valid when comparing values through their tenths place representation.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A running track is 1 kilometre long. Neha ran $\frac{5}{10}$ of the track in the morning and another $\frac{3}{10}$ of the track in the evening. Write the total distance she ran as a decimal fraction, and explain how many tenths of a kilometre she has left to complete a full 1-kilometre run.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions with tenths and decimals
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the distances run in fraction form: morning = $\frac{5}{10}\text{ km}$, evening = $\frac{3}{10}\text{ km}$.
- step 2: Add the fractions together: $\frac{5}{10} + \frac{3}{10} = \frac{8}{10}\text{ km}$.
- step 3: Convert the total distance run into decimal form: $\frac{8}{10} = $ $0.8\text{ km}$.
- step 4: Subtract the distance run from the whole track (1 km or $\frac{10}{10}$) to find what is left: $\frac{10}{10} - \frac{8}{10} = \frac{2}{10}\text{ km} = 0.2\text{ km}$.
ANSWER: Neha ran a total of $0.8\text{ km}$ ($8$ tenths of a kilometre). She has $0.2\text{ km}$ ($2$ tenths of a kilometre) left to complete the full 1-kilometre run.
DIFFICULTY: hard
