## ELI5
Five and a half litres of paint, containers holding three-quarters of a litre: 'how many containers?' is division - total divided by the size of each part. Flip the divisor, multiply, and read the answer with its units and common sense. Division by a fraction smaller than one inflates the answer - more containers than litres - which surprises nobody who has filled tiny cups from a big bucket. The recurring trap is multiplying when the question says sharing; 'how many parts' always means divide.

## DEFINITION
Word problems involving fraction division require translating real-world scenarios of partitioning or sharing quantities into division expressions with fractions, and solving them using the reciprocal method. These problems typically ask how many portions of a given fractional size can be made from a total quantity, or what the whole quantity is given a fractional part.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
Can read a multi-sentence word problem, correctly identify the total amount and the dividing amount or fraction, set up a fraction division expression, multiply by the reciprocal, and interpret the final answer with appropriate real-world units.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers single and multi-step word problems involving division of proper, improper, and mixed fractions by whole numbers, fractions, or mixed numbers within real-life contexts like length, weight, capacity, and time. Does not cover complex algebraic fractions or advanced calculus applications.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you bought $5\frac{1}{2}$ litres of paint to paint your school clubhouse, and each small container holds $\frac{3}{4}$ of a litre. How many containers can you fill? To begin, you must read carefully to identify the total quantity available and the size of each part or group being created. Next, translate this grouping scenario into a mathematical division statement: total quantity divided by the size of one part. Connect this to your prior understanding of dividing one fraction by another by rewriting the division problem as multiplication by the reciprocal of the divisor. Finally, compute the product, simplify the resulting fraction, and check your answer against the real-world context to see if it makes sense.

**Takeaway:** sharing word problems are division: total divided by portion size, solved by the reciprocal method. **In the exam:** identify total and portion, write the division, flip the divisor, and interpret the quotient with units. **Check yourself:** compute the paint-container count and round your interpretation the way a painter would. **Why students miss it:** they multiply total by portion when the question asks how many portions fit. Round the quotient the way the story demands, and state the leftover litres in one closing clause. Say the question aloud - how many containers - and the division sign picks itself.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
If a tailor has a ribbon of length $3\frac{1}{2}$ metres and cuts it into pieces that are each $\frac{1}{2}$ metre long, what mathematical operation should be used to find the total number of pieces?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Division ($3\frac{1}{2} \div \frac{1}{2}$). A wrong answer such as multiplication or subtraction reveals that the student views fractions in word problems solely as keywords rather than visualizing partitioning a total length into equal-sized segments.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
A recipe calls for $\frac{2}{3}$ cup of sugar per batch. Priya has $4$ cups of sugar. Why is the calculation to find the number of batches $4 \div \frac{2}{3}$ rather than $\frac{2}{3} \div 4$?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The total available resource ($4$ cups) must be divided by the amount consumed per single batch ($\frac{2}{3}$ cup), because we are partitioning the total into groups of $\frac{2}{3}$. Reversing the order shows a fundamental misunderstanding of what the dividend and divisor represent in sharing or grouping situations.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A ribbon of length $7\frac{1}{2}$ metres is cut into pieces of length $1\frac{1}{4}$ metres each. How many pieces are obtained?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
First, convert mixed numbers to improper fractions: $7\frac{1}{2} = \frac{15}{2}$ and $1\frac{1}{4} = \frac{5}{4}$. Next, set up the division: $\frac{15}{2} \div \frac{5}{4}$. Then, multiply by the reciprocal of the divisor: $\frac{15}{2} \times \frac{4}{5}$. Simplify by cross-cancelling: $\frac{15 \div 5}{2 \div 1} \times \frac{4 \div 2}{5 \div 5} = \frac{3}{1} \times \frac{2}{1} = 6$. Answer: $6$ pieces. A wrong answer like $9$ or $\frac{75}{8}$ usually stems from multiplying instead of dividing or failing to invert the second fraction correctly.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the results of dividing a number by a fraction less than $1$ versus dividing the same number by a whole number greater than $1$. What happens to the quotient relative to the original number, and why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Dividing by a fraction less than $1$ results in a quotient larger than the original number because you are asking how many small fractional parts fit into the whole, which yields more parts. Conversely, dividing by a number greater than $1$ splits the quantity into fewer, larger shares, making the quotient smaller. A wrong answer indicates confusion between the effects of fraction multiplication and fraction division.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Rahul solved a word problem where $6$ kg of sweets were shared into bags of $\frac{3}{4}$ kg each. His working was: $6 \times \frac{3}{4} = \frac{18}{4} = 4\frac{1}{2}$ bags. Is Rahul's solution correct? If not, identify his error.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Rahul's solution is incorrect. He multiplied instead of dividing; he should have calculated $6 \div \frac{3}{4} = 6 \times \frac{4}{3} = \frac{24}{3} = 8$ bags. A wrong answer means the student failed to notice that splitting a total into fractional portions requires division, not multiplication.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a real-life word problem about buying fruits or vegetables where the answer requires calculating $5\frac{1}{4} \div \frac{3}{4}$, and state the final answer.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
An acceptable problem: "A fruit vendor has $5\frac{1}{4}$ kg of apples and packs them into small bags weighing $\frac{3}{4}$ kg each. How many bags can he fill?" Solution: $5\frac{1}{4} = \frac{21}{4}$, so $\frac{21}{4} \div \frac{3}{4} = \frac{21}{4} \times \frac{4}{3} = 7$ bags. A poor answer uses incorrect units, improper fraction conversion, or sets up a multiplication instead of a grouping division.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often convert mixed numbers to improper fractions correctly but then forget to invert the divisor when performing division, accidentally multiplying the two fractions straight across as if it were a multiplication word problem. For example, when solving $3\frac{1}{2} \div \frac{1}{4}$, they compute $\frac{7}{2} \times \frac{1}{4} = \frac{7}{8}$ instead of $\frac{7}{2} \times \frac{4}{1} = 14$.
## ON_FAILURE
Remind the student to explicitly circle the word "each" or "per" in the problem to identify the size of the group. Write out the rule: "Keep the first fraction, Change division to multiplication, Flip the second fraction (Reciprocal)." Have them verify their answer by checking if multiplying the quotient by the divisor gives back the original total quantity.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A milkman has $12\frac{1}{2}$ litres of milk. He pours it into small bottles that each hold $\frac{3}{4}$ of a litre. How many full bottles can he fill, and how much milk is left over?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given values. Total milk = $12\frac{1}{2}$ litres; capacity of one bottle = $\frac{3}{4}$ litre.
- Step 2: Convert the mixed number into an improper fraction: $12\frac{1}{2} = \frac{12 \times 2 + 1}{2} = \frac{25}{2}$ litres.
- Step 3: Set up the division expression to find the number of bottles: $\frac{25}{2} \div \frac{3}{4}$.
- Step 4: Apply the reciprocal method by changing division to multiplication and inverting the divisor: $\frac{25}{2} \times \frac{4}{3} = \frac{25 \times 4}{2 \times 3} = \frac{100}{6}$.
- Step 5: Simplify the fraction $\frac{100}{6}$ by dividing numerator and denominator by $2$: $\frac{50}{3} = 16\frac{2}{3}$ bottles. Thus, he can fill $16$ full bottles. To find the leftover milk, multiply $16$ by $\frac{3}{4}$ to get $12$ litres used, leaving $12\frac{1}{2} - 12 = \frac{1}{2}$ litre left over.
REASONING: Dividing the total volume by the capacity of each bottle tells us how many equal portions fit into the total. Since we cannot sell a fraction of a full bottle in this context, the whole number part represents full bottles, and multiplying back reveals the exact remaining volume.
ANSWER: 16 full bottles and $\frac{1}{2}$ litre of milk left over
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A piece of cloth is $8\frac{1}{4}$ metres long. If strips of length $\frac{3}{4}$ metre are cut from it to make decorative banners, how many banners can be made?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given values. Total length of cloth = $8\frac{1}{4}$ metres; length of each banner = $\frac{3}{4}$ metre.
- Step 2: Convert the mixed number to an improper fraction: $8\frac{1}{4} = \frac{8 \times 4 + 1}{4} = \frac{33}{4}$ metres.
- Step 3: Set up the division expression: $\frac{33}{4} \div \frac{3}{4}$.
- Step 4: Rewrite division as multiplication by the reciprocal: $\frac{33}{4} \times \frac{4}{3}$.
- Step 5: Cancel common factors ($4$ in numerator and denominator, and divide $33$ by $3$): $\frac{33 \div 3}{1} \times \frac{1}{1} = 11$.
REASONING: When both fractions share the same denominator, dividing them simplifies directly to dividing their numerators because the denominator units cancel out ($\frac{33}{4} \div \frac{3}{4} = 33 \div 3 = 11$).
ANSWER: 11 banners
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: An athlete runs a total distance of $15\frac{3}{4}$ km during a week. If each daily training session is $2\frac{1}{4}$ km long, how many days did the athlete train?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify given values. Total distance = $15\frac{3}{4}$ km; daily distance = $2\frac{1}{4}$ km.
- Step 2: Convert both mixed numbers into improper fractions: $15\frac{3}{4} = \frac{63}{4}$ and $2\frac{1}{4} = \frac{9}{4}$.
- Step 3: Set up the division expression to find the number of days: $\frac{63}{4} \div \frac{9}{4}$.
- Step 4: Multiply by the reciprocal of the divisor: $\frac{63}{4} \times \frac{4}{9}$.
- Step 5: Cancel out the common factor of $4$ and divide $63$ by $9$: $\frac{63}{9} = 7$.
- Step 6: Verify the result by multiplying the number of days by the daily distance: $7 \times \frac{9}{4} = \frac{63}{4} = 15\frac{3}{4}$ km, which matches the given total.
REASONING: Finding the number of sessions from a total distance and a fixed daily distance is a classic grouping division problem, solved efficiently by converting to improper fractions and cancelling common denominators.
ANSWER: 7 days
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Ritu has a container with $4\frac{1}{2}$ litres of juice. She claims that she can fill $9$ glasses if each glass holds $\frac{1}{2}$ litre, and also claims she can fill $9$ glasses if each glass holds $\frac{1}{4}$ litre. Evaluate her statement using mathematical reasoning.
COMPETENCY: Evaluating statements and identifying errors in fraction division logic
STEPS:
- Step 1: Analyze Ritu's first claim by calculating $4\frac{1}{2} \div \frac{1}{2}$. Convert $4\frac{1}{2} = \frac{9}{2}$, then $\frac{9}{2} \times \frac{2}{1} = 9$ glasses. Her first claim is correct.
- Step 2: Analyze Ritu's second claim by calculating $4\frac{1}{2} \div \frac{1}{4}$. Convert $4\frac{1}{2} = \frac{9}{2}$, then $\frac{9}{2} \times \frac{4}{1} = \frac{36}{2} = 18$ glasses.
- Step 3: Compare the calculated result ($18$ glasses) with Ritu's claim ($9$ glasses).
- Step 4: Conclude that the second claim is false because halving the size of the glass doubles the number of glasses that can be filled.
ANSWER: Ritu's first claim is correct ($4\frac{1}{2} \div \frac{1}{2} = 9$), but her second claim is incorrect. When the glass size is halved to $\frac{1}{4}$ litre, the number of glasses doubles to $18$, not $9$.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Two students, Amit and Sneha, are given the problem: "A rope of length $10$ metres is cut into pieces of length $1\frac{1}{4}$ metres. How many pieces are formed?" 
Amit's method: $10 \div \frac{5}{4} = 10 \times \frac{4}{5} = 8$ pieces.
Sneha's method: $10 \times 1\frac{1}{4} = 10 \times \frac{5}{4} = \frac{50}{4} = 12\frac{1}{2}$ pieces.
Who is correct, and what conceptual error did the other student make?
COMPETENCY: Error identification and justifying method selection in word problems
STEPS:
- Step 1: Read the problem carefully to determine if it is a partitioning (division) or scaling (multiplication) problem. Since a whole rope is being split into smaller equal parts, division is required.
- Step 2: Evaluate Amit's work: he correctly converted $1\frac{1}{4}$ to $\frac{5}{4}$, set up division $10 \div \frac{5}{4}$, applied the reciprocal rule, and calculated $8$ pieces.
- Step 3: Evaluate Sneha's work: she used multiplication instead of division, which calculates the total length of $10$ pieces of size $1\frac{1}{4}$, rather than how many pieces fit into $10$ metres.
- Step 4: Formulate the final conclusion explaining why Amit is correct and Sneha misunderstood the operation.
ANSWER: Amit is correct. Sneha made the conceptual error of multiplying instead of dividing; she solved for the total length of $10$ ropes of length $1\frac{1}{4}$ metres instead of finding how many $\frac{1}{4}$ metre segments fit into $10$ metres.
DIFFICULTY: hard
