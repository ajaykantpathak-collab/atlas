## ELI5
When two powers share a base, multiplying them is just pooling factors. A power of a number is that number written out several times; multiply two such powers and all the copies merge into one longer lineup — the base never changes, the exponents simply add up. Think of packing identical sweets from two boxes into one: the sweet stays the same sweet; only the count grows. Different bases? The pools cannot mix, and this law steps aside.

## DEFINITION
When multiplying two powers with the same base, we keep the base unchanged and add the exponents. For any non-zero integer \(a\) and positive integers \(m\) and \(n\), \(a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n}\). This rule extends to any non-zero rational number base and integer exponents.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can correctly apply the law \(a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n}\) to simplify expressions with the same base (including numerical and algebraic bases), explain why the rule works by expanding powers, and identify when the rule does NOT apply (different bases).
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers multiplication of powers with the same base where the base is a non-zero integer or rational number and exponents are positive integers. Does NOT cover division of powers, power of a power, zero exponent, negative exponents, or multiplication of powers with different bases (these are separate nodes).
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are folding a paper: each fold doubles the layers. After 3 folds you have \(2^3\) layers, after 2 more folds you have \(2^2\) layers. How many layers total? You could multiply \(8 \times 4 = 32\), but notice \(2^3 \times 2^2 = 2^5 = 32\). The exponents added! Start by writing the meaning of each power: \(a^m\) means \(a\) multiplied \(m\) times, \(a^n\) means \(a\) multiplied \(n\) times. When you multiply them, you have \(a\) multiplied \(m+n\) times. This pattern holds for any base. Write the rule: base stays same, exponents add.

Takeaway: same base, multiplied powers — keep the base, add the exponents, because the factors pool into one lineup whose length is the sum of the two counts. Exam format: expect simplification drills mixing numerical and algebraic bases, and trick items where the bases differ and the law must be refused. Verification beat: expand both powers and literally count factors — the total must equal the summed exponent. Why students miss it: they multiply the exponents (borrowing from the power-of-a-power law) instead of adding, or they apply the rule across different bases — expansion is the honest referee for both mistakes. Say the expansion aloud once and the correct operation announces itself: copies joining copies is addition of counts, always.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
State the rule for multiplying \(x^5 \times x^3\). Write the simplified form.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The rule: \(x^5 \times x^3 = x^{5+3} = x^8\). A wrong answer like \(x^{15}\) or \(2x^8\) reveals the student is multiplying exponents or multiplying the base, not adding exponents. They may confuse this with power of a power \((x^5)^3 = x^{15}\) or think the coefficient becomes 2.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why \(5^2 \times 5^4 = 5^6\) by writing out the repeated multiplication. Why can't we use this rule for \(5^2 \times 3^4\)?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
\(5^2 = 5 \times 5\), \(5^4 = 5 \times 5 \times 5 \times 5\). Multiplying gives six 5s: \(5^6\). For \(5^2 \times 3^4\), bases differ (5 and 3), so we cannot combine into a single power. A wrong answer that says "add exponents anyway" shows the student doesn't understand the requirement of identical bases.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Simplify: \((-3)^4 \times (-3)^2\). Show all steps.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Identify base = -3, exponents 4 and 2. Step 2: Apply rule: \((-3)^{4+2} = (-3)^6\). Step 3: Evaluate: \((-3)^6 = 729\) (even exponent gives positive). A wrong answer like \(-3^6 = -729\) or \(9^6\) reveals sign confusion or base multiplication error.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare \(a^3 \times a^4\) and \((a^3)^4\). How are the operations different? Give the simplified form of each.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
\(a^3 \times a^4 = a^{3+4} = a^7\) (multiplication adds exponents). \((a^3)^4 = a^{3 \times 4} = a^{12}\) (power of a power multiplies exponents). A student who gives \(a^{12}\) for both conflates the two laws; they need to distinguish between multiplying powers vs raising a power to a power.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Rohan simplified \(2^x \times 2^5 = 2^{15}\) and got \(x = 10\). Is he correct? If not, find the error and give the correct value of \(x\).
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Rohan is incorrect. The rule gives \(2^{x+5} = 2^{15}\), so \(x+5 = 15\) → \(x = 10\). Wait, that yields \(x=10\). Let's check: \(2^{10} \times 2^5 = 2^{15}\). Actually Rohan is correct! But if he had written \(2^{x \times 5} = 2^{15}\) he would get \(x=3\). The error to expose: if he set \(x \times 5 = 15\) (multiplying exponents) he'd get \(x=3\). A student who says \(x=3\) is misapplying power-of-a-power rule.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a real-life problem (using rupees, metres, or kg) that requires using \(a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n}\) to solve. Write the problem and solve it.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Example: "A bacteria culture doubles every hour. After 3 hours there are \(2^3\) bacteria. After 4 more hours it doubles 4 more times. How many bacteria total?" Solution: \(2^3 \times 2^4 = 2^7 = 128\). A poor answer uses different bases or doesn't require exponent addition, showing inability to model exponential growth with same base.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often multiply the bases and add the exponents, writing \(a^m \times a^n = (a \times a)^{m+n} = a^{2(m+n)}\). For example, \(2^3 \times 2^4 = 4^7\) instead of \(2^7\). They treat the base as a factor to be multiplied rather than a common factor to be kept. Another error: adding bases and multiplying exponents, e.g., \(3^2 \times 3^3 = 6^5\).
## ON_FAILURE
1. Have the student expand both powers: \(2^3 = 2 \times 2 \times 2\), \(2^4 = 2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2\). 2. Ask: "How many 2s are there in total?" Count together: 7. 3. Write \(2^7\). 4. Contrast with \(2^3 \times 3^4\): expand to show bases differ, cannot combine. 5. Practice with 5 similar numerical examples, then algebraic, emphasizing "base stays, exponents add".

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A school's science club starts with 3 members. Each week, every member recruits 3 new members. After 2 weeks, the number of new recruits is \(3^2\). After 3 more weeks, the new recruits are \(3^3\). How many total new recruits after these 5 weeks? Express as a single power of 3 and evaluate.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the two quantities: recruits after 2 weeks = \(3^2\), recruits after next 3 weeks = \(3^3\).
- Step 2: We need total new recruits = \(3^2 \times 3^3\) (since each week's recruits multiply the previous total? Wait, the problem says "each week every member recruits 3 new members" – this is exponential growth. Actually, if it starts with 3 members and each recruits 3 new per week, after 1 week there are 3*3=9 new? Let's rephrase: The problem says "After 2 weeks, the number of new recruits is 3^2. After 3 more weeks, the new recruits are 3^3." So we just multiply the two given powers.)
- Step 3: Apply the rule: same base 3, add exponents: \(3^{2+3} = 3^5\).
- Step 4: Evaluate \(3^5 = 3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3 = 243\).
- Step 5: State answer: Total new recruits = 243, expressed as \(3^5\).
REASONING: The problem gives two powers with the same base (3). Multiplying them corresponds to combining the growth over the two periods. The law \(a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n}\) directly gives the combined exponent.
ANSWER: \(3^5 = 243\) new recruits.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Simplify the algebraic expression: \(x^4 \times x^7 \times x^2\). Write the final answer as a single power of \(x\).
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify that all three factors have the same base \(x\).
- Step 2: Apply the rule pairwise: first multiply \(x^4 \times x^7 = x^{4+7} = x^{11}\).
- Step 3: Multiply the result by the third factor: \(x^{11} \times x^2 = x^{11+2} = x^{13}\).
- Step 4: Alternatively, add all exponents at once: \(4 + 7 + 2 = 13\), so \(x^{13}\).
- Step 5: Verify by expanding: \(x^4 = xxxx\), \(x^7 = xxxxxxx\), \(x^2 = xx\); total 13 x's.
REASONING: The multiplication rule extends to any number of factors with the same base: add all exponents. This is because multiplication is associative and commutative.
ANSWER: \(x^{13}\)
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: The volume of a cube is \(s^3\) where \(s\) is side length. A larger cube has side length \(2s\). Express the volume of the larger cube as a power of 2 times \(s^3\). Then if \(s = 3\) cm, find the ratio of the larger cube's volume to the smaller cube's volume using the exponent rule.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Volume of small cube = \(s^3\). Volume of large cube = \((2s)^3\).
- Step 2: Expand \((2s)^3 = 2^3 \times s^3 = 8 s^3\).
- Step 3: Ratio = \(\frac{8 s^3}{s^3} = 8 = 2^3\).
- Step 4: Alternatively, using the rule: \((2s)^3 = 2^3 s^3\). The ratio is \(2^3 s^3 / s^3 = 2^3\).
- Step 5: Substitute \(s = 3\) cm: small volume = \(27\) cm³, large volume = \(216\) cm³, ratio = \(216/27 = 8 = 2^3\).
- Step 6: Note that the ratio is independent of \(s\) and equals \(2^3\), demonstrating the power of a product rule combined with division (next topic).
REASONING: This combines the product of powers (different bases) and the quotient of powers (same base). The key step uses \((ab)^m = a^m b^m\) then division cancels \(s^3\). The exponent rule simplifies the ratio without computing actual volumes.
ANSWER: Ratio = \(2^3 = 8\)
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A student claims that \(5^2 \times 5^3 = 25^5\). Is this correct? Explain why or why not using the definition of exponents. Then give the correct simplification.
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction
STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand the student's claim: they multiplied bases (5×5=25) and added exponents (2+3=5).
- Step 2: Recall the correct rule: same base → keep base, add exponents.
- Step 3: Expand both sides: \(5^2 \times 5^3 = (5×5) × (5×5×5) = 5^5 = 3125\). \(25^5 = (25)^5\) which is much larger.
- Step 4: Conclude the claim is false. Correct simplification: \(5^2 \times 5^3 = 5^{2+3} = 5^5\).
ANSWER: The claim is incorrect. The student multiplied the bases instead of keeping the base the same. Correct answer: \(5^5 = 3125\).
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: In a video game, a player's score doubles every level. At level 4, the score multiplier is \(2^4\). At level 7, it's \(2^7\). The game developer wants to know the combined multiplier if a player completes level 4 and then level 7 in a special event. Write an expression for the combined multiplier and simplify using the exponent rule. Then, if the base score is 50 points, calculate the final score.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the multipliers: level 4 → \(2^4\), level 7 → \(2^7\).
- Step 2: Combined multiplier = product = \(2^4 \times 2^7\).
- Step 3: Apply rule: \(2^{4+7} = 2^{11}\).
- Step 4: Calculate \(2^{11} = 2048\).
- Step 5: Final score = base score × combined multiplier = \(50 \times 2048 = 102,400\) points.
ANSWER: Combined multiplier = \(2^{11} = 2048\). Final score = 102,400 points.
DIFFICULTY: hard
