## ELI5
Nature rarely eats by a single menu. The owl takes mice, but so does the snake; the mouse nibbles seeds, and also insects. Cross every eater with every meal and the straight chains braid into a web — the honest portrait of who-feeds-whom in an ecosystem. The braiding is not decorative: if one dish vanishes, most diners find another counter. Webs, not chains, are why ecosystems bend without breaking.

## DEFINITION
A food web is a network of interconnected food chains that shows the multiple feeding relationships among different organisms in an ecosystem, illustrating how energy and nutrients flow through various pathways.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can construct a food web from a given list of organisms and their feeding relationships, and explain how the removal of one species affects multiple pathways in the web.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the concept of food webs as interconnected food chains, the complexity of feeding relationships, and the stability they provide. It does not cover quantitative energy transfer efficiencies (covered in c8_physics_ch8_6) or detailed population dynamics.
## WALKTHROUGH
Start by observing a local garden or park: notice that a butterfly feeds on nectar from flowers, a frog eats the butterfly, a snake eats the frog, and a hawk eats the snake. But also, the frog may eat grasshoppers, and the hawk may also eat mice. This shows that one organism can be part of multiple food chains. Connect this to the prior concept of food chains (c8_physics_ch8_4) where energy flows linearly. Explain that in reality, these chains interlock to form a web. Guide students to map out the feeding relationships using arrows to show energy flow, and discuss how this interconnectedness makes the ecosystem more resilient.

Takeaway: a food web interlocks many chains — arrows still point from eaten to eater, but each organism typically feeds and is fed along several routes, which spreads risk across the network. Exam format: expect web construction from a species list and 'what happens if this species is removed?' — trace at least two affected pathways, not one. Verification beat: every arrow must match a stated feeding habit; an invented link fails the construction even if the shape looks right. Why students miss it: they answer removal questions with a single chain — the web's defining property is the alternative route, and naming it is the resilience argument examiners wait for. Draw the alternatives first; the species's importance is measured in how few of them remain.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is a food web, and how does it differ from a food chain?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
A food web is a network of multiple interconnected food chains showing various feeding relationships in an ecosystem, while a food chain is a single linear sequence of who eats whom. A wrong answer (e.g., "food web is just a bigger food chain") reveals the student does not grasp the branching and multiple connections that define a web.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why does a food web provide more stability to an ecosystem than a single food chain?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
A food web provides alternative pathways for energy flow; if one species declines, predators can switch to other prey, preventing collapse. A wrong answer (e.g., "food webs have more organisms") shows the student misses the concept of alternative energy pathways and redundancy.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Given the following organisms in a pond ecosystem: algae, zooplankton, small fish, large fish, kingfisher, and bacteria (decomposers). Draw a food web showing at least three interconnected food chains.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
A correct food web would show: algae → zooplankton → small fish → large fish → kingfisher; algae → zooplankton → small fish → kingfisher; and dead organisms → bacteria → nutrients → algae. The student must draw arrows indicating energy flow. A wrong answer with only a single linear chain indicates inability to see multiple feeding links.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
In a forest food web, if the population of rabbits (primary consumers) suddenly decreases, predict the effect on the populations of foxes (secondary consumers) and grass (producers). Explain your reasoning.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Foxes may decline due to less food, but they might switch to other prey (e.g., mice) if available, showing the buffering effect of a food web. Grass may increase initially due to reduced grazing, but other herbivores may increase and compensate. A wrong answer that predicts only linear effects (foxes die out, grass grows unchecked) shows failure to analyze alternative pathways.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student claims: "Food webs are just complicated food chains; they don't tell us anything new about energy flow." Evaluate this statement.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is incorrect. Food webs reveal the complexity of energy flow, showing that energy can travel through multiple routes, and that ecosystems have resilience due to redundancy. They also show that organisms can occupy multiple trophic levels. A wrong evaluation indicates the student cannot critique the added value of food webs over chains.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple activity using locally available materials (e.g., yarn, cards with organism names) to demonstrate a food web for a nearby ecosystem (e.g., a farm, a lake, a garden). Explain how your activity shows interconnectedness.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good activity: Create organism cards (producers, consumers, decomposers) for a local ecosystem. Students hold cards and pass a ball of yarn to show who eats whom, creating a physical web. The web visualizes multiple connections. A poor activity might only create a single chain, missing the interconnectedness.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think that in a food web, each organism has only one predator and one prey, essentially treating the web as a set of separate linear chains. For example, they may draw a frog eating only grasshoppers and being eaten only by snakes, ignoring that frogs also eat flies and are eaten by herons. This misconception arises from over-reliance on simple food chain diagrams.
## ON_FAILURE
To address this, have students list all the foods a common animal (e.g., a crow) eats and all the animals that eat it. Then, using a whiteboard, draw a food web for a familiar ecosystem (like a school garden) where each student adds one organism and its feeding links. Emphasize that arrows can branch out from one organism to several others. Practice with at least three different ecosystems until they naturally draw branching arrows.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: In a grassland ecosystem, the following feeding relationships are observed: Grass is eaten by grasshoppers and rabbits. Grasshoppers are eaten by frogs and birds. Rabbits are eaten by foxes and eagles. Frogs are eaten by snakes and birds. Snakes are eaten by eagles. Birds are eaten by eagles. Foxes are eaten by eagles. Decomposers break down dead organisms. Construct a food web with at least four interconnected food chains.
STEPS:
- Step 1: List all organisms and categorize them as producers (grass), primary consumers (grasshoppers, rabbits), secondary consumers (frogs, birds, foxes), tertiary consumers (snakes, eagles), and decomposers.
- Step 2: Draw the producer at the bottom. Draw arrows from grass to grasshoppers and rabbits.
- Step 3: From grasshoppers, draw arrows to frogs and birds. From rabbits, draw arrows to foxes and eagles.
- Step 4: From frogs, draw arrows to snakes and birds. From snakes, draw arrow to eagles. From birds, draw arrow to eagles. From foxes, draw arrow to eagles.
- Step 5: Add decomposers with arrows from all dead organisms to decomposers, and from decomposers back to grass (nutrient cycling). Verify that there are at least four distinct chains (e.g., grass→grasshopper→frog→snake→eagle; grass→grasshopper→bird→eagle; grass→rabbit→fox→eagle; grass→rabbit→eagle).
REASONING: This step-by-step construction ensures all feeding links are captured and shows how multiple chains share organisms, forming a web. The branching arrows visually represent the interconnectedness.
ANSWER: A food web diagram with the described connections and at least four distinct food chains.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A student draws a food web for a pond: Algae → Zooplankton → Small Fish → Large Fish → Heron. They also draw Algae → Zooplankton → Small Fish → Frog → Snake → Heron. They claim this is a complete food web. Identify what is missing and improve the web by adding at least two more realistic connections.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Analyze the given chains: they share algae, zooplankton, small fish, and heron, but the frog and snake chain is separate after small fish.
- Step 2: Identify missing organisms: decomposers (bacteria) are absent; also, large fish might eat frogs, and herons might eat frogs directly.
- Step 3: Add decomposers with arrows from all dead organisms to bacteria, and from bacteria to algae (nutrients).
- Step 4: Add cross-links: large fish → frog (large fish eat frogs), heron → frog (herons eat frogs), snake → heron (snakes eaten by herons).
- Step 5: Check that the web now has multiple pathways to heron (via large fish, via snake, via frog) and includes nutrient cycling.
REASONING: A complete food web must include decomposers for nutrient cycling and show cross-links between chains to reflect real feeding flexibility. The improved web demonstrates greater stability.
ANSWER: An improved food web diagram including decomposers and additional cross-links (large fish→frog, heron→frog, snake→heron).
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Consider a forest food web where a disease wipes out the population of a primary consumer (deer). Using the concept of food webs, explain the possible short-term and long-term effects on the populations of tigers (apex predator), grass (producer), and wolves (another predator that also eats deer and rabbits). Assume wolves can switch to rabbits.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the direct links: deer are eaten by tigers and wolves. Grass is eaten by deer. Wolves also eat rabbits.
- Step 2: Short-term effect on tigers: Tigers lose a major food source; their population may decline unless they have alternative prey (not mentioned, so assume decline).
- Step 3: Short-term effect on grass: With fewer deer, grazing pressure reduces, so grass population may increase initially.
- Step 4: Short-term effect on wolves: Wolves lose deer but can switch to rabbits; their population may be stable or decline slightly depending on rabbit abundance.
- Step 5: Long-term effects: Increased grass may support other herbivores (e.g., rabbits increase), which could support wolves. Tigers may decline further or switch prey if possible. The web's complexity buffers the wolf population but not necessarily the tiger if it's specialized.
- Step 6: Conclude that food web structure (alternative prey for wolves) provides resilience, while specialized predators (tigers) are more vulnerable.
REASONING: This analysis uses the food web's multiple connections to predict differential impacts on species based on their feeding flexibility, illustrating the ecological importance of interconnectedness.
ANSWER: Short-term: tigers decline, grass increases, wolves stable (switch to rabbits). Long-term: rabbit increase may support wolves; tigers may decline further; grass may stabilize as other herbivores increase. The food web buffers wolves but not specialized tigers.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A farmer sprays pesticide that kills most insects in a rice field. Using a food web perspective, explain how this could affect the rice plants, frogs, snakes, and birds of prey in the surrounding area.
COMPETENCY: Predicting outcomes from changing variables in an ecosystem
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the feeding relationships: rice plants (producers) → insects (primary consumers) → frogs (secondary consumers) → snakes (tertiary consumers) → birds of prey (quaternary consumers). Also, insects may pollinate rice or be pests.
- Step 2: Direct effect: Insects die, so frogs lose food source. Frogs decline.
- Step 3: Indirect effect: Snakes lose frogs as prey; they may decline or switch to other prey (e.g., rodents).
- Step 4: Birds of prey lose snakes; they may decline or switch.
- Step 5: Rice plants: If insects were pests, rice may benefit initially; but if insects were pollinators or decomposers, long-term soil health may suffer. Also, loss of frogs may increase other pests.
- Step 6: Synthesize: The pesticide disrupts multiple trophic levels, showing how a change at one level cascades through the web.
ANSWER: The pesticide reduces insects, causing frog decline, then snake and bird declines. Rice may have short-term pest reduction but long-term ecosystem imbalance. The food web shows cascading effects.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: In a coastal mangrove ecosystem, crabs feed on mangrove leaves and detritus. Crabs are eaten by fish, birds, and monitor lizards. Fish are eaten by birds and crocodiles. Birds also eat insects. Monitor lizards eat eggs of birds and fish. Draw a food web for this system. Then, predict which population would be most affected if a new predator is introduced that specializes in eating crabs. Justify your answer using the concept of trophic cascades and food web complexity.
COMPETENCY: Interpreting physical situations using ecological models and reasoning about stability
STEPS:
- Step 1: Draw the food web: Mangrove leaves/detritus → Crabs → Fish, Birds, Monitor Lizards. Fish → Birds, Crocodiles. Birds also ← Insects. Monitor Lizards → Bird eggs, Fish eggs.
- Step 2: Identify the new predator's impact: It directly reduces crab population.
- Step 3: Direct effects: Fish, birds, monitor lizards lose a food source (crabs). But birds have insects, fish have other prey? Not specified. Monitor lizards have eggs.
- Step 4: Indirect effects: With fewer crabs, mangrove leaf litter may accumulate (crabs are detritivores), affecting nutrient cycling. Fish may decline, affecting crocodiles. Birds may rely more on insects and fish. Monitor lizards may eat more eggs.
- Step 5: Determine most affected: Fish that rely heavily on crabs may be most affected if they have no alternative prey. Birds have insects as alternative, so less affected. Monitor lizards have eggs. Crocodiles depend on fish, so indirectly affected.
- Step 6: Justify: The species with the fewest alternative pathways in the web (specialized fish) suffers most. This shows how food web structure determines vulnerability.
ANSWER: The fish population that specializes on crabs would be most affected because it has fewer alternative energy pathways in the web. Birds and monitor lizards have other food sources. This demonstrates that food web complexity provides stability for generalists but specialists are vulnerable.
DIFFICULTY: hard
