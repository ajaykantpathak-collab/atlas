## ELI5
A tailor holds three-quarters of a metre and needs two-thirds of that length: 'of' again - multiply across. Numerators pair up, denominators pair up, and the old habit of common denominators stays in the addition drawer where it belongs. Cross-cancelling before multiplying keeps the numbers small and friendly. And expect shrinkage: multiplying by a proper fraction takes a part of a part, so the finished border is less than the cloth you started with - exactly what the tailor ordered.

## DEFINITION
Multiplying two fractions involves multiplying their numerators together to form the new numerator and multiplying their denominators together to form the new denominator. The resulting fraction is then simplified to its lowest terms.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
Can correctly multiply two proper, improper, or mixed fractions by multiplying numerators and denominators, simplifying the result, and applying the operation to solve multi-step word problems involving real-world quantities.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers the multiplication of two fractions (proper, improper, and mixed fractions) and their simplification. It does NOT cover algebraic rational expressions, decimal multiplication, or fraction division.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you have a piece of cloth that is $\frac{3}{4}$ of a metre long, and you need to use only $\frac{2}{3}$ of that length for a craft project at school. To find out what fraction of a whole metre you are actually using, you need to find a fraction of a fraction, which means multiplying the two fractions together. To begin, write down the two fractions side by side with a multiplication sign between them, just like you did when multiplying whole numbers or integers. Recall from earlier work that fractions represent parts of a whole, and multiplying them scales one part by another. The key insight is the numerator and denominator rule: multiply the top numbers (numerators) together for the new top number, and multiply the bottom numbers (denominators) together for the new bottom number.

**Takeaway:** multiply straight across, cross-cancel early, and simplify at the end. **In the exam:** in 'fraction of a fraction' word problems, translate 'of' to multiplication and keep the units. **Check yourself:** compute the tailor's border length both ways - cancel-first and cancel-after - and compare effort. **Why students miss it:** they chase common denominators, a ritual that belongs to addition, not multiplication. Say 'of' aloud whenever the word appears, and the multiplication sign writes itself into the working.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the rule for finding the numerator when multiplying two fractions together?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The correct answer is that you multiply the numerators of both fractions directly across. If a student answers that you add the numerators, it reveals that they are confusing fraction multiplication with fraction addition, where common denominators are required.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why multiplying two proper fractions (like $\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2}$) results in a product ($\frac{1}{4}$) that is smaller than either of the original fractions, unlike whole number multiplication which usually makes numbers larger.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The correct explanation is that multiplying by a proper fraction means taking a part of a part, which shrinks the quantity rather than scaling it up. If a student states that multiplication always makes numbers larger, it reveals a rigid whole-number bias that fails to comprehend fractional scaling.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Calculate the product of $\frac{3}{5}$ and $\frac{4}{7}$, and simplify if possible.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The correct answer is $\frac{12}{35}$. Working: Step 1: Multiply numerators $3 \times 4 = 12$. Step 2: Multiply denominators $5 \times 7 = 35$. Step 3: Combine to get $\frac{12}{35}$. Step 4: Check for common factors between 12 and 35 (none other than 1), so it is in simplest form. If a student answers $\frac{7}{12}$, it reveals they added the numerators and denominators instead of multiplying them.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the methods of multiplying two fractions by multiplying numerators/denominators first versus cross-cancelling common factors beforehand. Which method prevents large numbers and why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Cross-cancelling before multiplying prevents large numbers because it divides out common factors between any numerator and any denominator early, keeping numbers small and easier to manage before final multiplication. If a student claims cross-cancelling changes the value of the fraction, it reveals a misunderstanding of equivalent fractions and simplification properties.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate the following student work: To find $\frac{8}{15} \times \frac{5}{12}$, the student multiplies numerators to get $40$ and denominators to get $180$, yielding $\frac{40}{180}$, and stops. Is this fully correct, and how can it be improved?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The work is mathematically correct in value but incomplete because the fraction is not in its simplest form. The student should simplify $\frac{40}{180}$ by dividing both numerator and denominator by their greatest common divisor, 20, to get $\frac{2}{9}$, or use cross-cancellation beforehand. If a student says the answer is completely wrong, it reveals they cannot distinguish between an unsimplified fraction and an incorrect calculation.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a multiplication problem involving two mixed fractions representing lengths of cloth in metres, where the product results in an improper fraction that must be converted back to a mixed fraction.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A valid problem is: A tailor has a fabric roll of length $1\frac{1}{2}$ metres and uses $2\frac{1}{3}$ rolls for a curtain. Find the total length used. Solution setup: Convert $1\frac{1}{2}$ to $\frac{3}{2}$ and $2\frac{1}{3}$ to $\frac{7}{3}$, multiply to get $\frac{21}{6}$, simplify to $\frac{7}{2}$, and convert back to $3\frac{1}{2}$ metres. ## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students frequently multiply the numerators but mistakenly add the denominators together (e.g., calculating $\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{4}{5}$ as $\frac{8}{8}$ instead of $\frac{8}{15}$). This happens because students inappropriately mix up the rules for addition/subtraction of fractions (which require a common denominator and combining numerators) with the rule for multiplication.
## ON_FAILURE
Remind the student that multiplication is an independent operation that scales quantities without needing common denominators. Show them visually with a grid: shade $\frac{2}{3}$ vertically and $\frac{4}{5}$ horizontally to prove that the overlapping shaded region has $2 \times 4 = 8$ parts out of a total $3 \times 5 = 15$ grid squares. Emphasize the verbal rule: "Top times top, bottom times bottom."

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Riya buys $\frac{3}{4}$ kg of sweets. Her brother eats $\frac{2}{3}$ of these sweets. What fraction of a kilogram of sweets did her brother eat?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the given quantities: Total sweets bought = $\frac{3}{4}$ kg, fraction eaten by brother = $\frac{2}{3}$ of the total.
- Step 2: Determine the operation required: Finding a fraction of a fraction requires multiplication, so we need to compute $\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{3}{4}$.
- Step 3: Apply the numerator and denominator rule: Multiply numerators together and denominators together: $\frac{2 \times 3}{3 \times 4}$.
- Step 4: Compute the intermediate product: $\frac{6}{12}$.
- Step 5: Simplify the fraction by dividing the numerator and denominator by their greatest common divisor, 6: $\frac{6 \div 6}{12 \div 6} = \frac{1}{2}$ kg.
REASONING: Multiplying the two fractions scales the initial amount ($\frac{3}{4}$) down by the factor $\frac{2}{3}$. Cross-cancelling the 3s before multiplying would also yield $\frac{2}{4}$, which simplifies directly to $\frac{1}{2}$.
ANSWER: $\frac{1}{2}$ kg
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A rectangular park has a length of $2\frac{1}{2}$ metres and a width of $1\frac{3}{5}$ metres. Calculate the area of the park.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the given mixed fractions for length ($2\frac{1}{2}$ m) and width ($1\frac{3}{5}$ m).
- Step 2: Convert both mixed fractions into improper fractions. For length: $2\frac{1}{2} = \frac{(2 \times 2) + 1}{2} = \frac{5}{2}$. For width: $1\frac{3}{5} = \frac{(1 \times 5) + 3}{5} = \frac{8}{5}$.
- Step 3: Set up the area multiplication formula: Area = Length $\times$ Breadth = $\frac{5}{2} \times \frac{8}{5}$.
- Step 4: Apply cross-cancellation or multiply directly: $\frac{5 \times 8}{2 \times 5} = \frac{40}{10}$.
- Step 5: Simplify the resulting fraction: $\frac{40}{10} = 4$ square metres.
REASONING: Mixed fractions must always be converted into improper fractions before applying the numerator and denominator multiplication rule. Cross-cancellation of the number 5 in numerator and denominator makes the calculation much simpler.
ANSWER: $4\text{ m}^2$
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Amit has a piece of wire measuring $\frac{9}{10}$ of a metre. He uses $\frac{5}{6}$ of it to build a model. Then, from that used portion, he paints $\frac{2}{3}$ of it red. What fraction of a total metre is the red-painted wire?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify all three fractional parts: Initial wire = $\frac{9}{10}$, fraction used = $\frac{5}{6}$, fraction painted red of the used portion = $\frac{2}{3}$.
- Step 2: Set up a chain multiplication expression to find the final fraction: $\frac{9}{10} \times \frac{5}{6} \times \frac{2}{3}$.
- Step 3: Multiply the numerators together and the denominators together: $\frac{9 \times 5 \times 2}{10 \times 6 \times 3} = \frac{90}{180}$.
- Step 4: Alternatively, cross-cancel common factors across the numerators and denominators: cancel 5 and 10 (leaving 2 in denominator), cancel 2 and 2, cancel 9 and 3/6. Let us do it systematically: $\frac{9}{10} \times \frac{5}{6} = \frac{45}{60} = \frac{3}{4}$.
- Step 5: Multiply the result by the final fraction: $\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{2}{3} = \frac{6}{12} = \frac{1}{2}$ of a metre.
REASONING: Consecutive fractional parts of a quantity can be multiplied sequentially using the numerator and denominator rule at each step or combined into a single large product before simplification.
ANSWER: $\frac{1}{2}$ metre
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1

Meera cross-cancels the 14 and 7 (leaving 2 and 1) before multiplying to immediately get $\frac{8}{15}$. Explain whose method is more efficient and why, using mathematical reasoning about factors.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying mathematical methods
STEPS:
- Step 1: Examine Aarav's method of direct multiplication followed by simplification.
- Step 2: Examine Meera's method of cross-cancellation prior to multiplication.
- Step 3: Compare the size of numbers generated in intermediate steps (56 and 105 vs 8 and 15).
- Step 4: Formulate justification based on divisibility and minimizing computational errors with large numbers.
ANSWER: Meera's method is more efficient because cross-cancelling common factors between numerators and denominators beforehand reduces numbers to their smallest terms, avoiding large numbers like 56 and 105 and lowering the risk of calculation errors.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A farmer owns a rectangular plot of land where the length is expressed as an improper fraction $\frac{25}{4}$ metres, and the width is $\frac{12}{5}$ metres. A pathway taking up $\frac{1}{3}$ of the total area is paved with stones. Calculate the exact area of the land that remains unpaved.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world multi-step situations to mathematical expressions and fraction operations
STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate the total area by multiplying length and width: $\frac{25}{4} \times \frac{12}{5}$.
- Step 2: Simplify the multiplication using cross-cancellation ($\frac{25}{5} = 5$ and $\frac{12}{4} = 3$) to get total area = $15\text{ m}^2$.
- Step 3: Find the paved area, which is $\frac{1}{3}$ of the total area ($15\text{ m}^2$): $\frac{1}{3} \times 15 = 5\text{ m}^2$.
- Step 4: Subtract the paved area from the total area to find the unpaved area: $15 - 5 = 10\text{ m}^2$.
ANSWER: The unpaved area of the land is $10\text{ square metres}$.
DIFFICULTY: hard
