## ELI5
Twelve red flags and eighteen blue flags must march in identical rows with none left over: the row sizes that work for both are the common factors. List every factor of each number, keep only the names on both lists, and remember one is always invited - every whole number divides by one exactly. Common factors never exceed their numbers; they are the divisors, not the multiples. The flags never lie: a leftover row means you picked a stranger from only one list.

## DEFINITION
Common factors of two numbers are the whole numbers that divide both given numbers exactly without leaving any remainder. In other words, they are the intersection of the factor sets of both numbers.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are organizing a school sports event and have 12 red flags and 18 blue flags. You want to divide both types of flags into identical rows so that no flags are left over, asking: what are the possible sizes of equal groups we can make? To solve this, we begin by listing every single factor of 12 and every single factor of 18 using our knowledge of multiplication tables. Building on our Class 6 work with factors and multiples, we find numbers that divide both 12 and 18. The key insight is that the numbers appearing in both lists are our common factors.

**Takeaway:** common factors are the shared divisors - numbers that divide both quantities exactly. **In the exam:** list each number's factors completely in pairs, then intersect the lists; never offer a multiple. **Check yourself:** write both factor lists for the flags in paired columns and circle the shared names. **Why students miss it:** they promote multiples into the list, offering big numbers that divide nothing. Circle the shared names twice - once per list - before calling any of them common. Cross out any name that appears on one list only, and the common set purifies itself.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
$C$ must be less than or equal to both $A$ and $B$ (except when $A$ or $B$ is negative, but in whole numbers, $C \le A$ and $C \le B$). A wrong answer suggesting $C$ can be larger than $A$ or $B$ confuses factors with multiples.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often confuse common factors with common multiples, listing numbers that are larger than the given numbers instead of smaller divisors. For example, when finding common factors of 4 and 6, a student might list 12, 24, and 36 because they calculate $4 \times 3$ and $6 \times 2$, performing multiplication instead of division.
## ON_FAILURE
Remind the student that factors always divide *into* a number, so they must be smaller than or equal to the number itself. Ask them to test division: "Can 12 fit inside 4?" If not, it's a multiple, not a factor. Guide them to write down division sentences like $4 \div 2 = 2$ to prove 2 is a factor.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Priya has two ribbon pieces measuring 18 cm and 24 cm. She wants to cut both into smaller pieces of equal length with no ribbon left over. Find all possible common lengths she can cut.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the task, which is to find all numbers that divide both 18 and 24 completely (the common factors).
- Step 2: List all individual factors of 18 by finding multiplication pairs: $1 \times 18$, $2 \times 9$, $3 \times 6$. Factors of 18 are 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18.
- Step 3: List all individual factors of 24 by finding multiplication pairs: $1 \times 24$, $2 \times 12$, $3 \times 8$, $4 \times 6$. Factors of 24 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24.
- Step 4: Compare both lists to find matching values: {1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18} and {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24}. The matching numbers are 1, 2, 3, and 6.
- Step 5: Verify each common factor by dividing both 18 and 24: $18 \div 6 = 3$ and $24 \div 6 = 4$, both with zero remainder.
REASONING: By listing every factor of each number and identifying intersections, we determine all possible equal segment lengths that satisfy the problem constraint.
ANSWER: 1 cm, 2 cm, 3 cm, and 6 cm
