## ELI5
Two spoons rest in the same hot dal on the kitchen stove: steel scorches your fingers, wood barely warms. Same heat, different messengers - good conductors relay energy eagerly; bad conductors stall it politely. That is why pans wear copper or aluminium bodies with wooden or plastic handles: the body cooks, the handle protects. Warm clothes work the same trick, trapping slow-conducting air. Materials choose their talents; design chooses which talent to use.

## DEFINITION
Good and bad conductors of heat are classifications of materials based on how easily thermal energy travels through them, measured qualitatively by their thermal conductivity (no standard SI unit required for Class 7 qualitative classification). Good conductors (such as metals like copper and aluminum) permit heat to transfer rapidly through particle collision, whereas bad conductors or thermal insulators (such as wood, plastic, wool, and air) significantly restrict the flow of heat.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Which of the following common kitchen items is classified as a good conductor of heat?
A) Wooden spatula
B) Plastic handle of a pressure cooker
C) Stainless steel frying pan
D) Woollen potholder
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The correct answer is C. Stainless steel is a metal alloy, and metals are good conductors of heat that allow thermal energy to transfer rapidly from the stove to the food. A student who picks A, B, or D is confusing thermal insulators (bad conductors) with conductors, likely because those items are used to *handle* hot objects safely without burning their hands.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why woollen clothes keep us warm in winter, even though wool itself does not generate any heat.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Wool is a bad conductor of heat (an insulator) and traps a large amount of air within its fibers, and trapped air is also a very poor conductor of heat. Therefore, wool prevents the body heat from escaping out into the colder surrounding winter air, keeping us warm. A student who answers that wool "produces heat" has a fundamental misconception that clothing or insulators are heat sources rather than thermal barriers that slow down heat loss.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A metal rod and a plastic rod of identical length and thickness are heated at one end for 5 minutes. If the temperature at the unheated end of the metal rod rises by 40 °C, predict what happens to the temperature at the unheated end of the plastic rod, and calculate the temperature difference if the plastic end rises by only 5 °C.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Steps:
1. Identify the given values: Temperature change at unheated end of metal rod = 40 °C; Temperature change at unheated end of plastic rod = 5 °C.
2. Identify what to find: The temperature difference between the two ends ($\Delta T$).
3. Set up the subtraction: $\Delta T = \text{Metal temperature change} - \text{Plastic temperature change}$.
4. Substitute values with units: $\Delta T = 40\text{ °C} - 5\text{ °C}$.
5. Compute and track units: $\Delta T = 35\text{ °C}$.
Answer: The temperature difference is 35 °C. A student who adds the values (45 °C) or subtracts them in reverse (-35 °C) has made an arithmetic error or misunderstood how to find the difference between two comparative thermal responses.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Analyze why the base of a modern cooking utensil is often made of copper or aluminum, while its handles are coated with thick bakelite or rubber.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The base is made of a good conductor (copper or aluminum) so that heat from the stove transfers quickly and uniformly to the food being cooked. The handles are coated with a bad conductor (bakelite or rubber) to prevent heat from conducting to the cook's hand, ensuring safe handling. A student who thinks the entire utensil should be made of plastic fails to analyze that a plastic base would melt on a stove and would fail to conduct heat to the food efficiently.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate the claim: "Air is a good conductor of heat because heat travels through the atmosphere from the Sun to the Earth." Is this statement scientifically accurate? Justify your answer.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is scientifically inaccurate. Heat travels from the Sun to the Earth through the vacuum of space via radiation, not conduction through air. In fact, air is a very bad conductor of heat (an excellent thermal insulator), which is why double-pane windows and bird feathers trap air to prevent heat transfer. A student who agrees with the claim is confusing the phenomenon of atmospheric radiation/convection with the specific property of thermal conduction through solid or gaseous media.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple, safe experiment using household items to test whether water is a good or bad conductor of heat, and describe what observation would confirm your conclusion.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
To test water, one could take a test tube filled with water, drop a small piece of ice weighted with a sinker at the very bottom, and gently heat only the upper part of the water using a candle flame until it boils. If the ice at the bottom takes a very long time to melt while the top water boils, it demonstrates that water is a poor conductor of heat (a bad conductor). A poor or incomplete answer would suggest heating the bottom of the tube directly, which causes bulk movement (convection) rather than testing pure conduction through stationary water.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think that bad conductors (insulators) actually "produce" cold or actively block cold from entering, rather than simply slowing down the transfer of thermal energy. For example, a student might believe a woollen blanket is cold because it feels cool when first touched, or that an insulated box creates cold inside it.
## ON_FAILURE
To correct this, guide the student to measure the temperature of a woollen blanket and a metal chair inside an air-conditioned room using a thermometer; both will show the exact same room temperature. Explain that the metal chair feels colder because it is a good conductor that rapidly draws heat away from your warm hand, whereas the woollen blanket is a bad conductor that fails to carry heat away from your hand, making it feel neutral or warm.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: In a school kitchen science lab in Mumbai, four rods of identical dimensions made of copper, iron, glass, and wood are coated with wax at one end and heated simultaneously at the opposite end. If copper melts its wax coating in 15 seconds, iron in 45 seconds, glass in 300 seconds, and wood does not melt its wax even after 600 seconds, calculate the time difference in seconds between copper and glass in melting their wax coatings.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify given values with units: Time taken by copper ($t_{\text{copper}}$) = 15 s; Time taken by glass ($t_{\text{glass}}$) = 300 s.
- step 2: Identify what to find: The time difference ($\Delta t$) between glass and copper.
- step 3: Set up the subtraction equation: $\Delta t = t_{\text{glass}} - t_{\text{copper}}$.
- step 4: Substitute values with units: $\Delta t = 300\text{ s} - 15\text{ s}$.
- step 5: Compute and verify units: $\Delta t = 285\text{ s}$, which is physically reasonable since glass is a much worse conductor than copper and takes significantly longer to transfer heat.
REASONING: The time taken for wax to melt indicates how quickly heat travels down the rod. Subtracting the two times gives the exact duration advantage copper has over glass due to its superior thermal conductivity.
ANSWER: 285 s
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: An ice box used by a street vendor in Delhi has double walls made of steel with a layer of sawdust (a bad conductor) packed in between. If the outer steel wall receives heat from the surroundings at a rate of 120 joules per minute, and the inner sawdust layer blocks 90 joules per minute from reaching the inside, calculate the net heat energy per minute that penetrates into the ice box chamber.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify given values with units: Heat entering outer wall ($H_{\text{in}}$) = 120 J/min; Heat blocked by sawdust layer ($H_{\text{blocked}}$) = 90 J/min.
- step 2: Identify what to find: Net heat entering the inner chamber ($H_{\text{net}}$).
- step 3: Formulate the equation: $H_{\text{net}} = H_{\text{in}} - H_{\text{blocked}}$.
- step 4: Substitute values with units: $H_{\text{net}} = 120\text{ J/min} - 90\text{ J/min}$.
- step 5: Compute and verify units: $H_{\text{net}} = 30\text{ J/min}$, which is physically reasonable because an insulating layer successfully reduces but does not completely eliminate heat flow.
REASONING: Insulators reduce the amount of heat passing through them. By subtracting the blocked heat from the incoming heat, we find the remaining heat that leaks into the ice chamber.
ANSWER: 30 J/min
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A composite cooking vessel in a kitchen has a copper base of thickness 4 mm and an aluminum base of thickness 6 mm. If heat travels through the 4 mm copper layer in 2 seconds and through the 6 mm aluminum layer in 3 seconds, calculate the total time taken for heat to traverse the combined 10 mm base thickness, and find the average speed of heat travel across the total thickness.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify given values with units: Time for copper layer ($t_1$) = 2 s; Time for aluminum layer ($t_2$) = 3 s; Total thickness ($d$) = $4\text{ mm} + 6\text{ mm} = 10\text{ mm}$.
- step 2: Identify what to find: Total time ($T$) and average speed of heat travel ($v_{\text{avg}} = d / T$).
- step 3: Calculate total time by summing individual times: $T = 2\text{ s} + 3\text{ s} = 5\text{ s}$.
- step 4: Substitute distance and total time into speed formula: $v_{\text{avg}} = 10\text{ mm} / 5\text{ s}$.
- step 5: Compute and track units: $v_{\text{avg}} = 2\text{ mm/s}$, and verify that the result is physically sensible for conductive heat propagation across thin metal plates.
REASONING: Total time is the sum of times taken through each consecutive layer. Dividing total thickness by total time gives the average rate (speed) at which heat traverses the composite solid barrier.
ANSWER: Total time = 5 s; Average speed = 2 mm/s
