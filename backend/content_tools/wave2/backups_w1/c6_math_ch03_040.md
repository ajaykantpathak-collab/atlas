## ELI5
Every fraction is a division story wearing a bar for a hat. The top is what gets shared; the bottom is who shares it. A few rotis among several friends: the rotis sit on top, the friends below, and the bar reads 'divided by'. Whoever swaps them hands each friend a fraction of a friend - nonsense. Total above, receivers below, and the bar does the rest.

## DEFINITION
A fraction represents a division operation where a total quantity is split into equal parts; the fraction $a/b$ is the result of dividing the quantity $a$ into $b$ equal shares. In this context, the numerator represents the total amount being shared, and the denominator represents the number of people sharing that amount.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to convert a division word problem into a fractional expression and visualize that expression as an equal distribution of items among a group.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the conceptual link between division and fractions for whole numbers resulting in shares smaller than or equal to the whole. It does not cover rational number theory, repeating decimals, or irrational numbers.
## WALKTHROUGH
If you have 3 rotis and want to share them equally among 4 friends, how much does each person get? Many students try to divide the people by the rotis, but the key is to remember that the "total to be shared" is the numerator and the "number of receivers" is the denominator. Start by imagining one roti divided into 4 pieces; since you have 3 rotis, you have three sets of "1/4 pieces." By giving each person one piece from each roti, each friend receives 3 pieces of 1/4 size. The key insight is that a fraction is simply a division problem that hasn't been turned into a single whole number yet.

**Takeaway:** the fraction bar is a division sign: numerator is the total being shared, denominator is the number of sharers. **In the exam:** in division-to-fraction questions, place the total on top and the sharers below, then read the bar as divided by. **Check yourself:** replay the walkthrough's rotis-and-friends table - the shared total goes above, the circle of friends below. **Why students miss it:** students flip the two roles - the walkthrough's table scene is a no to that; food is shared by people, never the reverse. Rehearse the exam move once more - total, sharers, bar - and the answer arranges itself in exam-ready order with nothing left to chance.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What does the fraction $5/8$ represent in terms of division?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
It represents 5 divided by 8 ($5 \div 8$). A student who answers "8 divided by 5" is confusing the numerator and denominator, failing to recognize that the top number is the amount being shared (the dividend).

**A wrong answer (reading the division upside down) reveals the student swapped the roles - the top is the amount being shared, the bottom the sharing count.**

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If 7 friends share 2 pizzas equally, can you write this as a division problem and then as a fraction?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Division: $2 \div 7$; Fraction: $2/7$. A student who says $7/2$ believes the larger number must always be the denominator, ignoring the context that the 2 pizzas are the items being cut.

**A wrong answer (letting the bigger number sink) reveals the student obeyed size, not story - the pizzas are what get cut, so the pizza count sits on top regardless.**

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A merchant has 5 kg of rice to pack into 4 equal bags. How much rice goes into each bag? Show your steps.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
1. Identify dividend: 5 kg. 2. Identify divisor: 4 bags. 3. Form division: $5 \div 4$. 4. Form fraction: $5/4$ kg (or $1 \frac{1}{4}$ kg). A student who answers $4/5$ has swapped the items and the containers.

**A wrong answer (packing people into rice) reveals the student flipped the packing - the rice is divided into the bags, so the rice leads the division.**

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Why is $3 \div 4$ the same as $3/4$? Use the example of sharing 3 rotis among 4 children.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
When you divide 3 rotis into 4 parts each, you have 12 pieces of size $1/4$. Giving each child 3 pieces means they get $3 \times 1/4 = 3/4$. If a student cannot explain this, they are memorizing the symbol without seeing the physical division.

**A wrong answer (denying the equivalence) reveals the student missed the cut-and-collect picture - slicing each roti and dealing the pieces reproduces the division exactly.**

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it possible for a fraction $a/b$ to be equal to a whole number? Give an example and explain why.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, if $a$ is a multiple of $b$. For example, $8/4 = 2$, because sharing 8 rotis among 4 people results in each person getting 2 whole rotis. Answering "no" suggests the student thinks fractions must always be "broken" pieces less than 1.

**A wrong answer (refusing whole-number results) reveals the student met the tidy case - when the total is an exact multiple, the share is a clean whole number.**

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a real-world scenario involving 5 litres of juice and 3 people that results in a fraction.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
"If 5 litres of juice are poured equally into 3 large glasses, how many litres of juice are in each glass?" An answer failing to connect the total amount (5) to the numerator (5) shows a lack of understanding of the division-fraction relationship.

**A wrong answer (mismatching total and sharers) reveals the student crossed the ledger - the litres must sit on top and the drinkers below for the bar to read truly.**

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe the larger number must always be the denominator (the bottom number), thinking $3/4$ is correct but $4/3$ is "impossible." This arises from seeing fractions only as "parts of a whole" rather than "division."
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to draw 4 rotis and try to give them to 3 people. Physically drawing the division helps them see that each person gets more than 1 whole roti, proving the numerator can be larger than the denominator.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A farmer has 9 kg of grain to be distributed equally into 5 sacks. How many kilograms of grain will be in each sack?
STEPS:
- Identify the total weight: 9 kg.
- Identify the number of containers: 5 sacks.
- Express as division: $9 \div 5$.
- Write as a fraction: $9/5$.
- Convert to mixed number: $1 \frac{4}{5}$.
REASONING: Since we are splitting the total mass (9) into 5 equal parts, we use the division definition of a fraction. The resulting fraction $9/5$ shows each sack contains more than 1 kg.
ANSWER: $1 \frac{4}{5}$ kg.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Three friends want to share 5 chocolate bars equally. What fraction of a bar does each friend receive?
STEPS:
- Identify total bars: 5 bars.
- Identify number of friends: 3 friends.
- Apply division: $5 \div 3$.
- Convert to fraction: $5/3$.
- Simplify to mixed number: $1 \frac{2}{3}$.
REASONING: Each person gets one whole bar, and the remaining 2 bars are split into 3 pieces each, providing $2/3$ more to each person.
ANSWER: $1 \frac{2}{3}$ bars.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A school project requires 11 metres of ribbon to be cut into 4 equal strips for a craft. How long is each strip?
STEPS:
- Identify total length: 11 metres.
- Identify number of pieces: 4 strips.
- Setup division: $11 \div 4$.
- Convert to fraction: $11/4$.
- Convert to mixed number: $2 \frac{3}{4}$.
- Verify: $2 \times 4 = 8$, plus $3/4 \times 4 = 3$, total is 11.
REASONING: By dividing the total length by the number of strips, we determine the unit length of each piece. The fraction $11/4$ represents this division perfectly.
ANSWER: $2 \frac{3}{4}$ metres.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You have 7 litres of milk to share among 4 bowls. A friend says, "You will get 1 litre, and 3 litres will be left over." Another friend says, "Each bowl gets $7/4$ litres." Who is correct and why?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying mathematical representations.
STEPS:
- Calculate $7 \div 4$.
- Verify the first friend's claim: 1 litre per bowl uses 4 litres, leaving 3.
- Verify the second friend's claim: $7/4$ is the mathematical result of the division $7 \div 4$.
- Conclude: Both are describing the same outcome, but the second friend uses a fraction, which is more precise.
ANSWER: Both are correct; the first friend describes the process (whole numbers), while the second friend provides the exact mathematical value of the share using a fraction.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: If you increase the number of people sharing 5 rotis from 4 to 10, does the fraction representing each person's share get larger or smaller? Explain.
COMPETENCY: Analyzing relationships between variables.
STEPS:
- Initial share: $5/4$.
- New share: $5/10$.
- Compare denominators: 10 > 4.
- Compare result: $5/4 = 1.25$ and $5/10 = 0.5$.
- Reasoning: As the denominator increases while the numerator stays the same, the portion size must decrease.
ANSWER: The share gets smaller. Because the same amount of roti is now being split into more pieces, each individual piece must be smaller.
