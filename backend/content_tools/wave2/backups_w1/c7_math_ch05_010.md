## ELI5
A carpenter sliding a door needs two tracks perfectly parallel, and ruler and compass alone deliver them: copy an angle. Draw a transversal through the given line and the outside point, swing an arc on the base line, then carry the same arc - compass width unchanged - to the point and copy the corresponding angle. Equal corresponding angles force the new line parallel. Change the compass width midway and the copied angle quietly distorts; the whole method lives on that discipline.

## DEFINITION
Drawing parallel lines using a ruler and a compass is a geometric construction method based on creating equal corresponding angles or alternate interior angles relative to a transversal line. By replicating an angle formed by a transversal with a given line, we guarantee that the newly constructed line never intersects the original line, no matter how far they are extended.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are a carpenter laying out two wooden tracks for a sliding door in your classroom cupboard; the tracks must maintain the exact same distance apart all along their length so the door never jams. How can you draw an exact parallel track on a wooden board if you only have a straight ruler and a marking compass? You begin by drawing your base line and picking a point outside it, then drawing a transversal line connecting the point to the base line. By using your compass to measure an angle at the base line and copying that exact same opening to the outer point, you create equal corresponding angles. Because lines with equal corresponding angles are parallel, your second track is guaranteed to be parallel to the first.

**Takeaway:** constructing parallels is angle-copying: reproduce a corresponding or alternate angle at the outside point. **In the exam:** describe construction steps in order - transversal, base arc, transferred arc, copied angle, final line through the point. **Check yourself:** perform the construction once on paper and verify with a set-square or folded edge. **Why students miss it:** they alter the compass width between arcs, copying a different angle and losing parallelism silently.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why must the compass width remain unchanged when transferring an angle from the base line to the external point during a parallel line construction?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Keeping the compass width unchanged ensures that the arcs drawn have the exact same radius, which is mathematically necessary to replicate an identical angle size. If a student thinks it is just to save time, they misunderstand that arc radius preserves distance consistency in geometric copying.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
When constructing a line parallel to line $L$ through an external point $P$ using a transversal $PQ$, if the angle formed at the transversal with line $L$ is measured by an arc, what property of parallel lines are you utilizing?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Corresponding angles (or alternate interior angles) are equal. Working steps: 1. Identify the transversal cutting line $L$ at point $Q$ and passing through point $P$. 2. Draw an arc from $Q$ cutting line $L$ and transversal $PQ$. 3. With the same radius and center at $P$, draw a corresponding arc. 4. Measure the chord length of the first arc and transfer it to the second arc to ensure equal angles, confirming the converse of the corresponding angles axiom. If a student answers "perpendicular distance", they confuse parallel line construction with dropping a perpendicular.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Analyze the effect of changing the compass radius *after* drawing the first arc on the base line but *before* drawing the arc at the external point. Will the resulting line be parallel?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
No, the resulting line will not be parallel. Changing the compass radius alters the arc length and thus changes the size of the angle being copied, violating the condition for equal corresponding angles. If a student says it will still be parallel, they do not understand that arc radii dictate angle congruence in compass constructions.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate this student's method for constructing a parallel line through point $X$ to line $AB$: "I drew a line through $X$ perpendicular to $AB$, and then another line through $X$ perpendicular to my first perpendicular line." Is this method valid?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, the method is completely valid. Two lines perpendicular to the same line (or where one is perpendicular to a perpendicular) are parallel to each other. If a student judges it incorrect, they are unaware of alternative valid construction theorems for parallel lines.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often change the compass radius halfway through copying an angle between the transversal intersection and the external point. For example, if they open the compass to 3 cm to draw an arc on the baseline, they mistakenly adjust it to 4 cm when drawing the matching arc at the point $P$, resulting in intersecting lines instead of parallel lines.
## ON_FAILURE
Remind the student that a compass is a tool for transferring exact distances. Say: "Your compass width is like a locked measuring tape; once you measure the distance from the baseline to the arc, you are not allowed to change the screw until both arcs are drawn." Demonstrate by locking the compass tighter or showing how changing the radius changes the angle opening.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Construct a line parallel to a given line $AB$ through a point $P$ that lies outside $AB$, using only a ruler and a compass.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Draw a line $AB$ and mark a point $P$ not on $AB$. Take any point $Q$ on line $AB$ and join $P$ to $Q$ to form a transversal line segment.
- Step 2: With $Q$ as center and any convenient radius, draw an arc intersecting line $AB$ at point $X$ and transversal $PQ$ at point $Y$.
- Step 3: With $P$ as center and the exact same radius (without changing the compass width), draw an arc cutting transversal $PQ$ at point $Z$.
- Step 4: Measure the distance between $X$ and $Y$ using the compass. With $Z$ as center and radius equal to length $XY$, draw an arc that intersects the previous arc at point $W$.
- Step 5: Draw a straight line passing through $P$ and $W$ using a ruler, producing line $CD$ parallel to $AB$.
REASONING: By making the arc from $P$ match the arc from $Q$ and setting the chord length from $Z$ equal to $XY$, we replicate the corresponding angle at point $P$. Equal corresponding angles guarantee that the constructed line $CD$ is strictly parallel to $AB$.
ANSWER: Line $CD$ passing through $P$ is parallel to line $AB$.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A local gardener needs to plant two parallel rows of marigolds in a school garden path in New Delhi. The baseline fence is line $EF$. The first flower is to be planted at point $R$, exactly 5 metres away from the fence. Using geometric construction steps, describe how the gardener can plot the second row line parallel to $EF$ passing through $R$.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify baseline line $EF$ and external point $R$ representing the planting position.
- Step 2: Draw an arbitrary transversal line through $R$ that intersects line $EF$ at a point $S$.
- Step 3: Place the compass pointer at $S$, choose a fixed radius $r$, and draw an arc cutting $EF$ at $G$ and $SR$ at $H$.
- Step 4: Without altering the compass radius $r$, place the compass pointer at $R$ and draw an arc cutting $SR$ at $T$.
- Step 5: Measure compass width $GH$. Keeping this exact span, place the compass pointer at $T$ and draw an arc intersecting the arc from Step 4 at point $U$. Join points $R$ and $U$ with a straightedge to form the parallel row line.
REASONING: The construction mimics the Alternate Interior Angles theorem. By ensuring that the alternate angle at $R$ matches the angle at $S$, the line $RU$ is parallel to $EF$.
ANSWER: The straight line through $R$ and $U$ forms the parallel path for the second row of marigolds.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Priya is designing a rangoli pattern for a school festival in Mumbai. She draws a baseline segment $AB$ of length 8 cm. She needs to construct a line parallel to $AB$ at a perpendicular distance of 3 cm. Show how she can achieve this using a compass, a ruler, and a set square or perpendicular construction.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Choose point $A$ on the baseline segment $AB$. Construct a perpendicular line to $AB$ at $A$ using a compass by drawing arcs above and below.
- Step 2: Mark a point $P$ on this perpendicular line such that the length of segment $AP$ measured by a ruler is exactly 3 cm.
- Step 3: Choose another point $B$ on line $AB$ and construct a second perpendicular line to $AB$ at $B$ using the compass.
- Step 4: Mark a point $Q$ on this second perpendicular line such that the length of segment $BQ$ is also exactly 3 cm.
- Step 5: Align the straightedge ruler along points $P$ and $Q$, and draw line $PQ$.
- Step 6: Verify that line $PQ$ is parallel to $AB$ by measuring the perpendicular distance at 3 different intervals; each must measure exactly 3 cm.
REASONING: According to the geometric property that two lines everywhere equidistant or perpendicular to the same base line are parallel, constructing two equal perpendicular heights of 3 cm guarantees strict parallelism.
ANSWER: Line $PQ$ is parallel to $AB$ at a constant perpendicular distance of 3 cm.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A carpenter wants to prove that two wooden battens are parallel without measuring their entire lengths. He draws a transversal line crossing both battens and measures the corresponding angles formed at the intersections using a paper protractor. He finds one angle is $65^\circ$ and the other is $62^\circ$. Can the carpenter conclude the battens are parallel? If not, what geometric theorem does this violate?
COMPETENCY: Justifying and evaluating geometric properties of parallel lines
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the given angle measurements: $65^\circ$ and $62^\circ$.
- Step 2: Recall the condition for lines cut by a transversal to be parallel (Corresponding angles must be equal).
- Step 3: Compare the measured angles ($65^\circ \neq 62^\circ$).
- Step 4: Apply the Corresponding Angles Axiom / Converse to judge parallelism.
ANSWER: No, the carpenter cannot conclude the battens are parallel. This violates the Converse of the Corresponding Angles Axiom, which states that lines cut by a transversal are parallel if and only if their corresponding angles are equal. Since $65^\circ \neq 62^\circ$, the battens are intersecting slightly.
DIFFICULTY: hard
