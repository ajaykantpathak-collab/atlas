## ELI5
Pocket money in the piggy bank climbs 10, 15, 20, 25 rupees - four steps, one steady rhythm of plus five. Identifying a pattern is verifying that rhythm: check the differences between at least three consecutive pairs, because two terms can impersonate any rule. Once the jump is certified constant, the tenth day's saving is a short multiplication, not ten deposits. Some sequences grow by multiplying instead, and some by two-step recipes - the checking habit tells them apart.

## DEFINITION
Identifying patterns in number sequences involves observing how consecutive terms change by a constant difference, ratio, or rule to predict subsequent terms or find a general expression. It requires discovering the underlying mathematical relationship governing the progression of numbers.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
Can analyze any given linear or non-linear number sequence, determine the underlying rule (such as constant differences or products), calculate missing intermediate terms, and predict the $n$-th term of the sequence with algebraic reasoning.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers arithmetic sequences (constant differences), geometric sequences (constant multipliers), and basic quadratic or mixed difference patterns appropriate for Class 7. Does NOT cover advanced calculus limits or complex recursive functions.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are saving pocket money in your piggy bank: ₹10 on Day 1, ₹15 on Day 2, ₹20 on Day 3, and ₹25 on Day 4. How much will you save on Day 10 without counting every single day? To answer this, we start by writing down the given numbers in a row and examining the difference between consecutive terms. We connect this to our prior knowledge of operations and integer patterns. The key insight is that instead of computing step-by-step blindly, we look for a hidden rule or relationship—a formula that links the position of a term directly to its value.

**Takeaway:** a pattern is confirmed by testing the change across several consecutive pairs, not just the first two. **In the exam:** compute differences, then ratios, then two-step rules; state the rule and test it on every given term. **Check yourself:** extend the piggy-bank savings by three days and re-check the difference each time. **Why students miss it:** they certify a rule from one pair of terms and meet a different sequence on the fifth term. Certify the rule on every term given, and the tenth day answers itself in one line of arithmetic. Differences first, ratios second, recipes last - the order of checks never changes.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the primary difference between identifying a pattern in a sequence of whole numbers versus a sequence of integers?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Integers can involve both positive and negative values, meaning the sequence can decrease as well as increase across zero, whereas whole numbers only start from zero and increase or stay non-negative. If a student thinks they are identical, they miss how negative signs flip the direction of addition and subtraction rules.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why must we check at least three consecutive pairs of numbers when identifying a pattern in a sequence rather than just looking at the first two terms?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Checking only the first two terms can mislead you because two numbers can fit many different rules (like both arithmetic and geometric rules). Checking three or more pairs ensures the rule is consistent across the entire sequence. If a student says two numbers are enough, they fail to recognize that non-linear or multi-step patterns require multiple confirmations.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Find the next two terms in the number sequence: $5, 11, 17, 23, \dots$
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The correct answer is $29$ and $35$. Working steps: 1) Find the difference between the second and first term: $11 - 5 = 6$. 2) Verify with the next pair: $17 - 11 = 6$, and $23 - 17 = 6$. 3) Conclude that the rule is to add $6$ to the previous term. 4) Calculate the next terms: $23 + 6 = 29$, and $29 + 6 = 35$. If a student gives numbers like $28$ or $31$, they miscalculated the constant difference or used an incorrect operation.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Analyze the sequence: $2, 4, 8, 16, 32, \dots$. Is the rule for this sequence additive or multiplicative? Explain how you can tell the difference.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The rule is multiplicative. We can tell because the differences between consecutive terms are not constant ($4-2=2$, but $8-4=4$), whereas the ratios between consecutive terms are constant ($4 \div 2 = 2$, $8 \div 4 = 2$). If a student claims it is additive with a varying difference, they failed to check for constant multiplication.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student claims that the sequence $1, 4, 9, 16, 25$ follows a rule of adding consecutive odd numbers ($+3, +5, +7, +9$). Another student claims it follows the rule $n^2$ where $n$ is the position number. Are both students correct, and which explanation is more powerful?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Both students are mathematically correct. The first student describes the recursive pattern of differences, while the second student provides the explicit algebraic formula for the $n$-th term. The explicit formula ($n^2$) is more powerful because it allows us to find the 100th term directly without calculating the previous 99 terms. If a student says one is wrong, they misunderstand that different valid perspectives can describe the same pattern.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a number sequence of 5 terms that starts with the number 3 and uses a two-step rule: "multiply by 2, then subtract 1". Show all terms.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The expected sequence is: $3, 5, 9, 17, 33$. Working criteria: Term 1 is 3. Term 2: $(3 \times 2) - 1 = 5$. Term 3: $(5 \times 2) - 1 = 9$. Term 4: $(9 \times 2) - 1 = 17$. Term 5: $(17 \times 2) - 1 = 33$. A poor answer shows arithmetic errors in the multi-step calculation or applies the operations in the wrong order.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often assume every sequence is arithmetic (uses addition/subtraction) and fail to test for geometric (multiplication/division) or squared relationships. For example, given the sequence $2, 6, 18, 54$, a student might look at the differences ($+4, +12, +36$) and get confused instead of noticing that each term is multiplied by 3.
## ON_FAILURE
Guide the student to test both addition and multiplication rules side-by-side. Say: "Let's check addition first by finding differences: $6 - 2 = 4$ and $18 - 6 = 12$. Are these equal? No. Since differences are not constant, let's check multiplication or division: $6 \div 2 = 3$ and $18 \div 6 = 3$. Since the multiplier is constant, this is a multiplicative pattern!"

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Riya saves money in her piggy bank for a school trip. On Week 1 she saves ₹50, on Week 2 she saves ₹65, on Week 3 she saves ₹80, and on Week 4 she saves ₹95. If this pattern continues, how much will she save on Week 10?
STEPS:
- step 1: Write down the given sequence of savings: $50, 65, 80, 95, \dots$ and identify that we need the 10th term ($n = 10$).
- step 2: Find the difference between consecutive terms to check for an arithmetic pattern: $65 - 50 = 15$, $80 - 65 = 15$, $95 - 80 = 15$. The common difference $d = 15$.
- step 3: Identify the first term ($a_1 = 50$) and use the explicit formula for an arithmetic sequence: $a_n = a_1 + (n - 1)d$.
- step 4: Substitute $n = 10$, $a_1 = 50$, and $d = 15$ into the formula: $a_{10} = 50 + (10 - 1) \times 15$.
- step 5: Compute step by step: $a_{10} = 50 + (9 \times 15) = 50 + 135 = 185$ rupees.
REASONING: By checking that the difference between weeks is always a constant ₹15, we confirm it is an arithmetic sequence. Using the formula $a_n = a_1 + (n-1)d$ saves us from having to write out all 10 terms individually.
ANSWER: ₹185
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A biologist observes a bacterial culture in a laboratory in Delhi. At minute 0, there are 3 bacteria. At minute 1, there are 6 bacteria. At minute 2, there are 12 bacteria. At minute 3, there are 24 bacteria. Following this geometric pattern, how many bacteria will there be at minute 6?
STEPS:
- step 1: Write down the sequence of bacterial counts: $3, 6, 12, 24, \dots$ for minutes $0, 1, 2, 3$.
- step 2: Test for an arithmetic rule ($6-3=3$, but $12-6=6$, so it is not arithmetic) and then test for a multiplicative ratio: $6 \div 3 = 2$, $12 \div 6 = 2$, $24 \div 12 = 2$. The common ratio $r = 2$.
- step 3: Set up the calculation for subsequent terms by multiplying the last known term by 2 repeatedly until minute 6.
- step 4: Compute the terms: Minute 4 = $24 \times 2 = 48$; Minute 5 = $48 \times 2 = 96$; Minute 6 = $96 \times 2 = 192$.
- step 5: Verify the total count with units, concluding there are 192 bacteria at minute 6.
REASONING: Since the difference between terms grows larger, addition does not work. Checking the ratio shows each term is doubled, making it a geometric progression where we multiply successively.
ANSWER: 192 bacteria
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A pattern of floor tiles in a courtyard follows a sequence where the number of tiles in successive rows is given by $-4, -1, 2, 5, 8, \dots$. Find the rule for the $n$-th term and determine which row will have exactly 35 tiles.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the sequence: $-4, -1, 2, 5, 8, \dots$ and find the difference between consecutive terms: $-1 - (-4) = -1 + 4 = 3$; $2 - (-1) = 3$. The common difference $d = 3$.
- step 2: State the first term $a_1 = -4$ and set up the explicit formula: $a_n = a_1 + (n-1)d \Rightarrow a_n = -4 + (n-1)3$.
- step 3: Simplify the algebraic expression for the $n$-th term: $a_n = -4 + 3n - 3 = 3n - 7$.
- step 4: Set the expression equal to 35 to find which row number ($n$) has 35 tiles: $3n - 7 = 35$.
- step 5: Solve the linear equation: $3n = 35 + 7 \Rightarrow 3n = 42 \Rightarrow n = 14$. Verify by checking row 14: $3(14) - 7 = 42 - 7 = 35$.
REASONING: By finding the common difference with negative integers, we build the algebraic formula $3n - 7$. Setting this equal to 35 and solving as a linear equation gives us the exact position number.
ANSWER: Row 14
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Amit notices that bus ticket numbers on his route follow a sequence: $7, 14, 21, 28, \dots$. He claims that the 50th ticket number will be $7 \times 50 = 350$. Priya claims that the sequence is just multiples of 7, so you can also find it by adding 7 fifty times. Are both reasoning methods valid? Which method is more efficient for finding the 1000th term, and why?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying mathematical reasoning methods
STEPS:
- step 1: Analyze Amit's method (multiplication/formula approach: $7n$) and Priya's method (repeated addition).
- step 2: Verify both methods for small terms (e.g., 3rd term: $7 \times 3 = 21$ vs $7+7+7=21$).
- step 3: Compare efficiency for large values like the 1000th term ($7 \times 1000 = 7000$ vs adding 7 one thousand times).
- step 4: Formulate the justification concluding that multiplication/algebraic rule is vastly superior and less prone to human counting error.
ANSWER: Both methods yield the correct values for small terms because multiplication is repeated addition. However, Amit's method (using the algebraic rule $7n$) is much more efficient for the 1000th term because a single multiplication takes one step, whereas repeated addition would require 999 separate addition steps and high risk of error.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A gardener arranges flower pots such that Row 1 has 2 pots, Row 2 has 6 pots, Row 3 has 12 pots, and Row 4 has 20 pots. A student looks at this and says, "The difference is 4, then 6, then 8. The differences are increasing by 2 each time!" Using this discovery, calculate how many pots will be in Row 6, and explain the rule of second differences.
COMPETENCY: Analyzing non-linear patterns through differences
STEPS:
- step 1: List the sequence of pots: $2, 6, 12, 20, \dots$ for rows $1, 2, 3, 4$.
- step 2: Calculate the first differences: $6-2 = 4$, $12-6 = 6$, $20-12 = 8$.
- step 3: Calculate the second differences (differences of the first differences): $6-4 = 2$, $8-6 = 2$. Notice the second difference is constant ($+2$).
- step 4: Extend the first differences using the constant second difference: Next first difference is $8 + 2 = 10$, and the one after is $10 + 2 = 12$.
- step 5: Use these new first differences to find Row 5 and Row 6: Row 5 = $20 + 10 = 30$ pots; Row 6 = $30 + 12 = 42$ pots.
ANSWER: Row 6 will have 42 pots. The explanation of second differences shows that when first differences are not constant but increase by a constant amount (second difference), the pattern represents a quadratic sequence. By extending the first differences ($4, 6, 8, 10, 12$), we add 10 to Row 4 to get 30 (Row 5), and add 12 to Row 5 to get 42 (Row 6).
DIFFICULTY: hard
