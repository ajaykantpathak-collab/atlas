## ELI5
After the farewell party, three-quarters of the cake survives; you carry home half of that leftover. Fraction multiplication is 'of' made arithmetic: numerators multiply, denominators multiply, and the answer shrinks to lowest terms. Half of three-quarters is three-eighths of the original cake - multiplying by a proper fraction always takes a piece of a piece, so the result lands smaller than either factor. Flip-sense returns too: any fraction times its own upside-down twin equals exactly one whole.

## DEFINITION
Multiplication of fractions is an operation where two or more fractional numbers are combined by multiplying their numerators together to form the new numerator and their denominators together to form the new denominator. The fundamental rule states that for any fractions $\frac{a}{b}$ and $\frac{c}{d}$ where $b \neq 0$ and $d \neq 0$, the product is given by $\frac{a \times c}{b \times d}$.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you bought a large cake for your school farewell party, and after the party, $\frac{3}{4}$ of the cake is left over. The next day, your teacher asks you to take home $\frac{1}{2}$ of that leftover cake. How much of the whole original cake are you taking home? To solve this, you need to find $\frac{1}{2}$ of $\frac{3}{4}$, which translates mathematically to multiplying $\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{3}{4}$. Building on your prior knowledge of fractions as parts of a whole from Class 6, fraction multiplication represents finding a fractional part of another fractional quantity. You begin by setting up the two fractions side by side with a multiplication sign between them. The key insight is that instead of finding a common denominator like you do in addition and subtraction, you multiply straight across: numerators multiply together and denominators multiply together.

**Takeaway:** fraction multiplication is straight across - numerators with numerators, denominators with denominators - then simplify. **In the exam:** convert mixed numbers first; simplify by the highest common factor at the end or cross-cancel early. **Check yourself:** compute half of the leftover cake two ways - direct and cross-cancelled - and match the results. **Why students miss it:** they hunt a common denominator first, importing the addition rule into a multiplication sum.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the primary rule for multiplying two fractions, such as $\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{4}{5}$?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The correct method is to multiply the numerators together ($2 \times 4 = 8$) and the denominators together ($3 \times 5 = 15$), resulting in $\frac{8}{15}$. A student who incorrectly finds a common denominator (like $\frac{10}{15} \times \frac{12}{15}$) is confusing fraction multiplication with fraction addition or subtraction, where common denominators are mandatory.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why multiplying two proper fractions (like $\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{3}$) results in a product that is smaller than either of the original fractions.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
When you multiply by a fraction less than 1, you are taking a portion of a portion, which shrinks the total value rather than expanding it. A student who thinks multiplication always makes numbers larger is applying whole-number multiplication rules universally without understanding that proper fractions represent parts of a whole.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Calculate the product of $\frac{3}{5}$ and $\frac{10}{9}$, and write your answer in its simplest form.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
First, multiply numerators and denominators: $\frac{3 \times 10}{5 \times 9} = \frac{30}{45}$. Next, find the Highest Common Factor (HCF) of 30 and 45, which is 15. Divide both numerator and denominator by 15 to get $\frac{2}{3}$. A student who answers $\frac{30}{45}$ without simplifying has missed the crucial step of reducing the fraction to its lowest terms using factorisation.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the result of multiplying $\frac{4}{7} \times \frac{7}{4}$ with the result of multiplying $\frac{3}{5} \times \frac{5}{3}$. What mathematical pattern do you observe?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Both products equal $1$ ($\frac{28}{28}$ and $\frac{15}{15}$). The pattern reveals that multiplying any fraction by its reciprocal (flipped version) always yields a product of 1. A student who calculates different numbers or fails to see the inverse relationship lacks pattern recognition regarding reciprocal fractions.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A student evaluates $\frac{2}{3} \times 4$ by writing $\frac{2 \times 4}{3 \times 4} = \frac{8}{12}$. Is this student's working correct? Justify your answer.

The student's working is incorrect. A whole number like 4 must be written as a fraction with a denominator of 1 ($\frac{4}{1}$), making the calculation $\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{4}{1} = \frac{8}{3}$. The student incorrectly multiplied both the numerator and the denominator by 4, which is actually multiplying the fraction by 1 ($\frac{4}{4}$) and changes nothing, violating the rule of multiplying a fraction by a whole number.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often mistakenly apply the rules of fraction addition and subtraction to multiplication by finding a common denominator before multiplying the numerators, or they forget to convert mixed numbers into improper fractions before multiplying. For example, when solving $2\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{3}{4}$, a student might multiply the whole numbers and fractions separately ($2 \times \frac{3}{4}$ and $\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{3}{4}$) or incorrectly add denominators instead of multiplying them.
## ON_FAILURE
Stop the student immediately and draw a visual area model, such as a rectangle divided into grids, to show that $\frac{1}{2} of \frac{3}{4}$ means shading half of three-fourths. Explicitly review the rule: "Numerators straight across, denominators straight across." Write out the steps clearly: step 1, check for mixed numbers and convert to improper fractions; step 2, multiply top numbers; step 3, multiply bottom numbers; step 4, simplify.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Rahul buys $2\frac{1}{2}$ kg of potatoes from the local vegetable market. If he uses $\frac{3}{5}$ of these potatoes to prepare dinner for his family, what weight of potatoes does he use?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the given quantities: total potatoes = $2\frac{1}{2}$ kg, fraction used = $\frac{3}{5}$.
- step 2: Convert the mixed fraction $2\frac{1}{2}$ into an improper fraction: $\frac{(2 \times 2) + 1}{2} = \frac{5}{2}$.
- step 3: Set up the multiplication expression: $\frac{5}{2} \times \frac{3}{5}$.
- step 4: Multiply the numerators together and the denominators together: $\frac{5 \times 3}{2 \times 5} = \frac{15}{10}$.
- step 5: Simplify the fraction by dividing numerator and denominator by their HCF (5), and attach units: $\frac{15 \div 5}{10 \div 5} = \frac{3}{2} = 1\frac{1}{2}$ kg.
REASONING: Mixed numbers cannot be multiplied directly as whole numbers and fractions combined; they must first be converted to improper fractions. Multiplying straight across gives the total fraction of potatoes used, which is then simplified and converted back to a mixed number with units of kilograms.
ANSWER: 1\frac{1}{2} kg
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A rectangular school garden has a length of $\frac{12}{5}$ metres and a breadth of $\frac{15}{8}$ metres. Calculate the area of the garden.
STEPS:
- step 1: Recall the formula for the area of a rectangle: Area = Length $\times$ Breadth.
- step 2: Substitute the given fractional dimensions into the formula: Area = $\frac{12}{5} \times \frac{15}{8}$.
- step 3: Multiply the numerators and denominators: $\frac{12 \times 15}{5 \times 8} = \frac{180}{40}$.
- step 4: Alternatively, simplify before multiplying by cancelling common factors across numerators and denominators: $\frac{12^{3}}{5^{1}} \times \frac{15^{3}}{8_{2}} = \frac{3 \times 3}{1 \times 2} = \frac{9}{2}$.
- step 5: Convert the improper fraction to a mixed number and attach square units: $\frac{9}{2} = 4\frac{1}{2}$ square metres.
REASONING: Area calculation requires multiplying two fractional dimensions. Cross-canceling common factors before multiplication makes computation easier and prevents large numbers, yielding $\frac{9}{2}$ or $4\frac{1}{2}\text{ m}^2$.
ANSWER: 4\frac{1}{2} \text{ m}^2
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Priya has a ribbon measuring $3\frac{1}{3}$ metres. She cuts off $\frac{3}{4}$ of it for a craft project. From the remaining piece, she further uses $\frac{2}{5}$ of it to wrap a gift. What exact length of ribbon is used for wrapping the gift?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the initial length of the ribbon: $3\frac{1}{3} = \frac{10}{3}$ metres.
- step 2: Calculate the length of the remaining ribbon after cutting off $\frac{3}{4}$ (meaning $\frac{1}{4}$ remains, or find total minus cut piece): Remaining = $3\frac{1}{3} \times (1 - \frac{3}{4}) = \frac{10}{3} \times \frac{1}{4} = \frac{10}{12} = \frac{5}{6}$ metres.
- step 3: Identify the fraction of the remaining ribbon used for wrapping the gift: $\frac{2}{5}$ of $\frac{5}{6}$.
- step 4: Set up the multiplication for the gift-wrapping ribbon: $\frac{2}{5} \times \frac{5}{6}$.
- step 5: Multiply numerators and denominators while cross-canceling common factors of 5 and 2: $\frac{2^{1}}{5^{1}} \times \frac{5^{1}}{6_{3}} = \frac{1 \times 1}{1 \times 3} = \frac{1}{3}$.
- step 6: Verify the steps and attach the final unit of measurement: $\frac{1}{3}$ metre.
REASONING: This multi-step problem requires finding a fraction of a remaining quantity. First, we determine what fraction remains after the initial cut, and then we multiply that remaining fraction by the portion used for the gift, using cross-cancellation to simplify the calculation.
ANSWER: \frac{1}{3} \text{ metre}
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Two students, Amit and Sneha, are trying to solve $\frac{4}{9} \times \frac{3}{8}$. Amit multiplies straight across to get $\frac{12}{72}$ and then simplifies. Sneha simplifies the fractions diagonally before multiplying, cancelling 4 with 8 and 3 with 9, to get $\frac{1}{3} \times \frac{1}{2}$. Evaluate both methods. Which method is more efficient and why?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying mathematical methods
STEPS:
- step 1: Analyze Amit's method: multiplying first ($4 \times 3 = 12$, $9 \times 8 = 72$) gives $\frac{12}{72}$, which simplifies to $\frac{1}{6}$ by dividing by 12.
- step 2: Analyze Sneha's method: cross-canceling common factors ($\frac{4}{8} = \frac{1}{2}$ and $\frac{3}{9} = \frac{1}{3}$) gives $\frac{1}{3} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{6}$.
- step 3: Compare the complexity of numbers involved in both procedures.
- step 4: Conclude which method is superior for handling larger numbers.
ANSWER: Both methods yield the correct answer of $\frac{1}{6}$. However, Sneha's method is more efficient because cross-canceling reduces numbers before multiplication, preventing large products like 72 that are harder to simplify later.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A milk dairy has a container with $12\frac{1}{2}$ litres of milk. $\frac{2}{5}$ of this milk is packed into small packets. Then, $\frac{3}{4}$ of the remaining milk is used to make paneer. Calculate the exact volume of milk left over after making paneer.
COMPETENCY: Translating multi-step real-world situations into fraction operations
STEPS:
- step 1: Convert initial mixed number to improper fraction: $12\frac{1}{2} = \frac{25}{2}$ litres.
- step 2: Calculate milk packed into small packets ($\frac{2}{5}$ of $\frac{25}{2}$): $\frac{2}{5} \times \frac{25}{2} = \frac{50}{10} = 5$ litres.
- step 3: Determine the milk remaining after packing: $\frac{25}{2} - 5 = \frac{25}{2} - \frac{10}{2} = \frac{15}{2}$ litres.
- step 4: Calculate milk used for paneer ($\frac{3}{4}$ of the remaining $\frac{15}{2}$): $\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{15}{2} = \frac{45}{8}$ litres.
- step 5: Find the final leftover milk by subtracting paneer milk from remaining milk: $\frac{15}{2} - \frac{45}{8} = \frac{60}{8} - \frac{45}{8} = \frac{15}{8}$ litres ($1\frac{7}{8}$ litres).
ANSWER: The exact volume of milk left over is $\frac{15}{8}$ litres or $1\frac{7}{8}$ litres.
DIFFICULTY: hard
