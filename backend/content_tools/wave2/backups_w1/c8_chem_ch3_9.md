## ELI5
Scoop white salt crystals from the jar and you would never guess they hide a partnership: sodium, a reactive metal, joined to chlorine. A compound is exactly that — two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed proportion by mass; water joins hydrogen and oxygen in the same fixed ratio every single time. Unlike a mixture, the partners lose their old identities and gain brand-new properties, and the recipe never changes. Fixed members, fixed ratio, fixed formula.

## DEFINITION
A compound is a pure substance composed of two or more different elements chemically combined in a fixed proportion by mass. For example, water ($H_2O$) is a compound formed when hydrogen and oxygen combine chemically in a fixed mass ratio of $1:8$.

## MASTERY_CRITERIA

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY

## WALKTHROUGH
When you dissolve table salt into a glass of drinking water in your kitchen, you are mixing two different chemical substances. If you taste the water, it tastes salty throughout because the salt is uniformly distributed. However, salt itself is made of sodium and chlorine, which are entirely different from the white crystals you scoop from the jar. Building on your prior knowledge of pure substances and elements from Class 7, we now look at how different elements bond together to form entirely new substances with unique properties. Unlike mixtures where components keep their original traits, compounds possess fixed formulas and distinct properties. We will explore how water, sodium chloride, and sugar exemplify this chemical combination.

Takeaway: a compound is chemistry's fixed-ratio recipe — hydrogen and oxygen lock at one-to-eight by mass into water, a new substance owning none of its parents' properties. Exam format: expect constituent elements named for common compounds, and element-versus-compound reasoning. Verification beat: give the ratio and the new property — water's wetness belongs to neither hydrogen nor oxygen. Why students miss it: they treat compounds as carrying cases — combination replaces the ingredients' properties with the product's own, and the fixed ratio is the recipe's law. The revision frame is the recipe: fixed ratio, chemical marriage, brand-new properties — miss any of the three and the substance is only a mixture wearing confidence.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The constituent elements are hydrogen and oxygen. A wrong answer shows that the student is confusing chemical compounds with physical mixtures or elements, often mistaking the name "water" for a single fundamental element rather than a combination of two gases.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why is table salt (sodium chloride, $NaCl$) classified as a compound rather than a mixture of sodium and chlorine?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Table salt is a compound because its components, sodium and chlorine, are chemically combined in a fixed ratio, and it possesses entirely different properties from shiny sodium metal and poisonous chlorine gas. A wrong answer indicates that the student thinks components in compounds keep their individual physical properties like they do in mixtures.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A student burns $12\text{ g}$ of carbon completely in oxygen to form $44\text{ g}$ of carbon dioxide ($CO_2$). Using the law of fixed proportions, how much oxygen gas reacted with the carbon?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Identify the total mass of the compound formed ($44\text{ g}$ of $CO_2$).
Step 2: Identify the mass of the starting carbon element ($12\text{ g}$).
Step 3: Apply the conservation of mass / fixed proportion principle: Mass of oxygen = Total mass of compound - Mass of carbon.
Step 4: Calculate: $44\text{ g} - 12\text{ g} = 32\text{ g}$ of oxygen.
A wrong answer reveals that the student is adding the numbers together or does not understand that compounds have mass made strictly from their fixed constituent elements.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Analyze the chemical formulas for water ($H_2O$), carbon dioxide ($CO_2$), and table salt ($NaCl$). What common feature do all three share regarding their elemental composition?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
All three are compounds consisting of two or more different elements combined in a fixed, definite atomic ratio. A wrong answer suggests the student views chemical formulas as arbitrary letters rather than precise representations of fixed elemental ratios in compounds.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate the statement: "Dissolving sugar in water creates a new compound because the sugar disappears and the water becomes sweet." Is this scientifically correct?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is incorrect because dissolving sugar in water forms a physical mixture (solution), not a new chemical compound; the sugar molecules retain their chemical identity and can be recovered by evaporating the water. A wrong answer shows confusion between physical dissolution (mixtures) and chemical combination (compounds).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple home-based reasoning test to prove that table sugar ($C_{12}H_{22}O_{11}$) is a compound and not an element. Describe what you would observe.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good answer would suggest heating white sugar strongly in a test tube; it decomposes into black carbon residue and water droplets on the container walls, proving it breaks down into multiple simpler substances. A poor answer relies merely on appearance or fails to show how compounds decompose into constituent elements.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think that because a compound contains certain elements, it will exhibit the properties of those elements. For example, they believe water should burn because it contains hydrogen (a flammable gas) and support combustion because it contains oxygen.
## ON_FAILURE
Remind the student that chemical combination creates an entirely new substance with completely new properties. Demonstrate or explain how sodium (a reactive metal) and chlorine (a toxic gas) react to form harmless, edible salt ($NaCl$), proving that properties change completely during compound formation.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Pure water ($H_2O$) is decomposed in a laboratory experiment by electrolysis into hydrogen gas ($H_2$) and oxygen gas ($O_2$). If $18\text{ g}$ of water produces $2\text{ g}$ of hydrogen gas, calculate the mass of oxygen gas produced and state the fixed mass ratio of hydrogen to oxygen in water.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify total mass of the compound starting the reaction ($18\text{ g}$ of $H_2O$).
- Step 2: Identify the mass of one product obtained from decomposition ($2\text{ g}$ of $H_2$).
- Step 3: Apply the Law of Conservation of Mass to find the missing product mass: $\text{Mass of } O_2 = \text{Total mass of } H_2O - \text{Mass of } H_2$.
- Step 4: Calculate mass of oxygen: $18\text{ g} - 2\text{ g} = 16\text{ g}$ of $O_2$.
- Step 5: Express the mass ratio of hydrogen to oxygen: $2\text{ g} : 16\text{ g}$, which simplifies to $1:8$.
REASONING: Compounds always decompose into their constituent elements in a fixed mass ratio. By applying mass conservation during decomposition, we find the exact quantity of each element.
ANSWER: $16\text{ g}$ of oxygen gas; mass ratio of hydrogen to oxygen is $1:8$.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Carbon dioxide ($CO_2$) is a compound formed by carbon and oxygen in a fixed mass ratio of $3:8$. If an Indian chemistry student reacts carbon completely with $64\text{ g}$ of oxygen, what mass of carbon dioxide is formed?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the given mass ratio of carbon to oxygen in carbon dioxide ($3:8$).
- Step 2: Identify the given mass of oxygen reacting ($64\text{ g}$).
- Step 3: Set up a proportion where $8\text{ parts} = 64\text{ g}$, so $1\text{ part} = 64 / 8 = 8\text{ g}$.
- Step 4: Calculate the mass of carbon required using its parts ($3 \text{ parts} \times 8\text{ g/part} = 24\text{ g}$).
- Step 5: Add mass of carbon and oxygen to find total compound mass: $24\text{ g} + 64\text{ g} = 88\text{ g}$ of $CO_2$.
REASONING: The fixed proportion rule dictates that elements combine in constant mass ratios. Finding the value of a single ratio part allows us to calculate any unknown reactant or product mass.
ANSWER: $88\text{ g}$ of carbon dioxide formed.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A sample of common kitchen table salt ($NaCl$) contains $23\text{ g}$ of sodium and $35.5\text{ g}$ of chlorine. If a food processing factory uses $460\text{ g}$ of sodium to manufacture sodium chloride, what total mass of salt is produced, and how much chlorine is required?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Determine the ratio of sodium to chlorine in the compound from initial data ($23\text{ g} Na : 35.5\text{ g} Cl$).
- Step 2: Calculate the scaling factor for sodium used in the factory: $460\text{ g} / 23\text{ g} = 20$.
- Step 3: Multiply the mass of chlorine by the same scaling factor to find total chlorine required: $35.5\text{ g} \times 20 = 710\text{ g}$ of $Cl$.
- Step 4: Sum the mass of sodium and chlorine to find total salt produced: $460\text{ g} + 710\text{ g} = 1170\text{ g}$.
- Step 5: Verify the result by checking the mass percentage or component ratios.
REASONING: Compounds have invariant elemental compositions. Scaling up one element requires scaling the other element by the exact same multiplier to maintain the compound's fixed formula.
ANSWER: $1170\text{ g}$ of sodium chloride produced, requiring $710\text{ g}$ of chlorine.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Rahul prepares a mixture by stirring iron filings and sulfur powder together in a dish. Priya heats iron filings and sulfur powder strongly in a test tube until a black solid mass (iron sulfide) is formed. Contrast the properties of Rahul's mixture with Priya's compound regarding the ease of separating iron from the product using a magnet.
COMPETENCY: Distinguishing chemical compounds from physical mixtures based on property changes and component separation
STEPS:
- Step 1: Analyze Rahul's sample as a physical mixture where iron and sulfur retain individual properties.
- Step 2: Analyze Priya's sample as a chemical compound (iron sulfide) where elements are chemically bonded.
- Step 3: Apply magnetic property knowledge: Rahul's iron can be pulled out with a magnet, but Priya's iron is chemically bound in the compound and cannot be separated magnetically.
- Step 4: Conclude that compounds lose constituent properties while mixtures retain them.
ANSWER: In Rahul's mixture, iron can easily be separated using a magnet because the iron and sulfur are only physically mixed. In Priya's compound (iron sulfide), iron has chemically reacted and bonded with sulfur, so a magnet cannot pull it out since it no longer exists as free elemental iron.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A chemistry student tests three unlabeled liquid samples in a laboratory: Sample A is distilled water ($H_2O$), Sample B is a sugar solution, and Sample C is tap water containing dissolved minerals. Classify each sample as either an element, a compound, or a mixture, providing scientific justification for your classification based on composition and separation criteria.
COMPETENCY: Classifying matter into elements, compounds, and mixtures using compositional reasoning
STEPS:
- Step 1: Examine Sample A ($H_2O$): consists of two elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio, making it a chemical compound.
- Step 2: Examine Sample B (sugar solution): consists of sugar dissolved in water without a fixed chemical bond, making it a homogeneous mixture.
- Step 3: Examine Sample C (tap water): contains water plus multiple dissolved minerals in variable proportions, making it a mixture.
- Step 4: Summarize the distinct criteria separating compounds (fixed chemical bonds) from mixtures (variable physical combinations).
ANSWER: Sample A is a compound because it has a fixed chemical formula ($H_2O$) and constant composition. Sample B is a mixture because sugar and water are physically combined and can be separated by evaporation. Sample C is also a mixture because it contains water and various dissolved salts/minerals in variable amounts rather than being a single pure chemical compound.
DIFFICULTY: hard
