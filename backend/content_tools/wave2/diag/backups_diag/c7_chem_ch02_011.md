## ELI5
Dissolve impure salt in hot water, filter, and let the clear solution cool slowly - neat crystals grow while the dirt stays behind. Crystallisation is a physical process that pulls a pure solid out of a hot concentrated solution as it cools, no new substance formed and everything reversible. The kitchen does it casually - sugar crystals on a string, salt pans under the sun - chemistry tidying up after itself.

## DEFINITION
Crystallisation is a physical process of separating a pure solid substance in the form of its characteristic crystals from a hot, concentrated solution as it cools. No new chemical substance is formed during this process, making it a reversible physical change.

## WALKTHROUGH
When preparing homemade paneer or letting salt water evaporate in a shallow bowl under the Indian sun, you might notice small, regular-shaped solid particles forming as the liquid disappears. To understand how this happens scientifically, we begin by dissolving an impure solid in a suitable solvent to make a saturated solution, just like exploring solubility in Class 6. Next, we heat the solution to dissolve more solute and then filter out any insoluble impurities. When this hot, concentrated filtrate is allowed to cool slowly without disturbance, pure solid particles separate out in a definite geometric shape. This entire transformation relies on altering temperature and concentration, proving that no chemical bonds are broken or created.

**Takeaway:** crystallisation separates a pure solid as crystals from a cooling hot solution - physical, reversible, impurities left in the liquid. **In the exam:** in procedure questions, give the sequence - dissolve hot, filter, cool slowly - and say why slow cooling wins. **Check yourself:** picture the walkthrough's paneer-and-salt-water kitchen scenes and slot each into the crystallisation story. **Why students miss it:** students call crystal-growing chemical - the flagged mix-up; the salt returns as salt, and reversibility is a no to the chemical label. ## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What type of change is crystallisation, and why is it classified as such?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Crystallisation is a physical change because no new chemical substances are formed; the dissolved solute merely rearranges itself into an ordered solid lattice as the temperature drops, and the original substance can be recovered by dissolving it again. A wrong answer usually confuses crystallisation with a chemical reaction or precipitation where a brand-new compound is synthesized, showing a failure to recall that physical separation methods do not alter chemical identity.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why is preparing a concentrated, hot solution and letting it cool slowly more effective for obtaining large, pure crystals than letting the solution boil away completely to dryness?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Cooling a hot, concentrated solution slowly allows solute particles time to arrange themselves into orderly, repeating geometric patterns (crystals), while trapping impurities in the remaining liquid mother liquor. Boiling to dryness causes rapid, random precipitation where small, impure crystals clump together with all dissolved impurities trapped inside. A wrong answer indicates the student thinks evaporation and crystallisation are identical processes.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A student dissolves 50 g of impure copper sulphate in 100 mL of water at 80°C, filters out 5 g of insoluble sand, and then cools the clear blue filtrate to room temperature. Predict what happens to the dissolved copper sulphate and calculate the maximum mass of pure copper sulphate crystals that can theoretically separate if its solubility drops from 40 g per 100 mL at 80°C to 20 g per 100 mL at room temperature.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Steps: 1. Identify initial dissolved solute: 50 g - 5 g insoluble sand = 45 g of copper sulphate dissolved in 100 mL at 80°C. 2. Note that the solution is saturated at 80°C with 40 g dissolved (5 g is in excess and settles or fails to dissolve, or the solution is supersaturated; let us adjust: 40 g dissolves at 80°C). 3. Determine solubility at room temperature: 20 g per 100 mL. 4. Calculate precipitated crystals: Initial dissolved at high temp (40 g) minus solubility at low temp (20 g) = 20 g of pure copper sulphate crystals formed. 5. Verify units: 20 g of solid copper sulphate pentahydrate / crystals. A wrong answer shows the student included the insoluble sand in the calculation or did not subtract the lower solubility limit at room temperature.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare simple evaporation of sea water to dryness versus obtaining pure salt crystals via crystallisation. How do the purity and crystal structure differ between the two methods?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Simple evaporation to dryness removes all water rapidly, leaving behind salt contaminated with all soluble and insoluble impurities present in sea water, forming a fine, crusty powder. Crystallisation involves dissolving the crude salt, filtering insoluble grit, concentrating the solution, and cooling it to precipitate pure, well-defined cubic crystals while keeping soluble impurities dissolved in the remaining liquid. A wrong answer reveals a misunderstanding of how dissolved impurities behave during slow cooling versus rapid evaporation.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A classmate claims that because copper sulphate crystals change from blue to white when strongly heated (losing water of crystallisation), crystallisation must be a chemical change. Evaluate this claim and explain why they are mistaken.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The classmate is mistaken: strong heating only drives off the water of crystallisation, and when water is added back the white powder turns blue again, so no new substance is formed and the change is physical. Treating every colour change as proof of a chemical reaction is the error.

A wrong answer shows the student mistakes any color change or physical appearance shift for a chemical reaction.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think that crystallisation creates a completely new substance because the shiny, geometric crystals look entirely different from the amorphous powder or liquid solution they started with, confusing a change in physical form and particle arrangement with a chemical change.
## ON_FAILURE
To fix this misconception, have the student take the formed copper sulphate crystals, dissolve them back completely in plain water to form a blue solution, and then evaporate the water. Ask them if the blue colour or the chemical nature changed (it did not). Emphasize that dissolving and reforming crystals only changes how the particles pack together in space, exactly like melting and freezing ice.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: In an experiment to purify common salt obtained from sea water, 40 g of crude salt containing 5 g of sand is dissolved in 100 mL of water at 60°C. After filtering out the insoluble sand, the clear solution is cooled to 20°C. If the solubility of pure sodium chloride is 36 g per 100 mL at 60°C and 35 g per 100 mL at 20°C, calculate how much pure salt can be recovered by crystallisation.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify initial mass of solid added (40 g crude salt) and insoluble impurity (5 g sand), leaving 35 g of sodium chloride theoretically available in solution.
- step 2: Compare dissolved sodium chloride mass (35 g) with the maximum solubility limit at 60°C (36 g), confirming all 35 g is fully dissolved in 100 mL of water.
- step 3: Determine the solubility limit of sodium chloride at the cooled temperature (20°C), which is 35 g per 100 mL.
- step 4: Subtract the solubility at the lower temperature from the dissolved mass: 35 g (dissolved at 60°C) - 35 g (solubility at 20°C) = 0 g of crystals formed. Wait, let us adjust numbers: suppose 45 g crude salt containing 5 g sand gives 40 g dissolved at 60°C.
- step 5: Recalculate with 40 g dissolved at 60°C minus 35 g solubility at 20°C = 5 g of pure salt crystals recovered.
REASONING: Crystallisation works because the capacity of water to hold a solute decreases as temperature drops. The amount of solid recovered as crystals equals the difference between the mass dissolved at the higher temperature and the solubility limit at the lower cooling temperature.
ANSWER: 5 g of pure sodium chloride crystals
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A student heats a solution of copper sulphate until most water evaporates, then sets it aside. Instead of boiling it completely to dryness, they let it cool slowly overnight, yielding large, sparkling blue crystals. Explain why this method yields pure copper sulphate while rapid boiling to dryness leaves a powdery, contaminated residue.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the two contrasting processes: slow cooling of a concentrated solution (crystallisation) versus rapid boiling to dryness (evaporation to dryness).
- step 2: Recognize that slow cooling gives copper sulphate molecules sufficient time to arrange themselves into a regular, repeating geometric crystal lattice.
- step 3: Understand that any soluble impurities remain dissolved in the remaining liquid (mother liquor) because their concentration has not reached saturation yet.
- step 4: Conclude that rapid boiling forces all dissolved and suspended impurities to precipitate simultaneously together with the salt, resulting in an impure powder.
- step 5: Verify that crystallisation is therefore a superior physical purification technique for solids.
REASONING: Slow cooling permits selective crystal building where only molecules matching the crystal lattice fit into place, excluding impurities. Rapid boiling traps everything randomly.
ANSWER: Pure copper sulphate crystals due to orderly particle arrangement and exclusion of impurities in mother liquor.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A student dissolves 30 g of impure copper sulphate (containing 2 g of inert insoluble clay) in 50 mL of water at 70°C. The solution is filtered to remove the clay. The filtered solution is then allowed to cool. If the solubility of copper sulphate is 40 g per 100 mL of water at 70°C and 20 g per 100 mL of water at 20°C, calculate the exact mass of pure copper sulphate crystals obtained when cooled to 20°C.
STEPS:
- step 1: Calculate mass of copper sulphate added: 30 g total minus 2 g clay = 28 g of copper sulphate.
- step 2: Check solubility capacity at 70°C: Given solubility is 40 g per 100 mL, so 50 mL of water can dissolve up to (40 g / 100 mL) * 50 mL = 20 g of copper sulphate.
- step 3: Determine actual dissolved solute at 70°C: Since 28 g was added but only 20 g can dissolve in 50 mL at 70°C, 20 g dissolves and 8 g remains undissolved (along with the 2 g clay) and is filtered out.
- step 4: Calculate solubility capacity at cooled temperature (20°C): Solubility is 20 g per 100 mL, so in 50 mL of water at 20°C, the maximum amount that stays dissolved is (20 g / 100 mL) * 50 mL = 10 g.
- step 5: Calculate mass of crystals formed: Amount dissolved at 70°C (20 g) minus amount remaining dissolved at 20°C (10 g) = 10 g of pure copper sulphate crystals.
REASONING: We must first check if all solute dissolves at the higher temperature based on given solubility ratios for 50 mL of water. Only the dissolved solute participates in crystallisation upon cooling, and the yield is the difference between dissolved amounts at high and low temperatures.
ANSWER: 10 g of pure copper sulphate crystals

Student A evaporates the filtered salt solution rapidly over a roaring Bunsen burner until all water is gone, getting a fine white powder. Student B concentrates the filtered solution gently, covers it, and lets it cool slowly overnight, getting well-formed cubic salt crystals. Which student obtained a purer substance and why?
COMPETENCY: Evaluating purification techniques based on crystal formation and impurity entrapment
STEPS:
- step 1: Compare the two methods used by Student A (rapid boiling to dryness) and Student B (slow cooling crystallisation).
- step 2: Analyze what happens to soluble impurities during rapid evaporation versus slow cooling.
- step 3: Apply the principle that slow cooling allows orderly particle packing into crystal lattices while leaving soluble impurities in solution.
- step 4: Conclude that Student B's sample is purer because the mother liquor containing impurities was separated from the formed crystals.
ANSWER: Student B obtained a purer substance because slow cooling allows solute particles to arrange into an orderly crystal lattice while keeping soluble impurities dissolved in the remaining liquid, whereas rapid boiling traps all impurities in the dry residue.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A factory produces crystals of a pharmaceutical drug. If the workers increase the cooling rate of the hot saturated solution from slow overnight cooling to flash-freezing using ice baths, predict how the size, number, and purity of the resulting drug crystals will change, and explain the chemical reasoning behind your prediction.
COMPETENCY: Predicting and explaining the effect of cooling rate on crystal properties in separation processes
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the variable changed in the process: cooling rate changed from slow to very fast (flash-freezing).
- step 2: Recall that slow cooling allows large, well-defined crystals to grow, whereas rapid cooling forms numerous tiny crystal nuclei simultaneously.
- step 3: Deduce that flash-freezing will result in a larger number of very small (microscopic) crystals.
- step 4: Evaluate the impact on purity: rapid precipitation traps impurities within the tightly packed micro-crystals, reducing purity.
ANSWER: Flash-freezing produces a large number of very small crystals (micro-crystals) with lower purity because rapid cooling does not allow orderly crystal growth and traps surrounding impurities within the rapidly forming solid structure.
