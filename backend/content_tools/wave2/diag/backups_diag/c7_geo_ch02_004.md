## ELI5
Coastal Chennai on a summer afternoon: clothes cling, sweat refuses to dry. The villain is humidity - invisible water vapour already filling the air. Humid air is a nearly full sponge: it cannot accept your shirt's extra moisture, so evaporation stalls and the body's cooling system fails. Dry Rajasthan air, by contrast, drinks sweat eagerly. Humidity is invisible, but your damp collar is its signature.

## DEFINITION
Humidity is the amount of invisible water vapour present in the air at any given time and place. It plays a critical role in shaping local weather conditions, influencing how hot or sticky we feel, and determining whether clouds, fog, or rainfall will form.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think that high humidity means it is about to rain immediately, or they confuse the temperature of the air with the amount of moisture it holds. For example, they might believe that a hot desert has high humidity because it is hot, failing to realize that deserts have very low moisture despite high temperatures.
## ON_FAILURE
To fix this, guide the student to compare two contrasting locations: a coastal city (like Kochi) and a desert region (like Jaisalmer). Use the concept that air acts like a sponge; coastal air is a wet sponge holding lots of water, while desert air is a dry sponge ready to absorb moisture, regardless of how hot both places might be.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: During a humid monsoon day in Kolkata, a weather station measures that 1 cubic metre of air contains 20 grams of water vapour. If the maximum capacity of air to hold water vapour at the current temperature of 25°C is 25 grams, calculate the relative humidity.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the current water vapour content in the air (20 g/m³).
- Step 2: Identify the maximum water vapour capacity at 25°C (25 g/m³).
- Step 3: Set up the formula for relative humidity: (Current Content / Maximum Capacity) × 100.
- Step 4: Substitute the values: (20 / 25) × 100 = 0.8 × 100 = 80%.
- Step 5: Interpret the result: A relative humidity of 80% means the air is holding 80% of the total moisture it can possibly hold at 25°C, indicating very muggy and humid weather.
REASONING: Relative humidity tells us how close the air is to saturation. By dividing the actual moisture by the maximum possible moisture at that specific temperature, we find the exact saturation percentage.
ANSWER: 80% relative humidity.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: In New Delhi during a dry summer day at 40°C, the maximum capacity of air is 50 grams of water vapour per cubic metre, but it only contains 10 grams. Calculate the relative humidity and explain what it means for human comfort.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify current water vapour content (10 g/m³).
- Step 2: Identify maximum capacity at 40°C (50 g/m³).
- Step 3: Apply the relative humidity formula: (10 / 50) × 100.
- Step 4: Compute the calculation: 0.2 × 100 = 20%.
- Step 5: Interpret the outcome: A low relative humidity of 20% means sweat evaporates very quickly from the skin, cooling the body efficiently, though the air feels very dry.
REASONING: Higher temperatures increase air's capacity to hold moisture. When actual moisture is low compared to this high capacity, relative humidity drops significantly.
ANSWER: 20% relative humidity, indicating dry air where sweat evaporates rapidly.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Two cities have the same amount of water vapour in the air (15 g/m³). City A has a temperature of 15°C (maximum capacity 15 g/m³), while City B has a temperature of 35°C (maximum capacity 40 g/m³). Calculate the relative humidity for both cities and determine which city is closer to precipitation.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate City A relative humidity: (15 / 15) × 100 = 100%.
- Step 2: Calculate City B relative humidity: (15 / 40) × 100 = 37.5%.
- Step 3: Compare the two percentages: City A is at 100%, City B is at 37.5%.
- Step 4: Analyze temperature impact: City A's air is fully saturated because its temperature is lower, reducing its capacity.
- Step 5: Conclude which city is closer to precipitation: City A is at 100% saturation and is prone to fog, dew, or rain, whereas City B has dry-feeling air despite having the same absolute moisture amount.
REASONING: Temperature directly dictates capacity. Even with the same amount of water vapour, cooler air saturates much faster than warm air, making temperature a key factor in humidity and weather changes.
ANSWER: City A has 100% relative humidity (saturated); City B has 37.5%. City A is much closer to precipitation.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: If a mountain breeze forces warm, humid air up the windward slope of the Western Ghats, causing it to cool down as it rises, how will its relative humidity change, and what weather event will result?
COMPETENCY: Analyzing physical geography processes, adiabatic cooling, and weather formation.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Trace the movement of warm, humid air moving up a mountain slope (windward side of Western Ghats).
- Step 2: Recall that as air rises to higher altitudes, temperature decreases (cooling).
- Step 3: Apply the rule of temperature and capacity: colder air holds less water vapour.
- Step 4: Deduce that as capacity decreases while moisture remains constant, relative humidity increases until it hits 100% (saturation).
- Step 5: Conclude that condensation occurs, forming clouds and heavy orographic rainfall on the windward slopes.
ANSWER: As the air rises and cools, its capacity to hold water vapour decreases, causing relative humidity to increase rapidly to 100%. Once saturated, the excess water vapour condenses to form clouds and heavy orographic rainfall on the windward side of the Ghats.
DIFFICULTY: hard
