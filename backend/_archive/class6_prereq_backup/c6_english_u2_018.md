## DEFINITION
Tenses indicate the time of an action: the Present Tense describes actions happening now or general facts, while the Past Tense describes actions that finished at a specific time before now.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can identify the tense of a sentence and transform a simple present action into a past action accurately using standard verb forms.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers simple present and simple past tense forms. It excludes future tense, continuous (progressive) tenses, and perfect tenses.
## WALKTHROUGH
Think about your day: you 'eat' breakfast every morning (Present), but yesterday you 'ate' a special meal (Past). To begin, ask yourself, "Is this happening right now as I speak?" If yes, use the present form. If the event is over and done with, use the past form. Look at the verb—the action word—to see if it has changed to show time. The key insight is that time is like a timeline, and our verbs must change their shape to show where on that timeline the action sits.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the main difference between an action in the present tense and an action in the past tense?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The present tense describes things happening now or regularly, while the past tense describes things that already happened. A wrong answer suggests the student hasn't linked verb forms to the concept of time, requiring a review of basic timelines.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If I say "The bird flies," how can I change this to show it happened yesterday?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The answer is "The bird flew." A wrong answer indicates the student is struggling with irregular verbs, which do not always follow the simple -ed pattern.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Change the verb in brackets to the correct past tense: "The mahout (talk) to the elephant."
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The answer is "talked." Step 1: Identify the action (talk). Step 2: Determine the time (past). Step 3: Add the suffix -ed. A wrong answer reveals the student might be confused about when to use -ed versus irregular forms.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Analyze this sentence: "The dog wags his tail." Is this a story event or a general statement? How do you know?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
It is a general statement because the present tense "wags" is used to describe a habit, not a specific finished event. If the student thinks it is a story event, they are ignoring the clue provided by the present tense verb.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
"Jahnavi sat by the river." Can we change this to present tense to describe a habit? How?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, it becomes "Jahnavi sits by the river." This shows we are shifting from a specific past memory to a general, repeatable habit. A wrong answer suggests a lack of understanding regarding how verb forms dictate the "state" of the action (habit vs. event).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Write a short sentence about a friend in the present, then turn it into a past tense sentence.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Example: "My friend helps me." (Present) / "My friend helped me." (Past). A poor answer fails to change the verb form or uses incorrect tense markers, showing they haven't internalized the rule of verb modification.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often add "-ed" to every verb to make it past tense, regardless of whether it is regular or irregular (e.g., saying "eated" instead of "ate"). They do this because they learn the "-ed" rule first and try to apply it as a universal shortcut.
## ON_FAILURE
First, show the student a list of "Action Changers" (irregular verbs) versus "Add-on verbs" (-ed). Have them practice sorting verbs into "Follows the Rule" and "Breaks the Rule." Then, ask them to read their sentence aloud to hear if it sounds natural, helping them distinguish between correct and incorrect forms like "ate" vs "eated."

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Change "The dog plays in the garden" to past tense.
STEPS:
- step 1: Find the verb (plays).
- step 2: Find the base form (play).
- step 3: Check if it is a regular verb (Yes, add -ed).
- step 4: Construct the sentence (The dog played in the garden).
REASONING: Following the step-by-step identification ensures the student doesn't change the wrong word (like the noun).
ANSWER: The dog played in the garden.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Identify the tense in "The farmer tossed a rope."
STEPS:
- step 1: Locate the verb (tossed).
- step 2: Check for markers (-ed).
- step 3: Determine time (The action is finished).
- step 4: Label as Past Tense.
REASONING: By checking the verb suffix, the student can definitively prove the tense is past.
ANSWER: Past Tense.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Rewrite the following to reflect a past event: "The elephant splashes water on his friend."
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the main verb (splashes).
- step 2: Find the base form (splash).
- step 3: Identify if it is regular or irregular (Regular, add -ed).
- step 4: Apply the past form (splashed).
- step 5: Rewrite the full sentence.
REASONING: This requires mapping the present-tense habit to a specific past occurrence.
ANSWER: The elephant splashed water on his friend.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If a story uses "The elephant lived in a stable," why is "lived" used instead of "lives"?
COMPETENCY: Conceptual Understanding
STEPS:
- step 1: Analyze the context (a story).
- step 2: Recognize that stories describe completed events.
- step 3: Link the past tense form to the nature of storytelling.
ANSWER: It is used because the story describes events that have already happened; therefore, the past tense "lived" is required to show the time of the action.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Compare "I walk to school" and "I walked to school." How does the meaning change for the listener?
COMPETENCY: Analytical Thinking
STEPS:
- step 1: Define the first sentence (a habit/fact).
- step 2: Define the second sentence (an event that happened).
- step 3: Compare the difference in timing.
- step 4: Formulate the impact on the listener.
- step 5: Conclude the result.
ANSWER: The first sentence tells the listener about a daily habit (what happens generally), while the second tells the listener about a specific, completed experience (what happened at one point in time).
