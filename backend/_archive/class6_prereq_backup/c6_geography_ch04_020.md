## DEFINITION
The beginning of farming in India refers to the period around 7000 BCE when early human societies in the northwest region transitioned from food-gathering to cultivating crops like wheat and barley and domesticating animals. This shift allowed people to settle in one place, leading to the development of early villages and eventually, the sophisticated Indus-Sarasvatī civilisation.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify the key geographical regions (northwest/Indus plains and the Ganga) where early agriculture began and list the specific crops associated with these areas. They should also be able to explain how the transition to farming enabled the growth of the first urban settlements.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the transition from basic food production to organized agriculture in the Indian subcontinent. It does not cover the specific archaeological techniques used to date these findings, nor does it discuss genetic crop evolution or pre-agricultural hunter-gatherer tools.
## WALKTHROUGH
Think about your favorite meal—where do you think the rice or wheat on your plate actually comes from? Long ago, people didn't have supermarkets; they had to learn that seeds could be planted to grow food near water sources. We begin by looking at the northwest region, where the Indus River provided water for the very first farmers to grow wheat and barley. Just as you need a steady supply of water to keep a home garden alive, these early farmers settled near rivers like the Sarasvatī and the Ganga to ensure their crops would survive. The key insight is that once people could grow enough food to store, they stopped wandering and started building the first stable cities.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Around what year did agriculture begin in the northwest region of the Indian subcontinent?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Agriculture began around 7000 BCE. If a student says "2600 BCE," they are confusing the start of farming with the height of the Indus Valley Civilisation; this reveals they aren't distinguishing between the initial transition to farming and the later urban period.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why did early farmers prefer to settle near rivers like the Indus, Sarasvatī, and Ganga?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
They settled there because the rivers provided fertile soil and a consistent water supply for their crops and livestock. A wrong answer like "to use boats for travel" shows they missed the fundamental link between water, soil, and the survival of agricultural crops.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If you were a farmer in the northwest region around 6000 BCE, what animals would you likely have kept?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
You would have kept cattle, sheep, and goats. Steps: 1. Identify region (Northwest). 2. Recall historical evidence for that region. 3. List the domesticated species mentioned in history. If a student lists "horses" or "chickens," they are applying modern knowledge rather than historical evidence.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
How did the discovery of rice cultivation in the Ganga region differ from the early wheat farming in the northwest?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
It happened later, around 5000 BCE, and occurred in a different geographical region, showing that agriculture developed in stages across the subcontinent. A student who misses the timeline difference fails to see that farming was not a single event but a spread of knowledge over time.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Could a civilization exist if its agriculture was not "productive" enough to feed cities?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, because cities require a large, stable food supply for people who are not farmers themselves (like craftspeople and administrators). If a student says "yes," they misunderstand the dependency between rural food production and urban survival.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Imagine you are an early inhabitant. How would you explain to a friend why we are moving from moving from place to place to living in one village?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The answer should focus on the stability of having a food source (crops) that stays in one place. A poor answer ignores the role of agriculture and suggests reasons like "better weather," which ignores the human effort of farming.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think agriculture appeared everywhere in India at the exact same time. They assume that because they learn about the "Indus Civilisation" first, everyone was farming by 2600 BCE, which ignores the thousands of years of earlier development that started in the northwest.
## ON_FAILURE
1. Ask the student to look at a timeline and point to 7000 BCE vs 2600 BCE. 2. Explain that the 7000 BCE date is the "seed" (the beginning of farming) while the 2600 BCE date is the "tree" (the full-grown city civilization). 3. Have them draw a simple sequence: "Farming starts in NW -> Villages grow -> Cities emerge."

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A group of early farmers in the northwest needs to choose a location for a new village. What factors should they prioritize?
STEPS:
- 1. Identify the need: Stable food and water.
- 2. Identify the resource: Proximity to a river tributary.
- 3. Identify the climate: A region with enough rainfall to support wheat/barley.
- 4. Analyze the terrain: Flat, fertile plains are best for large-scale planting.
- 5. Verify: Does this match the Indus-Sarasvatī region? Yes.
REASONING: Farming requires predictable resources; therefore, rivers are the most logical starting point for any early agricultural society.
ANSWER: They should choose a location near a river, such as the Indus or its tributaries, to ensure fertile soil and water access.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: If a city like Mohenjo-daro grew to 30,000 people, how did they solve the food problem?
STEPS:
- 1. Identify the core issue: Many people living in one place cannot grow all their own food.
- 2. Identify the solution: Creating a network of surrounding rural villages.
- 3. Apply the concept: Specialized agriculture (wheat, rice, pulses) grown in surplus.
- 4. Analyze the logistics: Transporting food from rural areas to the city center.
- 5. Interpret: Without rural farming, the city would run out of food.
REASONING: Urban life is only possible if there is a surplus of food produced outside the city walls.
ANSWER: They established a system where rural villages produced extra crops, which were then traded or transported to sustain the urban population.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Why is the "Indus Valley" name considered less accurate than the "Indus-Sarasvatī" name?
STEPS:
- 1. Recall the map: Look at the distribution of sites.
- 2. Observe: Are there many sites outside the Indus valley? Yes, many are near the Ghaggar-Hakra (Sarasvatī).
- 3. Compare: The term "Valley" implies only one river area.
- 4. Evaluate: The "Sarasvatī" name includes the massive cluster of sites in the east.
- 5. Conclude: The name reflects the full geographic range of the civilisation.
REASONING: Naming historical sites based on modern understanding prevents us from overlooking major regions where these people lived.
ANSWER: It is more accurate because archaeological evidence shows the civilization expanded far beyond the Indus river, specifically across the region of the ancient Sarasvatī river.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If the Sarasvatī river dried up, what would happen to the villages in that basin?
COMPETENCY: Cause and Effect Reasoning
STEPS:
- 1. Analyze the dependence of the village on the river.
- 2. Recognize that farming requires constant water.
- 3. Predict the loss of agriculture as the primary result.
- 4. Conclude the necessity of migration or lifestyle change.
ANSWER: The villages would no longer be able to farm effectively, forcing the inhabitants to either leave the area or revert to a lifestyle that requires less water-intensive agriculture.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Compare the life of an early farmer in 6000 BCE to a city dweller in 2600 BCE regarding their daily work.
COMPETENCY: Comparative Analysis
STEPS:
- 1. Define the early farmer's role: Tilling, planting, herding.
- 2. Define the city dweller's role: Potentially specialized (bead-making, trade, administration).
- 3. Compare the food source: Direct vs. indirect.
- 4. Analyze the social structure: Village self-sufficiency vs. city trade networks.
- 5. Synthesize: City life required complex systems that village life did not.
ANSWER: The early farmer lived a self-sufficient life focused on direct food production, while the city dweller was part of a complex, interdependent society where they likely performed specialized tasks and relied on trade for food brought from outside.
