## DEFINITION
A line of symmetry is an imaginary line that divides a 2D shape into two identical mirror halves, such that if the shape were folded along this line, both parts would overlap perfectly. It represents reflectional symmetry, where one side is a direct mirror image of the other.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify, draw, and count the lines of symmetry for various polygons and simple 2D shapes by mentally or physically visualizing a fold.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers reflectional symmetry in 2D shapes (triangles, quadrilaterals, regular polygons). It excludes 3D shapes, rotational symmetry, and point symmetry.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you have a piece of paper cut in the shape of a heart; if you fold it down the middle, the left side lands perfectly on the right. To find a line of symmetry, ask yourself: "Can I draw a line through this shape so that the two sides are exact mirror images?" Start by using a piece of paper to physically fold the shape, checking if the edges align. If they don't, that line is not a line of symmetry. Remember that some shapes have only one line, while others have many, and some have none at all.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
How many lines of symmetry does a scalene triangle have?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
A scalene triangle has 0 lines of symmetry. A wrong answer (like saying 1 or 3) reveals the student does not understand that "scalene" means all sides are of different lengths, preventing any perfect fold.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you draw a line through a rectangle that is not a square, does it always create a line of symmetry?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
No, only lines passing through the midpoints of opposite sides are lines of symmetry; diagonals in a non-square rectangle do not create identical halves. This reveals a misunderstanding where students assume any line cutting a shape in half is a "line of symmetry."
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A square has 4 lines of symmetry. Using a paper square, explain where these lines are located.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Two lines pass through the midpoints of opposite sides (horizontal and vertical), and two lines are the diagonals connecting opposite vertices. A wrong answer suggests the student hasn't visualized the folding process for the diagonals.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the lines of symmetry in an equilateral triangle and an isosceles triangle.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
An equilateral triangle has 3 lines of symmetry (one from each vertex), whereas an isosceles triangle has only 1 (from the vertex between the equal sides). Confusing these indicates a lack of connection between side length equality and symmetry.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Can a shape have a line of symmetry if it is "tilted" or rotated?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, a line of symmetry is a property of the shape's own structure, regardless of its orientation in space. A wrong answer shows the student relies on visual orientation rather than the geometric properties of the shape.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Draw a shape that has exactly two lines of symmetry, and explain why a third line cannot exist for your shape.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A rectangle (that is not a square) is a classic example. A third line (like a diagonal) would result in parts that do not overlap, meaning the symmetry property is strictly bound by the rectangle's dimensions.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that any line that divides a shape into two equal areas is a line of symmetry. For example, they might think a diagonal of a non-square rectangle is a line of symmetry because it cuts the area in half, failing to check if the two parts are mirror images.
## ON_FAILURE
Take the student back to the "folding test." Ask them to trace the shape on a piece of paper and cut it out. Have them fold it along the line they think is a symmetry line. If the edges don't match, ask them to identify where the overlap fails. This physical feedback corrects the "area-division" error.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A rectangular piece of chart paper measures 10 cm by 6 cm. How many lines of symmetry can be drawn on this paper? 
STEPS:
- Step 1: Analyze the rectangle (10 cm x 6 cm). 
- Step 2: Test a vertical line through the center of the 10 cm sides. The sides will fold perfectly.
- Step 3: Test a horizontal line through the center of the 6 cm sides. The sides will fold perfectly.
- Step 4: Test a diagonal. When folded, the corners will not overlap.
- Step 5: Conclude there are exactly 2 lines of symmetry.
REASONING: The rectangle only allows folding along the axes that maintain mirror-image alignment. Diagonals fail because the sides are unequal.
ANSWER: 2 lines of symmetry.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: An equilateral triangle has sides of 5 cm each. How many lines of symmetry can be drawn?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the shape as an equilateral triangle.
- Step 2: Draw a line from the top vertex to the midpoint of the base; it creates two congruent right triangles.
- Step 3: Repeat this for the other two vertices.
- Step 4: Count the total lines drawn.
- Step 5: Verify that all three lines create mirror images.
REASONING: Because all sides and angles are equal, every vertex acts as a starting point for a line of symmetry.
ANSWER: 3 lines of symmetry.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A sign board is in the shape of a regular hexagon. How many lines of symmetry does it have? 
STEPS:
- Step 1: Recognize a regular hexagon has 6 equal sides.
- Step 2: Identify lines passing through opposite vertices (3 lines).
- Step 3: Identify lines passing through the midpoints of opposite sides (3 lines).
- Step 4: Sum the lines: 3 + 3 = 6.
- Step 5: Confirm that folding along any of these 6 lines results in perfect overlap.
REASONING: Regular polygons have as many lines of symmetry as they have sides.
ANSWER: 6 lines of symmetry.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You have a square and a circle. If you draw a line through the center of each, which one allows more lines of symmetry? Explain.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify symmetry lines for a square (4: horizontal, vertical, 2 diagonals).
- Step 2: Identify symmetry lines for a circle (infinite, as any diameter is a line of symmetry).
- Step 3: Compare the count (finite vs. infinite).
- Step 4: Conclude that the circle has more.
ANSWER: The circle has more because any diameter passing through the center is a line of symmetry, whereas the square is limited to lines connecting specific vertices and midpoints.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A friend says that a parallelogram (that is not a rectangle or rhombus) has symmetry lines. Is this correct? Explain your answer using a drawing or description.
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Visualize/draw a non-rectangular parallelogram.
- Step 2: Attempt to draw a vertical or horizontal line; observe that the corners do not overlap.
- Step 3: Attempt to draw diagonals; observe the shapes do not match.
- Step 4: Conclude that the friend is incorrect.
ANSWER: Incorrect. A non-rectangular parallelogram has 0 lines of symmetry because the sides are slanted in a way that prevents mirror-image overlap regardless of how you fold it.
DIFFICULTY: hard
