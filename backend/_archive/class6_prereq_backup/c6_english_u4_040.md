## DEFINITION
Reading aloud and dramatisation are performance-based communication skills that use voice, pacing, and physical expression to convey the emotions and events of a story to an audience. It transforms static text into a living experience by engaging both the speaker's vocal range and their body language.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must demonstrate the ability to adjust their reading volume, speed, and tone to match the mood of a narrative passage and use one simple physical gesture or facial expression to portray a character's feeling.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers vocal expression (pace, tone, volume) and basic physical dramatisation for storytelling. It excludes full-scale theatrical productions, film acting techniques, and choral reading.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever told a friend about a scary movie you saw and noticed your voice getting lower or faster? Reading aloud and dramatisation work exactly like that. First, scan the text to identify the "mood"—is it a fast-paced game or a quiet, thoughtful moment? Next, match your breathing and voice to that mood; whisper if it’s a secret, or speak firmly if it’s a command. Finally, use your hands or your face to show what the character is feeling, rather than just saying it. By connecting your own feelings to the words on the page, you bring the story to life for your listeners.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What are the three main elements of voice that change when reading a story aloud?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The three elements are pace, volume, and expression (or tone). If a student answers only "reading fast," it reveals they are missing the importance of volume and expression in creating a mood.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If a character in a story is running in a race, how should your reading speed change compared to when they are standing still and thinking?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Your speed should increase during the race and slow down during the thinking part. A wrong answer suggests the student treats all sentences with the same "flat" rhythm, failing to see the connection between action and pace.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
When you read a line like "Prabhat felt terrible about losing," how can you use your face or body to dramatise this without adding new words?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
You could slump your shoulders and look down while reading the line to show sadness. A wrong answer (like jumping around) shows a failure to link physical movement to the specific emotion of the text.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare how you would read a sentence about someone "whispering a secret" versus "shouting for help." What changes?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
You would lower your volume and slow down for the secret, but increase volume and pitch for the shout. If a student keeps the same volume for both, they are not using "vocal variety" to signal meaning.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Why is it better to slow down during a suspenseful part of a story rather than reading through it as quickly as possible?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Slowing down builds anticipation and allows the listener to feel the tension. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks reading is only about finishing the words, not about the effect on the audience.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a short action sentence for a character who is happy about winning a game, and describe how you would say it out loud.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Example: "I finally did it!" with a bright, energetic tone and a wide smile. A poor answer would just be a sentence without any description of the performance, failing to demonstrate the "dramatisation" part of the task.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that "reading aloud" means reading every word at a constant, robotic speed without any pauses. This happens because they focus entirely on word recognition rather than understanding the character's intent. For example, they might read a line about a person being out of breath with the same steady, calm rhythm they use for a normal sentence.
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to read the sentence silently and imagine being the character in that exact situation. Ask: "If you were truly feeling [emotion], would you be talking fast or slow? Would you be loud or quiet?" Guide them to mark a "pause" or "slow down" note on their paper with a pencil to help them remember to change their rhythm.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Read the line: "He ran so fast he could not catch his breath."
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the character's physical state (exhausted/out of breath).
- step 2: Decide on the pace (fast, short, choppy breaths).
- step 3: Decide on the volume (slightly breathy and lower).
- step 4: Practice the delivery with short pauses to simulate gasping for air.
REASONING: Matching the voice to the physical reality of the character makes the performance believable.
ANSWER: Read the sentence with quick, short bursts of air between phrases, slowing down at the end to show he is stopping.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Read the line: "Surya stood smiling, not taking the match seriously."
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the mood (relaxed, confident, light-hearted).
- step 2: Choose a tone (cheerful, calm, not hurried).
- step 3: Add a physical action (a small, relaxed smile or a shrug).
- step 4: Deliver the line with a gentle, upbeat rhythm.
REASONING: A relaxed character should not sound stressed or loud; the performance must reflect their lack of worry.
ANSWER: Deliver the line with a soft, steady tone while keeping a light, relaxed posture.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Dramatise a scene where a student realizes they made a mistake and says "I am sorry."
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the emotion (regret, shame, or guilt).
- step 2: Choose the pace (slowly, as if hesitant).
- step 3: Adjust volume (quietly, not proud).
- step 4: Add body language (look down, drop shoulders).
- step 5: Combine into a performance.
REASONING: Apologies require a specific tone to sound sincere; rushing or being loud would make the apology seem fake.
ANSWER: Say "I am sorry" in a quiet, low voice while avoiding direct eye contact to show genuine regret.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You are reading a passage where a character is angry. Your friend says you sound like you are telling a joke. How do you fix your reading?
COMPETENCY: Self-correction and tone adjustment
STEPS:
- step 1: Analyze the current performance (too cheerful/light).
- step 2: Adjust the pitch (lower the voice).
- step 3: Adjust the rhythm (make sentences sharper and more clipped).
- step 4: Add a furrowed brow to convey the feeling physically.
ANSWER: I would lower my voice, speak with more force, and use sharper facial expressions to shift the tone from "happy" to "angry."
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A character has just won a game but feels guilty, so they don't celebrate. How would you perform this?
COMPETENCY: Analyzing complex/conflicting emotions
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the conflict (winning vs. feeling bad).
- step 2: Choose a tone (muted, not excited).
- step 3: Decide on pace (slower, hesitant).
- step 4: Use body language (avoid looking at the trophy/score).
- step 5: Deliver the lines without any "cheering" sounds.
ANSWER: I would read the lines in a flat, somewhat heavy tone, avoiding any excitement, while looking away to show that the character is distracted by their guilt rather than the win.
