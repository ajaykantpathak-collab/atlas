## DEFINITION
Comparing fractions is the process of determining which of two or more fractions represents a larger or smaller portion of a whole. It involves evaluating either the size of equal parts (denominator) or the number of parts collected (numerator).
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify the larger fraction by either matching denominators or reasoning about unit sizes, and justify their answer using the concept of equal shares.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the comparison of fractions with like denominators, like numerators, and unlike denominators using equivalent fractions. It excludes decimal comparison and algebraic expressions.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you have two rotis of the same size. If you share one roti equally among 3 friends and another equally among 5 friends, who gets a larger piece? To figure this out, look at the number of people sharing: the more people there are, the smaller each person's piece becomes. When denominators are different, we use the "common denominator" method—like finding a common language—to rewrite both fractions so we can directly compare the number of parts. The key insight is that fractions represent shares of a whole; if the "size" of the share (the denominator) is the same, we simply count how many shares we have.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
If two fractions have the same denominator, which one is larger: the one with the larger numerator or the smaller numerator?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The one with the larger numerator is larger. A wrong answer suggests the student confuses the numerator (how many parts we have) with the denominator (the size of each part).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why is 1/5 larger than 1/9? Explain using the idea of sharing a single item.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
1/5 is larger because when you divide one item into 5 parts, each part is bigger than when you divide the same item into 9 parts. An incorrect answer indicates the student is looking only at the numbers 5 and 9 rather than the size of the pieces created by those numbers.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Compare 2/3 and 3/4. Which is greater? Show your steps.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
3/4 is greater. Convert both to a common denominator (12): 2/3 becomes 8/12, and 3/4 becomes 9/12. Since 9 > 8, 3/4 > 2/3. A wrong answer suggests the student failed to find a common denominator or incorrectly multiplied the numerator and denominator.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
If you have 5/7 and 5/9, which is larger? Explain your reasoning without calculating a common denominator.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
5/7 is larger because both have 5 pieces, but in 5/7, each piece is a 'seventh', which is larger than a 'ninth'. A wrong answer reveals the student does not understand that as the denominator increases, the value of the unit fraction decreases.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it always true that a fraction with a bigger denominator is smaller? Give an example to support your judgment.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Only if the numerators are the same. For example, 1/10 < 1/2, but 9/10 > 1/2. A wrong answer shows the student has over-generalized the rule for unit fractions and isn't considering the numerator's effect.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create two different fractions that are both smaller than 1/2. How did you decide they were smaller?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Examples include 1/3 and 2/5. A student might use the denominator rule (since 3 > 2, 1/3 < 1/2) or equivalent fractions (2/5 = 4/10 < 5/10). A poor answer suggests the student is picking random numbers without a clear strategy.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that the fraction with the larger denominator is always the larger fraction (e.g., thinking 1/9 > 1/5 because 9 > 5). This happens because they see 9 as a "bigger number" than 5, forgetting that in fractions, the denominator represents the number of pieces the whole is cut into, meaning more pieces equals smaller pieces.
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to draw two identical rectangles on paper. Have them divide one into 5 equal strips and the other into 9 equal strips. Ask them to point to one strip from each rectangle and physically compare the size of the pieces. Explain that the denominator is the "divider"—the bigger the number of divisions, the smaller the result.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Meena has 3/4 kg of sugar and Rohan has 5/6 kg of sugar. Who has more sugar?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify denominators (4 and 6).
- step 2: Find a common denominator (the smallest multiple of 4 and 6 is 12).
- step 3: Convert 3/4: (3 × 3) / (4 × 3) = 9/12.
- step 4: Convert 5/6: (5 × 2) / (6 × 2) = 10/12.
- step 5: Compare 9/12 and 10/12.
REASONING: By converting to a common denominator, we make the "units" of measurement the same, allowing us to compare the count of those units directly.
ANSWER: Rohan has more sugar (10/12 > 9/12).
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Which is larger: 2/5 of a litre of milk or 4/9 of a litre of milk?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify numerators (2, 4) and denominators (5, 9).
- step 2: Choose a common denominator (5 × 9 = 45).
- step 3: Convert 2/5: (2 × 9) / (5 × 9) = 18/45.
- step 4: Convert 4/9: (4 × 5) / (9 × 5) = 20/45.
- step 5: Compare 18/45 and 20/45.
REASONING: Since 20 is larger than 18, the fraction representing 20 parts of the whole is greater than the one representing 18 parts.
ANSWER: 4/9 is larger.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Arrange 1/2, 2/3, and 3/4 in ascending order.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify denominators (2, 3, 4).
- step 2: Find the Least Common Multiple (LCM) of 2, 3, 4, which is 12.
- step 3: Convert 1/2 = 6/12; 2/3 = 8/12; 3/4 = 9/12.
- step 4: Compare numerators: 6 < 8 < 9.
- step 5: Sequence the original fractions based on the numerators.
REASONING: Once all fractions are in "twelfths," the order of the numerators directly reflects the order of the total values.
ANSWER: 1/2, 2/3, 3/4.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You are dividing a cake among friends. Friend A gets 2/7 of the cake, and Friend B gets 2/9 of the cake. Who got more cake? Explain your reasoning without using a common denominator.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- step 1: Recognize that the numerators are the same.
- step 2: Identify the denominators as 7 and 9.
- step 3: Reason that dividing a cake into 7 parts creates larger pieces than dividing it into 9 parts.
- step 4: Conclude that 2 larger pieces (2/7) are more than 2 smaller pieces (2/9).
ANSWER: Friend A got more. Because the number of pieces is the same, we only need to compare the size of the pieces; 1/7 is bigger than 1/9.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A student claims that 4/5 is smaller than 4/7 because 5 is smaller than 7. How would you correct this student? Provide a real-world example using a litre of water.
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the error (treating the denominator rule for unit fractions backwards).
- step 2: Use the "sharing" logic to show that 5 shares is a "less crowded" division than 7 shares.
- step 3: Apply to the litre of water example.
- step 4: Verify the comparison (4/5 = 28/35, 4/7 = 20/35).
ANSWER: The student is wrong. If you have 4 bottles of water and pour them into 5 large jugs, each jug is fuller than if you poured them into 7 smaller jugs. Since 4/5 > 4/7, the student's reasoning is reversed.
DIFFICULTY: hard
