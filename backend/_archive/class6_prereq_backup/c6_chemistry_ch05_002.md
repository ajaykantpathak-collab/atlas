## DEFINITION
A chemical change is a process in which one or more substances undergo a transformation to create entirely new substances with different properties. Unlike physical changes, these transformations are often permanent and cannot be easily reversed.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to distinguish between physical and chemical changes by identifying whether a new substance with unique properties has been created. They should provide at least two examples of chemical changes and explain why they are not reversible.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the definition and observable signs of chemical changes (e.g., color change, ash formation, gas evolution). It excludes reaction kinetics, chemical equations, and complex molecular structures.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever wondered why a piece of paper turns into ash when it burns, but simply turns into smaller pieces when you rip it? Start by observing a simple household change, like a piece of iron left in rain starting to rust. In a physical change, the substance stays the same—if you cut a piece of wood, it is still wood. However, in a chemical change, the wood disappears and becomes something new: ash and smoke. By looking for the birth of a "new substance" that wasn't there before, you can easily identify a chemical change.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the most important sign that a chemical change has occurred?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The formation of a new substance with different properties is the key sign. If a student says "a change in shape," they are likely confusing physical changes with chemical changes.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you melt an ice cube into water, is this a chemical change? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
No, it is not a chemical change because the water is still water (H2O), just in a different state. If the student says "yes," they are failing to recognize that no new substance was created.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A baker heats sugar until it turns into a brown, sweet-smelling liquid called caramel. Can this be reversed to get the original white sugar crystals back?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
No, it cannot be reversed because the heat caused a chemical change, creating a new substance (caramel). If the student thinks it can be reversed by cooling, they misunderstand that chemical changes are usually permanent.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare burning a candle wick to melting the candle wax. Which one is more likely to be a chemical change?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Burning the wick is a chemical change because it reacts with oxygen to produce smoke and ash, whereas melting the wax is a physical change. Students often struggle to see that a single object can undergo both types of changes at once.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it possible for a chemical change to occur without heat? Provide an example.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, rusting of iron occurs at room temperature when iron reacts with oxygen and moisture. If a student answers "no," they believe that chemical changes always require fire or high heat.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Think of a kitchen activity. Describe a process where you mix two things and a chemical change happens.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Baking a cake is a great example, as the batter (flour, eggs, sugar) changes into a solid, spongy structure through heat. A poor answer would be "mixing salt and water," because that is just a mixture, not a chemical change.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that any change in appearance (like color) or any change involving heat is a chemical change. They fail to realize that ice melting or water boiling (which involves heat) are physical changes because the substance remains the same.
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student: "If you change the shape or state of an object, can you get the original back?" Explain that if the answer is 'yes' (like freezing water), it is physical. If the answer is 'no' (like burnt paper), it is chemical because the identity of the material has changed forever.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A student leaves a shiny iron nail outside in the rain for a week. The nail becomes reddish-brown and flaky. Is this a chemical change? How many steps can you identify in this process?
STEPS:
- 1: Observe the initial state (shiny iron).
- 2: Observe the final state (reddish-brown, flaky substance).
- 3: Determine if the substance is the same (Iron vs. Iron Oxide).
- 4: Conclude that a new substance has formed.
REASONING: The formation of the new reddish layer (rust) indicates that iron has reacted with oxygen and water to form a new compound.
ANSWER: Yes, this is a chemical change.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: You take 50 grams of sugar and dissolve it in 200 ml of water. You then evaporate the water. You get back the 50 grams of sugar. Is this a chemical change?
STEPS:
- 1: Identify the ingredients (sugar + water).
- 2: Identify the process (dissolving then evaporating).
- 3: Check the final product (sugar).
- 4: Compare with original substance.
REASONING: Since the sugar properties remain the same after evaporation, no new substance was formed.
ANSWER: No, this is a physical change.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A firework is lit. It produces a bright flash, a loud noise, and grey ash remains on the ground. Classify the changes occurring.
STEPS:
- 1: Identify the inputs (chemicals in firework).
- 2: Identify the visible changes (light, sound, ash).
- 3: Compare inputs and outputs.
- 4: Evaluate if outputs are different substances.
- 5: Combine findings to name the process.
REASONING: The production of light, sound, and ash are markers of a chemical reaction where original chemicals are consumed.
ANSWER: This is a chemical change.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You have a piece of wax. You melt it, and then you burn it. Explain why one is physical and one is chemical.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying.
STEPS:
- 1: Define physical change (no new substance).
- 2: Define chemical change (new substance formed).
- 3: Analyze melting (state change only).
- 4: Analyze burning (reaction with oxygen).
ANSWER: Melting is physical because the wax remains wax; burning is chemical because it turns into smoke and energy.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: If you have a mixture of iron filings and sand, you can separate them with a magnet. Is this a chemical change? What if you heat the mixture until the iron reacts with the sand?
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction.
STEPS:
- 1: Evaluate the mixture (physical separation).
- 2: Evaluate the reaction (new compound).
- 3: Justify the difference.
ANSWER: Separating with a magnet is a physical process. Reacting them with heat is a chemical change because a new substance is formed that cannot be separated by a magnet.
DIFFICULTY: hard
