## DEFINITION
Storytelling and recitation are speaking skills that involve narrating a story or performing a poem aloud by using voice modulation, rhythm, and body language to bring the content to life. Storytelling focuses on narrative engagement, while recitation focuses on the artistic delivery of poetic rhythm and emotional tone.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can deliver a short story using at least three distinct vocal tones and perform a poem while maintaining a steady, appropriate rhythm and clear pronunciation. Mastery is demonstrated when the student can explain why a specific tone or pause was used at a certain point in their performance.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the fundamental vocal and delivery techniques for storytelling and poetic recitation. It excludes formal theatrical acting, debate procedures, and prolonged public speaking presentations.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever noticed how your favorite cartoon character sounds different when they are excited versus when they are scared? To start storytelling or recitation, begin by reading the text silently to identify the "mood" of each part. Next, practice "over-exaggerating" your voice—if a character is happy, make your pitch higher; if something is mysterious, slow down your speed. Use your hands to emphasize key moments but keep the focus on your voice. The key insight is that your voice is an instrument, and you must "play" it to match the story's emotions.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What are two vocal techniques a storyteller can use to keep an audience interested?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Two techniques are varying the pace (speed) and changing the pitch (tone) of the voice for different characters. A wrong answer, such as "reading very fast," reveals the student thinks the goal is just finishing the text rather than communicating a story.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why is it important to pause when reciting a poem or telling a funny story?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Pausing creates anticipation, highlights important words, or allows a "punchline" in a funny story to sink in. A wrong answer suggests the student views the text as a continuous stream of noise rather than a series of meaningful beats.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If you are telling a story about a very tall giant and a very small mouse, how would you change your voice to show the difference?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Steps: 1. Identify the traits of the characters. 2. Choose a deep, slow tone for the giant. 3. Choose a high, quick tone for the mouse. 4. Practice saying one sentence for each. A wrong answer suggests the student is not paying attention to character traits in the narrative.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Analyze this: Why does a poem require a different delivery style than a newspaper report?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
A poem relies on rhythm, musicality, and emotion, while a report relies on facts and clear, neutral information. A wrong answer reveals the student does not understand that different types of texts require different levels of "performance."
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Critique this: A student recites a sad poem with a loud, fast, and cheerful voice. Why is this ineffective?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
This is ineffective because the vocal tone contradicts the emotional content, confusing the listener. A correct judgment shows the student understands the importance of "tone matching."
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a one-sentence introduction to a story about a magical chair, and decide how you would say it out loud (e.g., in a whisper, with a booming voice).
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Example: "The chair looked like any other, but it held a secret that could change everything." This should be delivered in a mysterious, lowered whisper. A poor answer lacks a specific delivery instruction or chooses a tone that doesn't match the mystery.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think "recitation" is just memorizing lines and saying them as fast as possible to show they know the poem. They fail to realize that recitation is a *performance* where rhythm and pauses are as important as the words themselves. For example, a student might rush through a line like "The wind whispered softly" without actually letting the "whisper" sound soft.
## ON_FAILURE
1. Ask the student to read the line slowly, syllable by syllable. 2. Ask them to identify one word in the line that describes a sound or feeling (e.g., "whispered"). 3. Tell them to change their volume to match that word. 4. Have them re-read the line, focusing only on that one word's emotional quality.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Deliver the line: "The elephant moved slowly, but the dog sprang up with a yelp!"
STEPS:
- 1. Identify the two actions: the slow elephant and the sudden dog.
- 2. Select a slow, deep pace for the elephant.
- 3. Select a sharp, high, and quick pace for the dog.
- 4. Insert a pause after "slowly" to show the transition.
REASONING: Matching vocal speed to the action (slow vs. quick) helps the listener "see" the story.
ANSWER: Say "The elephant moved... slowly..." (very slow) then "...but the dog sprang up with a yelp!" (sudden burst of speed).
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Recite a short poem line: "The stars shine bright in the quiet night."
STEPS:
- 1. Identify the keyword "quiet" and "bright."
- 2. Lower the volume for "quiet."
- 3. Increase the brightness/clarity of the voice for "bright."
- 4. Use a steady, rhythmic cadence throughout the line.
REASONING: By emphasizing keywords with volume changes, the meaning of the poem is highlighted.
ANSWER: A rhythmic, soft, and clear delivery that honors the poem's structure.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: You have a story where a character is surprised. How do you deliver the phrase "I cannot believe it!"?
STEPS:
- 1. Analyze the emotion: Surprise/Shock.
- 2. Determine the pitch: Higher than normal speech.
- 3. Determine the speed: Fast, with emphasis on "cannot."
- 4. Add a facial expression: Wide eyes to support the vocal tone.
- 5. Pause after the phrase to show the character is stunned.
REASONING: Combining physical cues with vocal changes creates a believable delivery.
ANSWER: A quick, high-pitched delivery with a surprised facial expression.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If you are telling a story about a sad elephant, why would a monotone (flat) voice be a bad choice?
COMPETENCY: Critical Analysis of Delivery
STEPS:
- 1. Define monotone.
- 2. Identify the mood of the story (sadness).
- 3. Contrast monotone with the needs of a sad mood.
- 4. Explain why the listener would lose interest.
ANSWER: A monotone voice lacks the emotional "color" needed to express sadness; it makes the story sound like a list of facts rather than an emotional experience, making the listener bored.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Describe how you would change your storytelling style if the audience was made up of five-year-olds versus your classmates.
COMPETENCY: Audience Awareness
STEPS:
- 1. Analyze the difference between five-year-olds and classmates (attention span, interests).
- 2. Choose vocal techniques for five-year-olds (more energy, higher pitch, simpler rhythm).
- 3. Choose vocal techniques for classmates (more nuance, sophisticated pacing).
- 4. Contrast the two approaches.
- 5. Justify why the change is necessary.
ANSWER: For five-year-olds, I would use more exaggerated voices and faster transitions to keep them engaged. For classmates, I would use more subtle tone changes and longer pauses to allow for deeper reflection on the story's meaning.
DIFFICULTY: hard
