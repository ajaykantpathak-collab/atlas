## DEFINITION
The Civil Disobedience Movement was a non-violent protest started by Mahatma Gandhi in 1930 to challenge British rule by intentionally breaking unfair laws, beginning with the salt tax. It invited Indians to peacefully refuse to follow British mandates as a way to demand freedom.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can identify the Dandi March as the starting point of the movement and explain why a common item like salt was chosen as a symbol of protest against British oppression.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the launch of the movement and the Dandi March. It excludes regional movements, Round Table Conferences, and detailed analysis of women's participation.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are playing a game where someone suddenly makes a rule that you have to pay a fee every time you take a breath of air; would you follow that rule? This is how Indians felt when the British taxed salt, a basic necessity for every family. To begin understanding this, look at the map from Sabarmati to Dandi and visualize Gandhi and his followers walking for days to reach the sea. Think of this not just as a walk, but as a silent, powerful message that the people would no longer obey an unfair system. The key insight is that by breaking the salt law, Gandhi turned a simple act of gathering seawater into a national movement for independence.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What event marked the official start of the Civil Disobedience Movement in 1930?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The Dandi March (or Salt March) marked the start of the movement. A wrong answer suggests the student has not yet associated the movement's beginning with Gandhi's specific journey to the coast.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why did Gandhi choose to break the salt law specifically, rather than any other law?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
He chose salt because it was a basic necessity used by everyone, rich or poor, and the British monopoly and tax on it represented how they exploited all Indians. A wrong answer shows the student does not understand that the protest was meant to connect with the daily lives of common people.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If you were a villager in 1930, how would you show your support for this movement without using violence?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Steps: 1. Acknowledge the need for non-violence. 2. Identify a peaceful action, like making salt at home or refusing to buy British-made goods. 3. Explain that this refusal is a form of 'disobedience.' A wrong answer indicates the student thinks 'disobedience' must involve fighting or physical harm.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
How does the term 'Civil Disobedience' differ from a violent rebellion?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Civil Disobedience is the peaceful, public refusal to follow a law one believes is unjust, whereas violent rebellion involves force and destruction. A wrong answer reveals confusion between peaceful protest and armed conflict.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Was the Dandi March an effective way to protest? Justify your answer.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, it was effective because it turned a simple, legal issue into a national movement that gathered widespread support through non-violence. A wrong answer suggests the student believes only physical force can make a political impact.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple poster slogan that Gandhi might have used to explain the movement to villagers who could not read.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The slogan should focus on 'Salt is our right' or 'Free salt for all.' A poor answer would be a complex essay or a violent slogan, which fails to capture the spirit of simple, non-violent mass participation.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think the Civil Disobedience Movement was a small, secret group of people sneaking around to make salt. In reality, it was a massive, open public protest meant to be seen and joined by thousands of people to show the British that their laws were losing authority.
## ON_FAILURE
If a student thinks it was a 'secret' act, ask them: "If Gandhi wanted to change the law for everyone, would it be better to hide his actions or do them in front of everyone?" Guide them to see that the march was a public demonstration to inspire others to join the cause.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Analyze the choice of the Dandi March route (Sabarmati to Dandi).
STEPS:
- 1: Locate Sabarmati and Dandi on a map.
- 2: Calculate that this was a long journey (240 miles).
- 3: Note that it is a coastal destination.
- 4: Conclude that the length of the journey allowed the message to spread to many villages on the way.
REASONING: The journey was a calculated public relations act. The longer the walk, the more people would see and hear about the protest.
ANSWER: The route was chosen to maximize public awareness and demonstrate the determination of the protestors.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Identify why the salt tax was considered "oppressive."
STEPS:
- 1: Define what salt is used for (food, survival).
- 2: Identify who pays the tax (everyone).
- 3: Identify who gets the money (the British).
- 4: Determine the impact on the poor (cannot afford basic food seasoning/preservation).
REASONING: Taxes on luxuries don't hurt the poor, but a tax on a survival necessity creates a moral crisis for a government.
ANSWER: It was oppressive because it targeted the poorest Indians for a basic biological necessity, highlighting British greed.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Explain the difference between 'Disobedience' and 'Ignoring a law.'
STEPS:
- 1: Define ignoring (doing it because you don't know it exists).
- 2: Define disobedience (doing it on purpose to show you disagree).
- 3: Analyze Gandhi's act (he told the world he was going to break the law).
- 4: Compare intent.
REASONING: Disobedience requires a moral stand; ignoring is just accidental behavior.
ANSWER: Gandhi's act was 'Disobedience' because it was a deliberate, public challenge to a law he deemed immoral, not an accidental act.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Why would Gandhi's 78 followers be important to the success of the march?
COMPETENCY: Critical Thinking
STEPS:
- 1: Consider the power of numbers.
- 2: Consider the visual impact of a group vs. one person.
- 3: Explain the role of leadership and solidarity.
- 4: Conclude how this invites others to join.
ANSWER: A group shows that the movement is organized and has support, making it harder for the British to dismiss it as the action of one person. It inspires others to join the growing crowd. (Medium)
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: If the British had allowed Indians to make their own salt, would the movement have started in the same way?
COMPETENCY: Analytical Reasoning
STEPS:
- 1: Identify the root cause of the movement (the unfair salt law).
- 2: Imagine the law being absent.
- 3: Evaluate if the protest would have a 'target' to break.
- 4: Conclude the role of an 'unjust law' in starting a protest.
- 5: Summarize the logic.
ANSWER: No, the movement needed an unjust law to challenge. Without the salt tax, there would be no specific law to 'disobey' as a symbol of British oppression, so the movement would have needed a different focal point. (Hard)
