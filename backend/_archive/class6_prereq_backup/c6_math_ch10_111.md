## DEFINITION
A tessellation is a pattern created by repeating one or more geometric shapes to cover a flat surface completely, leaving no gaps and having no overlaps. It is like a puzzle where pieces lock together perfectly to tile an area.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify whether a given shape can tessellate by physically arranging cut-outs or drawing them, and explain why shapes like regular pentagons fail to form a tessellation.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the definition of tessellation and the properties of regular/irregular shapes that allow them to tile a plane. It excludes complex tiling patterns like Penrose tiling, 3D tessellation (space-filling), and non-Euclidean geometry.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever looked at a brick wall or a tiled floor and wondered why they look so neat and perfectly fitted? To understand this, try taking several identical paper cut-outs of a shape—like a square or a triangle—and move them around on your desk to see if they fit side-by-side without leaving any empty spaces. If you can cover your whole desk with them, you have created a tessellation! Start by trying regular shapes like squares, as they are the easiest to fit. Notice how their corners meet perfectly at a single point, which is the secret to a successful pattern.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the primary rule that a pattern must follow to be called a tessellation?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The pattern must cover a flat surface completely with no gaps and no overlaps. A student who says "it just needs to be a pattern" misses the core requirement of "fitting together" perfectly.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you try to tile a floor with regular pentagons, you will see gaps. Why does this happen?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Regular pentagons cannot tessellate because their interior angles do not add up to 360 degrees when they meet at a point. If a student says "because they are round," they are confusing pentagons with circles, failing to understand the relationship between interior angles and tiling.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Can an irregular triangle always tessellate? If so, how would you prove it?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Yes, any triangle can tessellate. You can prove it by placing two identical triangles together to form a parallelogram, which is a shape known to tile easily. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks only "perfect" or regular shapes can form patterns.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Look at a honeycomb. Why is it a perfect example of a tessellation in nature?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
It is composed of regular hexagons that fit together without any gaps to maximize space for honey storage. A student who misses this is likely focusing on the color or material rather than the geometry of the repeating unit.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
If a shape has curved edges, like a circle, can it form a standard tessellation? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, because curved edges cannot meet to fill the space between them; they will always leave gaps. A student who thinks circles can tessellate is ignoring the "no gaps" rule of the definition.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple tessellation on a piece of grid paper using two different colors to show the repeating unit. What happens if you rotate every second tile?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The expected response describes how the pattern shifts or changes orientation while maintaining the "no gap" rule. A poor answer fails to acknowledge that the "no gap" rule must be preserved regardless of rotation.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think that ANY shape can tessellate if you just force it together. They fail to realize that the "edges" of the shape must be compatible. For example, they might try to tile with circles, thinking that as long as they touch, it counts as a tessellation, ignoring the gaps created between the curves.
## ON_FAILURE
Provide the student with a set of square, circular, and hexagonal cut-outs. Ask them to cover a 10cm x 10cm area on paper with each. When they see the gaps left by the circles, ask: "Do these shapes touch everywhere, or is there empty space?" Then, have them slide the squares together and ask: "Why are there no empty spaces here?"

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A mason has a square floor measuring 4 metres by 4 metres. He uses square tiles that are 0.5 metres on each side. How many tiles does he need to cover the floor without gaps?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the area of the floor: 4m * 4m = 16 sq metres.
- Step 2: Identify the area of one tile: 0.5m * 0.5m = 0.25 sq metres.
- Step 3: Divide the total area by the area of one tile: 16 / 0.25.
- Step 4: Calculate the result: 16 / (1/4) = 16 * 4 = 64 tiles.
REASONING: Since square tiles tessellate, the total number of tiles is simply the total area divided by the area of a single tile. This works because there is no wastage of space.
ANSWER: 64 tiles.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: If a room is 6 metres long and 3 metres wide, and you are using rectangular tiles of 1 metre by 0.5 metres, explain if these will tessellate perfectly.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate the area of the room: 6m * 3m = 18 sq metres.
- Step 2: Calculate the area of one tile: 1m * 0.5m = 0.5 sq metres.
- Step 3: Check if the dimensions are multiples: 6 is divisible by 1 (length) and 3 is divisible by 0.5 (width).
- Step 4: Conclude that since they fit perfectly into the dimensions, they form a perfect tessellation.
REASONING: Rectangles are excellent for tessellation because their sides are parallel and their angles are 90 degrees, allowing them to align perfectly along the axes of the room.
ANSWER: Yes, they will tessellate perfectly, and you would need 36 tiles.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A designer wants to create a pattern using regular hexagons with a side length of 2 cm. If the total area to be covered is 120 square cm, and each hexagon covers approximately 10.4 square cm, how many hexagons are needed?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the total area: 120 sq cm.
- Step 2: Identify the area of one hexagon: 10.4 sq cm.
- Step 3: Divide 120 by 10.4.
- Step 4: 120 / 10.4 ≈ 11.53.
- Step 5: Interpret: Since you cannot have a partial hexagon without cutting it, you need 12 hexagons to cover the space fully.
REASONING: Tessellation in design often requires rounding up because you must cover the entire surface, and cutting tiles might be necessary if the area isn't a perfect multiple of the tile size.
ANSWER: 12 hexagons.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You are given a shape that is a combination of a square and a triangle attached to one side. Can you predict if this new, more complex shape will tessellate? Explain your reasoning.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- Step 1: Recognize that the new shape is a composite polygon.
- Step 2: Test the edges: Do the "protrusions" of one shape fit into the "indentations" of the next?
- Step 3: If the combined edges are straight (or complementary), the shape will tessellate.
- Step 4: Justify based on the "no gap" rule.
ANSWER: Yes, if the combined shape has edges that can lock together (like a jigsaw piece), it will tessellate. The reasoning is that as long as the shape can be repeated so that every bump meets a corresponding dip, the surface will remain flat and gapless.
DIFFICULTY: hard
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Compare the ability of a regular octagon and a regular hexagon to tessellate. Why does the hexagon succeed while the octagon leaves a small gap?
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction
STEPS:
- Step 1: Determine the interior angle of a hexagon: 120 degrees.
- Step 2: Determine the interior angle of an octagon: 135 degrees.
- Step 3: Check the sum of angles at a meeting point: 120 * 3 = 360 (Hexagon); 135 * 2 + ? (Octagon).
- Step 4: Explain that 360/135 is not a whole number.
ANSWER: A regular hexagon has an interior angle of 120 degrees. When three hexagons meet at a point, they sum to 360 degrees, which is a full circle, leaving no gaps. An octagon has an interior angle of 135 degrees. Two octagons sum to 270 degrees, leaving a 90-degree gap, and three octagons sum to 405 degrees, which overlaps. Therefore, octagons cannot tessellate on their own.
DIFFICULTY: hard
