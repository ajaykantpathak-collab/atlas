## DEFINITION
Rotational symmetry occurs when a shape is rotated around a central point by less than a full turn (360°) and appears exactly the same as it did in its starting position. The "order" of rotational symmetry is the number of times the shape perfectly matches its original outline during one complete 360° rotation.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to visually identify if a shape has rotational symmetry and correctly calculate its order by observing how many times it repeats its appearance during a full 360° turn.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers identifying rotational symmetry and determining the order of symmetry for 2D shapes. It excludes 3D rotational symmetry, transformation matrices, and point symmetry definitions.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever watched a ceiling fan spin or a pinwheel turning in the wind? Even though the blades are moving, the fan often looks like it’s in the same position over and over again. To test a shape for rotational symmetry, imagine placing a pin in the very center of the object. Hold that center point steady and slowly turn the shape clockwise, keeping a close eye on your starting position. If the shape looks identical to its original state *before* you finish a full circle, it has rotational symmetry. Remember, every shape looks the same after a full 360° turn, so we only count the "matches" that happen along the way!
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the minimum number of degrees you must rotate a square to see it look exactly like it did at the start?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The answer is 90°. If a student says 360°, they have forgotten that the square matches itself at quarter-turns; this reveals they are confusing "full rotation" with "rotational symmetry."
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If a shape has an order of rotational symmetry of 3, how many times does it look the same in one full 360° turn?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
It looks the same 3 times. A student answering "4" is likely including the 360° position as an "extra" count or confusing order with the number of sides, showing they haven't grasped that order is the total count of identical positions.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A rectangle is rotated 180°. Does it look the same as it did at the start?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Yes, it does. By mentally tracking the corners (e.g., top-left corner moving to bottom-right), the student can see the overlap; a "No" answer suggests the student is only looking at the outline and not the orientation of the sides.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare a square and a regular hexagon. Which one has a higher order of rotational symmetry, and why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The hexagon has an order of 6, while the square has an order of 4; therefore, the hexagon is higher. A wrong answer suggests the student is counting lines of symmetry instead of rotational positions.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it possible for a shape to have rotational symmetry but NOT line symmetry? Give an example.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, a parallelogram (that is not a rectangle) has rotational symmetry of order 2 but no lines of symmetry. Students who answer "No" believe that if a shape is symmetric in one way, it must be symmetric in all ways.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple 2D figure that has rotational symmetry of order 2. Explain how you would verify this.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A simple rectangle or a "Z" shape would work. The verification must involve tracing the shape, marking the center, and showing that rotating the tracing by 180° aligns perfectly with the original.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often confuse "Order of Symmetry" with the number of sides of a polygon. For example, a student might incorrectly say a rectangle has an order of 4 because it has 4 sides, ignoring that it only looks the same after two 180° turns. They fail to physically rotate the object in their mind.
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to draw the shape on a piece of paper and cut it out. Place a pencil tip on the center point of the shape and hold it against the desk. Have them rotate the cutout slowly while watching the corners. Ask: "At what angle does the corner reach the spot where a different corner started?"

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A rectangular garden plot measures 4 meters by 2 meters. If you rotate the plan of the garden 180 degrees around its center, will it look the same? What is its order of symmetry?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the shape as a rectangle.
- Step 2: Mark the four corners as A, B, C, D.
- Step 3: Rotate the shape 180 degrees mentally.
- Step 4: Confirm that corner A moves to the position of C, and B to D, resulting in the same outline.
- Step 5: Check if it matches at any other angle (it does not, only at 180° and 360°).
REASONING: Since the rectangle returns to its original footprint after half a turn (180°) and a full turn (360°), it matches twice.
ANSWER: Yes, it looks the same; the order of rotational symmetry is 2.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: An equilateral triangle has three sides of equal length. If you rotate it around its center, what is its order of rotational symmetry?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the shape as an equilateral triangle.
- Step 2: Determine the angle of one turn: 360° / 3 = 120°.
- Step 3: Rotate the triangle 120 degrees; it occupies the same space.
- Step 4: Rotate it another 120 degrees (240°); it occupies the same space again.
- Step 5: Rotate it to 360°; it is back to the start.
REASONING: Because the three corners are spaced equally, each 120-degree jump lands a vertex exactly where another one was.
ANSWER: The order of rotational symmetry is 3.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A designer creates a logo shaped like a 'plus' sign (+). If the arms are of equal length, what is its order of symmetry? How many degrees must it rotate to look the same?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the shape as having 4 identical arms around a center.
- Step 2: Divide the full turn by the number of arms: 360° / 4 = 90°.
- Step 3: Verify that every 90-degree turn results in an identical image.
- Step 4: Count the matches: 90°, 180°, 270°, and 360°.
- Step 5: Total the matches to find the order.
REASONING: Since there are 4 identical parts distributed evenly, the shape repeats its appearance 4 times in one circle.
ANSWER: Order is 4; it must rotate 90 degrees to look the same.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You have a square and a rectangle. If you rotate both by 90 degrees, which one looks the same as it did before the rotation? Explain why.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Analyze the square: all sides and angles are equal, so a 90-degree turn moves one side into the next side's exact position.
- Step 2: Analyze the rectangle: sides are unequal. A 90-degree turn puts the long side where the short side was, changing the shape's appearance.
- Step 3: Compare results.
- Step 4: State the conclusion.
ANSWER: Only the square looks the same. Because a square's sides are all equal, shifting them by 90 degrees leaves the footprint unchanged. A rectangle's sides are different lengths, so a 90-degree shift creates a "taller" or "wider" shape than the original.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A wind turbine has 3 blades spaced equally. If one blade is painted red, how many rotations does the turbine need to complete for the red blade to return to its original starting position? What is the order of symmetry of the turbine blades alone (ignoring the color)?
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand that the red blade is a marker for one full rotation.
- Step 2: Determine the order of symmetry for the 3-blade structure.
- Step 3: Calculate the movement for the red blade (full 360°).
- Step 4: Formulate the final answer.
ANSWER: The red blade needs a full 360° rotation to return to its exact starting point. However, the turbine structure itself has an order of rotational symmetry of 3, because the blades are identical and spaced equally at 120° intervals.
DIFFICULTY: hard
