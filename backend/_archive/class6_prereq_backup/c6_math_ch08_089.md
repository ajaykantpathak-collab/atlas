## DEFINITION
The area of a circle is the total amount of flat space enclosed within its curved boundary. It is calculated by multiplying the constant pi (approximately 3.14 or 22/7) by the square of the radius.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to calculate the area of a circle given its radius and identify that area is measured in square units. They must also recognize how changing the radius affects the total area.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers calculating the area of a circle using the standard formula. It excludes sectors, annuli (rings), and 3D surface area calculations.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are helping your friend cut a circular piece of card paper for a school project. If you know the distance from the center to the edge (the radius), how do you figure out how much paper you have? To find this, we use the magic number "pi" and the radius. First, you multiply the radius by itself (r × r). Then, you multiply that result by pi (3.14). This gives you the total "flatness" or area inside the circle. Remember, the area grows very quickly if you make the radius bigger, because the radius is multiplied by itself!
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the formula used to calculate the area of a circle?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The formula is A = π × r². A student who says "A = 2 × π × r" is confusing the area formula with the circumference formula, which measures the distance around the edge, not the space inside.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you have two circles, one with a radius of 2 cm and one with a radius of 4 cm, is the area of the larger circle double the area of the smaller one? Explain why or why not.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
No, it is not double; it is four times larger. A student who says "yes" does not understand that the radius is squared (r²), so doubling the radius (2 × 2) results in a 4-fold increase in area (2² = 4).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Calculate the area of a circular garden with a radius of 7 meters (use π = 22/7).
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Identify radius (r = 7). Step 2: Square the radius (7 × 7 = 49). Step 3: Multiply by 22/7 (49 × 22/7 = 7 × 22 = 154). Answer: 154 m². If a student gets 44, they likely calculated 2 × π × r (circumference) instead of the area.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
If you are given the area of a circle, can you find the radius? How would you start?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Yes, by dividing the area by pi and then finding the square root of the result. A student who tries to divide the area by 2 or by pi alone does not understand the inverse relationship of squaring a number.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it better to use π = 3.14 or π = 22/7 when the radius is a multiple of 7? Why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
It is better to use 22/7 because it allows for easy cancellation of the denominator when the radius is 7 or 14, making the calculation simpler and more accurate. A student who insists on 3.14 may struggle with long multiplication decimals unnecessarily.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a real-world scenario where knowing the area of a circle is more important than knowing its circumference.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good answer would involve buying paint for a circular tabletop or choosing the size of a circular rug to cover a floor. An answer involving fences or rope refers to circumference, showing the student still confuses boundary length with internal space.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often confuse the area of a circle (πr²) with the circumference (2πr). They might see a problem asking for the space covered by a circular rug and calculate the distance around the edge instead, leading to an incorrect result in linear units (cm) rather than square units (cm²).
## ON_FAILURE
If the student is confused, ask them to draw a square inside a circle and a circle inside a square on grid paper. Have them count the squares covered by the circle. Remind them that "Area" is always "squared units" because we are filling a 2D surface, while "Perimeter" is just a length of string.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A circular pond has a radius of 14 meters. How much area does the surface of the pond cover? (Use π = 22/7)
STEPS:
- 1: Identify radius (r = 14m).
- 2: Use area formula A = π × r².
- 3: Calculate r² = 14 × 14 = 196.
- 4: Multiply by π: (22/7) × 196.
- 5: Simplify: 196 / 7 = 28; 22 × 28 = 616.
REASONING: We need to find the total internal space. Squaring the radius and applying pi gives the total surface area in square meters.
ANSWER: 616 m²
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A circular sticker has a radius of 5 cm. Calculate its area using π = 3.14.
STEPS:
- 1: Identify radius (r = 5 cm).
- 2: Square the radius (5 × 5 = 25).
- 3: Multiply 25 by 3.14.
- 4: 25 × 3 = 75; 25 × 0.14 = 3.5; 75 + 3.5 = 78.5.
REASONING: Since 5 is not a multiple of 7, 3.14 is the most efficient choice for pi to get the area in square centimeters.
ANSWER: 78.5 cm²
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A circular plate has a radius of 7 cm. A smaller circular bowl with a radius of 3 cm is placed on it. What is the area of the plate NOT covered by the bowl?
STEPS:
- 1: Calculate area of plate (r=7): (22/7) × 49 = 154 cm².
- 2: Calculate area of bowl (r=3): 3.14 × 9 = 28.26 cm².
- 3: Subtract bowl area from plate area: 154 - 28.26.
- 4: Result: 125.74 cm².
REASONING: By finding the area of both shapes individually, we can subtract the smaller space from the larger space to find the remaining uncovered area.
ANSWER: 125.74 cm²
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Two students are comparing circular fields. Student A says, "If I double the radius of my field, the area only doubles." Is Student A correct? Why or why not?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- 1: Write down the formula for the area of a circle.
- 2: Substitute a radius (e.g., r = 1) and calculate the area.
- 3: Substitute a doubled radius (r = 2) and calculate the area.
- 4: Compare the two results to see if the second is double the first.
ANSWER: Student A is incorrect. If r=1, A=π(1)²=π. If r=2, A=π(2)²=4π. The area becomes 4 times larger, not double, because the radius is squared.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: You have 100 square meters of grass seed. You want to plant a circular lawn. If you want the radius to be 6 meters, do you have enough seed? (Use π = 3.14)
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions
STEPS:
- 1: Identify the radius needed (6m).
- 2: Use the formula A = π × r² to find the required area.
- 3: Calculate 3.14 × 36.
- 4: Compare the calculated area to the available 100 square meters.
ANSWER: 3.14 × 36 = 113.04 m². No, you do not have enough seed, as the lawn requires 113.04 m² and you only have 100 m².
