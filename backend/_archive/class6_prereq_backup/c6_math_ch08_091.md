## DEFINITION
Volume is the measure of the total amount of space inside a three-dimensional object. It tells us how many unit cubes (like 1 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm cubes) can fit perfectly inside a shape.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to calculate the volume of a given cuboid or cube by identifying its dimensions and applying the correct formula. They must also express the final answer with the correct cubic units (cm³ or m³).
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the volume of cubes and cuboids based on length, width, and height. It excludes surface area, liquid capacity, and non-rectangular 3D shapes like spheres or cylinders.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you have a cardboard box and you want to fill it with small, solid wooden blocks that are each 1 cm long, 1 cm wide, and 1 cm high. To find the total space inside the box, you could count every single block, but that would take a long time! Instead, you can count how many blocks fit in one row (length), how many rows make a layer (width), and how many layers stack to the top (height). By multiplying these three numbers—length × width × height—you get the total count of unit cubes, which is the volume. This works because volume is just a way of counting the "space-filling" units inside a solid object.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the standard unit used to measure the volume of a box that has dimensions in centimetres?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The unit is cubic centimetres (cm³). If a student says "cm" or "sq cm," they are confusing volume with length or area, indicating they have not yet distinguished between 1D, 2D, and 3D measurements.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you have a cube with a side length of 2 cm, why is its volume 8 cm³ and not 6 cm³?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The volume is 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 cm³, because you are multiplying the length, width, and height (all of which are 2 cm). A student saying 6 cm³ is likely adding the sides (2+2+2) or multiplying by 3, which ignores the 3D nature of the object.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A shipping box is 5 m long, 2 m wide, and 3 m high. Calculate its volume.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Volume = 5 m × 2 m × 3 m = 30 m³. A student who gets a different answer likely added the numbers (10) or missed one dimension, showing a struggle with the multiplication rule for 3D space.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
If you double the length, width, and height of a cube, does the volume also double? Explain why or why not.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
No, the volume increases by 8 times (2 × 2 × 2 = 8). A student who says "double" is applying linear thinking to 3D volume, failing to realize that changing all three dimensions compounds the effect.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it possible for two different-looking cuboids to have the same volume? Provide an example.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes. A cuboid of 4 × 3 × 2 cm (volume 24 cm³) and a cuboid of 6 × 2 × 2 cm (volume 24 cm³) have the same volume. If a student says no, they believe volume is tied to the shape's specific "look" rather than the total capacity.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a box that has a volume of 36 cm³. What could be its possible dimensions?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Any three numbers that multiply to 36 (e.g., 6 × 3 × 2, or 9 × 2 × 2, or 4 × 3 × 3). A poor answer would be a single number (like 36) or an incorrect set of factors, indicating a lack of understanding of the volume formula.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often confuse "Perimeter," "Area," and "Volume." They might try to add the dimensions (l + w + h) or multiply only two (l × w) when asked for volume. This happens because they are familiar with perimeter and area formulas and try to force those patterns into a 3D context.
## ON_FAILURE
If a student adds dimensions, ask them to draw a 1 cm cube. Then, ask them to draw a 2 cm × 2 cm × 1 cm layer and count the cubes. Show them that to fill the *entire* space, they must account for the "depth" or "height" by multiplying, not adding.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A rectangular water tank is 4 metres long, 3 metres wide, and 2 metres high. What is the total volume of the tank?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify given values: length (l) = 4 m, width (w) = 3 m, height (h) = 2 m.
- step 2: Select formula: Volume = l × w × h.
- step 3: Multiply: 4 m × 3 m = 12 m² (this is the base area).
- step 4: Multiply by height: 12 m² × 2 m = 24 m³.
- step 5: Verify units: Since we multiplied metres three times, the unit is m³.
REASONING: Multiplying the length and width gives the area of the floor, and multiplying by the height fills the layers from bottom to top.
ANSWER: 24 m³
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A wooden cube has a side length of 6 cm. What is the volume of this cube?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify given value: side (s) = 6 cm.
- step 2: Select formula: Volume = s × s × s (or s³).
- step 3: Calculate: 6 cm × 6 cm = 36 cm².
- step 4: Multiply by the third side: 36 cm² × 6 cm = 216 cm³.
- step 5: Verify calculation: 6 × 6 = 36, and 36 × 6 = 216.
REASONING: A cube has equal length, width, and height, so multiplying the side by itself three times gives the total space.
ANSWER: 216 cm³
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A box has a base area of 50 cm² and a height of 10 cm. Find its volume.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify given: Base Area (l × w) = 50 cm², height (h) = 10 cm.
- step 2: Recall that Base Area is the result of l × w.
- step 3: Modify formula: Volume = (Base Area) × height.
- step 4: Calculate: 50 cm² × 10 cm = 500 cm³.
- step 5: Verify logic: Since Base Area already includes two dimensions, we only need to multiply by the height.
REASONING: Volume is just the base layer multiplied by how many layers high the object is.
ANSWER: 500 cm³
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A brick-layer has two piles of bricks. Pile A is 20 cm × 10 cm × 5 cm. Pile B is 10 cm × 10 cm × 10 cm. Which pile occupies more space?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- step 1: Calculate volume of Pile A: 20 × 10 × 5 = 1000 cm³.
- step 2: Calculate volume of Pile B: 10 × 10 × 10 = 1000 cm³.
- step 3: Compare results: 1000 = 1000.
- step 4: Conclude: Both occupy the same amount of space despite having different shapes.
ANSWER: Both occupy the same volume because 1000 cm³ = 1000 cm³. This shows that different dimensions can result in the same total space.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: If you have a rectangular container and you fill it with 1 cm³ cubes, what happens to the volume if you add a layer of cubes on top?
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions
STEPS:
- step 1: Define initial state: Volume = l × w × h.
- step 2: Define change: height becomes (h + 1).
- step 3: New volume: l × w × (h + 1).
- step 4: Interpret: The volume increases by the product of the length and width (the area of the base).
ANSWER: The volume increases by exactly the amount of space covered by one base layer (l × w). This demonstrates that adding height directly adds more "layers" of volume to the object.
DIFFICULTY: hard
