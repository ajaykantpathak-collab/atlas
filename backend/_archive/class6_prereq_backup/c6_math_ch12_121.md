## DEFINITION
A number pattern is a sequence of numbers arranged according to a specific, repeatable rule. It allows us to predict the next terms in the sequence by identifying the relationship between consecutive numbers.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
analyze
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify the rule of a given number sequence and correctly predict the next three terms. They should also be able to explain the logic behind the pattern in their own words.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers identifying and extending arithmetic, square, cube, and triangular number sequences. It excludes series convergence, recursive calculus, and complex non-linear patterns beyond the primary school curriculum.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are tiling a bathroom floor and placing tiles in a growing square pattern: first 1, then 4, then 9, then 16. To figure out how many tiles you need next, you don't just guess; you look at the "gap" or the "rule" between the numbers. Start by writing down the sequence and subtracting the first number from the second, and the second from the third, to see if the gap is constant. If it isn't, look to see if the numbers are related to multiplication or special shapes like squares or triangles. The key insight is that every pattern has a "DNA"—a hidden rule—that tells you exactly what comes next.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the next number in the sequence 2, 5, 8, 11, ...?
a) 12
b) 13
c) 14
d) 15
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The correct answer is c) 14. A wrong answer suggests the student is either guessing or failing to calculate the constant difference of +3 between terms, indicating they need practice in basic subtraction to find the "step."
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If a sequence follows the rule of square numbers (1, 4, 9, 16...), what is the 6th number in the sequence?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The answer is 36. If a student answers 25 or 20, they likely failed to recognize the pattern of multiplying a number by itself (n × n), showing a misunderstanding of how sequence positions relate to the values.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A shopkeeper sells decorative candles in packs: 1, 3, 6, 10, 15... If he wants to create the next pack size, how many candles will he need?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
He will need 21 candles. The gaps are +2, +3, +4, +5; therefore, the next gap must be +6. Adding 6 to 15 gives 21. A wrong answer shows the student hasn't identified the "growing" nature of the gaps.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the sequences 2, 4, 6, 8 and 2, 4, 8, 16. Why are they different even though they start the same?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The first is an arithmetic sequence (adding 2), while the second is a geometric sequence (multiplying by 2). A wrong answer reveals a failure to distinguish between additive and multiplicative growth patterns.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it possible for a number to be both a square number and a triangular number? Explain your reasoning.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, 36 is an example because it can be arranged as a 6x6 square and also as a triangle of dots. A wrong answer suggests the student views these patterns as isolated lists rather than geometric arrangements that can overlap.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create your own number sequence that grows by adding 5 each time, starting at 10. Write down the first five numbers.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The sequence is 10, 15, 20, 25, 30. If the student fails to start at 10 or adds a different number, they lack the skill to translate a verbal rule into a numerical string.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often assume all patterns are arithmetic (adding the same number). For example, in the sequence 1, 2, 4, 8, a student might add 1, then 2, then 4, but then incorrectly assume the next step is adding 5 instead of doubling the increment.
## ON_FAILURE
If a student misses a pattern, stop and draw the numbers as dots or shapes. Ask: "Can you see the shape growing?" If they still struggle, write the differences between the numbers in a row below the sequence; usually, the pattern becomes visible in the "row of differences."

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A gardener plants flower pots in rows. Row 1 has 2 pots, Row 2 has 5 pots, Row 3 has 8 pots. If this continues, how many pots will be in Row 6?
STEPS:
- Row 1=2, Row 2=5, Row 3=8.
- The difference between 5 and 2 is 3. The difference between 8 and 5 is 3.
- The rule is: add 3 to the previous row.
- Row 4 = 8+3=11, Row 5 = 11+3=14, Row 6 = 14+3=17.
- Verify: 2 + (6-1) × 3 = 2 + 15 = 17.
REASONING: By identifying the common difference, we can predict future terms without drawing every single row.
ANSWER: 17 pots
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A student saves money in a piggy bank. Day 1: 5 rupees, Day 2: 10 rupees, Day 3: 20 rupees. If this pattern continues, how much is saved on Day 5?
STEPS:
- Day 1=5, Day 2=10, Day 3=20.
- Check the difference: 10-5=5, 20-10=10. The difference is not constant.
- Check the ratio: 10/5=2, 20/10=2. The rule is multiply by 2.
- Day 4 = 20 × 2 = 40. Day 5 = 40 × 2 = 80.
- Verify: Each day the amount doubles.
REASONING: Not all patterns are additive; recognizing multiplication (geometric growth) is essential for rapid calculation.
ANSWER: 80 rupees
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A builder lays bricks in a square pattern. The total bricks used are 1, 4, 9, 16. If the builder continues this for 8 rows total, what is the total number of bricks in the 8th pattern?
STEPS:
- Identify the sequence as square numbers (n²).
- Row 1 = 1², Row 2 = 2², Row 3 = 3², Row 4 = 4².
- The question asks for the 8th pattern.
- Apply the rule n² where n = 8.
- 8 × 8 = 64.
REASONING: Recognizing the pattern as square numbers allows us to jump directly to the answer using the formula n², rather than adding odd numbers sequentially.
ANSWER: 64 bricks
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Look at the sequence 1, 3, 6, 10, 15. If the next number is 21, explain why the jump from 15 to 21 is larger than the jump from 1 to 3.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- Identify the jumps: 1 to 3 is +2. 3 to 6 is +3. 6 to 10 is +4. 10 to 15 is +5.
- The jump size is increasing by 1 each time.
- Therefore, the jump from 15 must be +6.
- 15 + 6 = 21.
ANSWER: The jump increases because these are triangular numbers, which are formed by adding an extra layer of dots that grows by one each time.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: You have a sequence: 100, 95, 90, 85. Is this a valid number pattern? If so, what is the rule and what is the 7th term?
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction
STEPS:
- Check differences: 95-100 = -5. 90-95 = -5. 85-90 = -5.
- Confirm it is a valid pattern with a constant difference of -5.
- Term 1=100, 2=95, 3=90, 4=85, 5=80, 6=75, 7=70.
- Verify using formula: 100 + (7-1) × (-5) = 100 - 30 = 70.
ANSWER: Yes, it is a valid arithmetic sequence with a rule of "subtract 5." The 7th term is 70.
DIFFICULTY: hard
