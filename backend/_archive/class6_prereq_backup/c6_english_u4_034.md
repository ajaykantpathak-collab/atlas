## DEFINITION
Personification is a literary device where human traits, emotions, or actions are given to non-human things like objects or nature. The tone of a poem is the poet's attitude or "feeling" toward the subject, which guides how the reader should interpret the mood.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
analyze
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can identify instances of personification in a text and explain how the chosen words create a specific tone (e.g., joyful, lonely, or urgent).
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers identifying personification and interpreting tone in poetry. It excludes onomatopoeia and the study of prose fiction.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever felt like your bicycle was "tired" after a long ride or that the sun was "smiling" at you on a bright morning? To begin, think about the objects around you—if they could talk or feel, what would they say? When a poet writes, they use personification to make an object act like a person so you can understand the mood better. Once you spot these human actions, ask yourself: "Does this make the poem feel happy, sad, or serious?" That feeling is the poem's tone.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the definition of personification?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Personification is giving human qualities or actions to non-human things. A wrong answer here suggests the student is confusing it with a metaphor or simile, which compare two different things rather than assigning human traits.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If a poem describes the wind as "whispering secrets to the trees," what human quality is being given to the wind?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The wind is being given the human ability to whisper or communicate. If a student says the wind is just "blowing," they have missed the human action entirely, showing they need help identifying figurative language.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A poet writes: "The angry clouds shouted at the earth." How would you describe the tone of these lines?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The tone is intense, aggressive, or stormy. A wrong answer like "happy" shows the student is not connecting the "angry" personification to the overall feeling of the poem.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare these two descriptions: "The train moved quickly through the station" and "The train raced hungrily toward the city." How does the second one change the reader's view?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The second version uses personification ("raced hungrily") to show the train is powerful and eager, creating an excited or urgent tone. Missing this distinction means the student doesn't see how word choice shapes the reader's mood.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Can a poem have a sad tone if it uses personification? Explain why.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, because the human traits given can be sad (e.g., "The lonely lamp wept in the dark"). If the student says no, they mistakenly believe personification must always be positive or "fun."
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Write a sentence using personification to describe a clock that makes the reader feel like time is moving very slowly.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Example: "The clock lazily dragged its hands across the face of the day." A poor answer would just be "The clock is slow," which lacks the creative personification required to set a mood.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often confuse personification with simply describing an object's function. For example, they might say "The car drives fast" is personification because the car is "driving." This is wrong because "driving" is what a car does naturally; personification requires an action *only* a human would do, like "The car groaned with exhaustion."
## ON_FAILURE
If a student fails, ask them to list three things humans do (e.g., cry, smile, argue) and three things objects do (e.g., roll, shine, break). Then, have them create a sentence by forcing an object to do one of the human actions. This helps them isolate the literary device from literal description.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Analyze this line: "The stars danced playfully in the night sky."
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the object (stars).
- step 2: Identify the action (danced).
- step 3: Determine if stars can actually dance (no, they are balls of gas).
- step 4: Identify the human trait (dancing is a human action).
- step 5: Decide the tone (playful/joyful).
REASONING: By mapping the action to the object and testing if it is literally possible, we confirm personification.
ANSWER: The personification is "danced," and the tone is playful.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Analyze this line: "The old house sighed as the wind pushed against its walls."
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the object (house).
- step 2: Identify the action (sighed).
- step 3: Can houses sigh? (No).
- step 4: Identify the human trait (sighing shows weariness or sadness).
- step 5: Determine the tone (melancholy/sad).
REASONING: Assigning the human feeling of a "sigh" to a house creates a sense of age and weariness.
ANSWER: The personification is "sighed," and the tone is melancholy.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Compare two sentences: "The rain fell hard on the roof" vs "The rain drummed its fingers on the roof."
STEPS:
- step 1: Analyze sentence 1 (literal description).
- step 2: Analyze sentence 2 (look for human trait).
- step 3: Identify personification in sentence 2 ("drummed its fingers").
- step 4: Compare tone (Sentence 1 is factual; Sentence 2 is vivid).
- step 5: Conclude why Sentence 2 is more effective.
REASONING: Comparing literal vs. figurative language helps show how personification adds "personality" to a scene.
ANSWER: Sentence 2 uses personification; it makes the rain sound active and rhythmic.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If you read that a tree "stood guard over the forest," what does the poet want you to feel about the tree?
COMPETENCY: Inferential thinking
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the personification (standing guard).
- step 2: Define what a guard does (protects).
- step 3: Connect this to the tree (it is a protector).
- step 4: Determine the feeling (strength, safety, or watchfulness).
ANSWER: The poet wants us to feel that the tree is strong and protective of its environment.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Why might a poet describe a winter night as "biting" instead of "cold"?
COMPETENCY: Evaluating word choice and tone
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the human-like quality ("biting").
- step 2: Compare it to "cold" (cold is just a temperature; biting is an action).
- step 3: Think about what a bite feels like (sudden, sharp, aggressive).
- step 4: Connect to tone (this makes the winter night feel harsh or mean).
- step 5: Conclude the effect on the reader.
ANSWER: "Biting" personifies the cold as an aggressive creature, making the winter feel harsh and painful, which creates a much more intense tone than just saying it is "cold."
