## DEFINITION
A perpendicular bisector of a line segment is a line that divides the segment into two equal halves while meeting it at a right angle (90°). It essentially acts as a mirror line that cuts the segment exactly in the middle.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to use a pair of compasses and a ruler to construct a perpendicular bisector for any given line segment and verify that the two halves are equal in length.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the physical construction of perpendicular bisectors using a compass and straightedge. It excludes the theoretical proof of why the construction works, 3D spatial bisectors, and circumscribed circles.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you and your friend are sitting at opposite ends of a 10-metre rope, and you want to mark the exact middle point to share it fairly. How would you do this without a measuring tape? To begin, open your compass to a width that is clearly more than half the length of the segment. Place the compass point on one end and draw an arc above and below the line; without changing the compass width, repeat this from the other end. Where these arcs cross, you have found two points that sit perfectly balanced between the ends, and joining them creates a line that perfectly bisects your rope at a 90° angle.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the primary characteristic of a perpendicular bisector in relation to the line segment it crosses?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
It divides the segment into two equal parts and forms a 90-degree angle. A wrong answer like "it just cuts the line in half" misses the "perpendicular" requirement, while "it just crosses at 90 degrees" misses the "bisector" requirement.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you draw arcs with a radius that is exactly half the length of the line segment, what will happen during your construction?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The arcs will meet exactly on the line segment, failing to create the two intersection points needed to draw the perpendicular line. This reveals a misunderstanding of why the radius must be "more than half" to ensure the arcs overlap above and below the segment.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A student draws a line segment AB of 8 cm. They set their compass to 3 cm and draw arcs from A and B. Why will they fail to construct the bisector?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Because 3 cm is less than half of 8 cm (which is 4 cm), the arcs will not reach each other to create the necessary intersection points. This shows the student has not internalized the "more than half" rule required for successful construction.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
If you have already constructed a perpendicular bisector for line segment XY, what can you say about any point lying on that bisector line?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Every point on the perpendicular bisector is at an equal distance from point X and point Y. A wrong answer suggests the student views the bisector only as a line, rather than a geometric locus of points balanced between the segment's endpoints.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A friend claims that a perpendicular bisector is the same as any line that crosses a segment at 90 degrees. How would you critique this statement?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The statement is incomplete; a line crossing at 90 degrees is a perpendicular line, but unless it also passes through the exact midpoint, it is not a "bisector." One must verify both the angle and the midpoint location to confirm it is a true perpendicular bisector.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a challenge: How would you use a perpendicular bisector construction to divide a piece of ribbon into four equal parts?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
First, construct the perpendicular bisector of the whole ribbon to get two equal halves. Then, repeat the construction on each of the two new halves to find their midpoints, effectively creating four equal sections. A poor answer would suggest measuring with a ruler, missing the geometric construction requirement.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often change the width of the compass after drawing the first set of arcs. They mistakenly believe that to find the second intersection point, they need a different radius, which results in the line not being a true bisector or failing to be perpendicular.
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to place the compass on point A and draw a circle, then keep that exact same compass width and place it on point B to draw a second circle. Explain that because the width (radius) didn't change, the two circles are identical, which is the secret to finding the perfectly balanced, perpendicular line.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: You have a 12 cm long wooden stick. Construct a perpendicular bisector to split it into two 6 cm pieces.
STEPS:
- step 1: Draw a 12 cm line segment AB using a ruler.
- step 2: Open the compass to a radius of 7 cm (which is more than half of 12 cm).
- step 3: Place the compass point on A and draw arcs above and below the line.
- step 4: Keeping the compass at 7 cm, place the point on B and draw arcs intersecting the first two.
- step 5: Use a ruler to join the two intersection points with a line.
REASONING: Setting the radius larger than half (6 cm) ensures the arcs intersect; the symmetry of the compass movement guarantees the line passes through the center at 90 degrees.
ANSWER: A line crossing the 12 cm stick exactly at 6 cm.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A rectangular piece of paper measures 10 cm in width. Construct a line that divides it into two 5 cm wide strips.
STEPS:
- step 1: Mark the two endpoints of the 10 cm side as P and Q.
- step 2: Open the compass to 6 cm (any value > 5 cm).
- step 3: With center at P, draw arcs above and below the paper width.
- step 4: With center at Q, draw arcs crossing the previous ones.
- step 5: Draw a line through the crossing points, which cuts the paper at 5 cm.
REASONING: The intersection points represent all locations equidistant from P and Q, creating a perpendicular line through the midpoint.
ANSWER: A 5 cm mark on the 10 cm side.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: You have a segment AB of 10 cm. Construct the bisector, then bisect one of the resulting halves to divide the original segment into segments of 2.5 cm, 2.5 cm, and 5 cm.
STEPS:
- step 1: Construct the perpendicular bisector of AB (10 cm) to create two 5 cm segments.
- step 2: Label the midpoint as M.
- step 3: Take one of the 5 cm segments (e.g., AM).
- step 4: Construct the perpendicular bisector of segment AM using a radius of 3 cm.
- step 5: This new bisector divides the 5 cm segment into two 2.5 cm segments.
REASONING: By bisecting the whole, we get two halves; by bisecting a half, we get quarters, allowing for precise division using only geometric tools.
ANSWER: The segment is now divided into 2.5 cm, 2.5 cm, and 5 cm parts.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If you were given a line segment and asked to find a point that is exactly the same distance from both ends, how would the perpendicular bisector help you?
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions
STEPS:
- step 1: Recognize that the perpendicular bisector contains all points equidistant from the endpoints.
- step 2: Construct the bisector of the given segment.
- step 3: Any point picked on this new line will satisfy the condition.
ANSWER: The perpendicular bisector is the path of all points equidistant from the ends; therefore, any point on that line solves the problem.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A classmate says, "I don't need a compass; I can just use a protractor to draw a 90-degree line through the middle of the segment." Is this a construction? Why or why not?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- step 1: Define "construction" as using only a compass and straightedge.
- step 2: Compare the protractor method (measuring) to the compass method (geometric construction).
- step 3: Conclude that the protractor method is a measurement, not a formal geometric construction.
ANSWER: This is not a formal construction because it relies on measurement rather than the geometric property of equidistance. Constructions are preferred because they are theoretically more precise.
DIFFICULTY: hard
