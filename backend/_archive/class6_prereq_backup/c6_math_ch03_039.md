## DEFINITION
A fraction represents a part of a whole, where a "whole" is divided into equal segments. It is expressed as two numbers: the denominator (bottom), representing the total number of equal parts in a whole, and the numerator (top), representing how many of those parts are being considered.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to visually represent a fraction, identify the numerator and denominator, and explain that a larger denominator means smaller individual pieces when sharing a single whole.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the identification, conceptual understanding, and simple real-world representation of fractions. It excludes addition/subtraction of fractions, multiplication, or complex algebraic manipulation.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you have one round roti and you want to share it equally with a friend. If you cut it into two equal parts, you each get one "half." How do we write that? We write it as 1/2. To begin, always look at the whole object first—count how many equal pieces it is cut into; this is your denominator. Then, count how many pieces you are actually talking about or using; this is your numerator. The key insight is that as you divide a single whole into more and more pieces (like 1/2 vs 1/4), the size of each individual piece gets smaller, even if the top number stays the same.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
In the fraction 3/4, which number tells us how many equal pieces the whole was divided into?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The number 4. If a student answers 3, they have confused the numerator (the part we have) with the denominator (the total number of parts).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you share a chocolate bar equally between 2 friends, each gets 1/2. If you share that same bar equally among 4 friends, each gets 1/4. Which piece is bigger, and why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The 1/2 piece is bigger. A wrong answer (thinking 1/4 is bigger because 4 > 2) reveals the student is looking at the numbers as whole integers rather than understanding that a larger denominator means the same whole is being split into more, smaller pieces.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Draw a rectangle and shade 3/5 of it. How many equal parts should you divide the rectangle into?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
You must divide it into 5 equal parts and shade 3. If the student divides it into a different number, they do not understand that the denominator represents the total number of equal divisions required to define the "whole."
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
If a pizza is cut into 8 equal slices and you eat 2, what fraction of the pizza did you eat? If you then cut those 8 slices into 16 smaller slices, how many of those new smaller slices would represent the same amount of pizza?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
You ate 2/8 (or 1/4). You would need to eat 4 of the new smaller slices (4/16). A wrong answer suggests the student struggles to see that different fractions can represent the same physical amount (equivalent parts).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A friend says, "I have 1/10 of a cake, and you have 1/5 of a cake, so I have more because 10 is bigger than 5." Is this reasoning correct?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, the reasoning is incorrect. 1/5 is larger than 1/10 because if you split a cake into only 5 pieces, each piece is much larger than if you split it into 10 pieces. The student likely ignored the "part of a whole" concept and treated the denominator as a simple counting number.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a scenario using everyday objects (like pencils or apples) where you would use a fraction. Explain why you used those specific numbers.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good answer would involve a clear "whole" (e.g., a packet of 6 pencils) and a "part" (e.g., taking 2 pencils out). A poor answer fails to ensure the parts are "equal" or misidentifies the total as the numerator.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often compare the sizes of fractions by only looking at the digits. For example, they believe 1/8 > 1/3 because 8 is greater than 3. They treat the numbers as independent integers rather than as parts of a divided whole, forgetting that the denominator represents the number of slices—the more slices you make, the smaller each slice must be.
## ON_FAILURE
Take a piece of paper (the "whole"). Fold it into 2 equal parts and label them 1/2. Then, take another piece of paper of the same size and fold it into 4 equal parts and label them 1/4. Physically place the 1/2 piece over the 1/4 piece to show that the 1/2 is physically larger. Explain: "The bigger the denominator, the more friends are sharing, so the smaller your piece becomes."

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Meena has a 1kg packet of sugar. She wants to divide it equally into 4 small bags. What fraction of the original 1kg packet is in each bag?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the whole (1kg packet) and the number of equal parts (4).
- step 2: Determine that the denominator is the total number of parts (4).
- step 3: Determine that the numerator is the part we are considering (1 bag).
- step 4: State the fraction: 1/4.
REASONING: Since the packet is the "whole" and it is split into 4 equal groups, one group represents one portion of four equal parts.
ANSWER: 1/4 kg
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A tailor has a 1-metre strip of cloth. He cuts it into 5 equal pieces to make ribbons. What fraction of the total cloth is one ribbon?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the whole (1 metre) and the number of equal parts (5).
- step 2: Set the denominator as 5 (total parts).
- step 3: Set the numerator as 1 (one ribbon).
- step 4: Express the share as 1/5.
REASONING: Dividing the total length into 5 equal segments means each segment is one-fifth of the total length.
ANSWER: 1/5 metre
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Four friends order 3 glasses of sugarcane juice. They share the total juice equally among themselves. What fraction of a glass does each friend get?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the total amount of juice (3 glasses) and total people (4).
- step 2: Recognize this as a division problem: 3 divided by 4.
- step 3: Understand that 3 glasses shared by 4 people results in 3 portions of 1/4 each.
- step 4: Combine the portions: 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 = 3/4.
REASONING: Sharing 3 units among 4 people means each person receives 3 of the 4 equal parts of the total.
ANSWER: 3/4 glass
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You have a square chocolate bar. You give 1/2 of it to your brother. Your friend has an identical square bar and gives 1/4 of it to her sister. Who gave away a larger piece of chocolate? Explain your reasoning.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- step 1: Visualize the two identical squares.
- step 2: Compare the size of 1/2 (the whole divided into 2) vs 1/4 (the whole divided into 4).
- step 3: Conclude that 1/2 covers half the square, while 1/4 covers only a quarter.
ANSWER: You gave away a larger piece. Reasoning: 1/2 means the bar is split into 2 large pieces, while 1/4 means it is split into 4 smaller pieces. Therefore, 1/2 is physically larger than 1/4.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A pizza shop cuts a pizza into 6 slices. You claim that 3 of those slices are equal to 1/2 of the pizza. Is your claim correct? Justify your answer using the concept of fractional units.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the total number of parts (6).
- step 2: Express 3 slices as a fraction: 3/6.
- step 3: Compare 3/6 to 1/2.
- step 4: Explain that 3 is half of 6.
ANSWER: Yes, the claim is correct. 3/6 represents 3 parts out of 6. Since 3 is exactly half of 6, 3/6 and 1/2 represent the same portion of the whole pizza.
DIFFICULTY: hard
