## DEFINITION
An angle bisector is a ray that starts at the vertex of an angle and divides it into two smaller angles of exactly equal measure.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to use a compass and ruler to construct an angle bisector for any given angle and verify that the two resulting angles are equal using a protractor.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the geometric construction of an angle bisector using a compass and straightedge. It excludes the formal geometric proof of why the construction works and does not cover external bisectors or inscribed circles.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you have a piece of paper shaped like a slice of pizza and you want to share it perfectly with a friend; how would you find the exact middle line to cut it? To start, place your compass point on the corner (vertex) of the angle and draw an arc that crosses both sides of the angle. Now, place the compass point on each of those two spots where the arc crossed the sides and draw two more arcs that overlap in the empty space between the sides. If you draw a line from the corner through that point where the two arcs cross, you have created the bisector. This works because the arcs ensure the points are equidistant from the sides, creating a perfect balance.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the primary purpose of an angle bisector?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
An angle bisector divides an angle into two equal parts. A wrong answer like "it makes the angle smaller" indicates the student does not understand that the division must result in two equal angles.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you have an angle of 60°, what should be the measure of each angle after you construct its bisector?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Each angle should be 30°. If the student says something other than 30°, they likely do not understand the concept of "bisecting" as splitting into two equal halves.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If you draw an arc from the vertex that crosses the sides of an angle, why do you need to draw two more arcs from those intersection points?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
You draw the two additional arcs to find a point that is the same distance from both sides of the angle. If the student says "just to make a mark," they missed the geometric necessity of finding the equidistance point.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
If a student constructs a bisector but the two resulting angles are 25° and 35° (instead of 30° each), what is the most likely error in their construction?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
They likely changed the width (radius) of the compass while drawing the two intersecting arcs. The radius must remain constant to ensure the point of intersection is perfectly centered between the two arms.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Can a reflex angle (greater than 180°) be bisected using the same compass method? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, the method remains the same because the principle of finding a point equidistant from the arms is universal. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks the method only works for acute or obtuse angles, failing to see the underlying geometric logic.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a short set of instructions for a friend who has never used a compass to bisect a 90° angle.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The instructions should include: 1) Place compass on vertex, draw arc cutting both arms. 2) Place compass on one intersection, draw arc inside. 3) Without changing width, place compass on other intersection, draw arc crossing the first. 4) Use ruler to connect vertex to the crossing point. Poor answers lack the "constant radius" step, which is critical for accuracy.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often change the compass radius while drawing the two arcs from the intersection points. They do this because they think the arcs need to "reach" each other, but the fixed radius is what guarantees the bisector is perfectly centered. For example, if they open the compass wider for the second arc, the intersection point shifts, resulting in an unequal split of the angle.
## ON_FAILURE
If a student fails, have them draw a large 80° angle on paper. Tell them to keep their compass locked by tightening the screw. Watch them draw the first arc from the vertex. Then, supervise them placing the needle on each side-mark without touching the adjustment knob. If they change the width, stop them immediately and explain: "The distance from the side to the bisector must be the same on both sides, which is why the compass width must stay 'frozen'."

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: You have a paper corner (a right angle) and want to create a 45° fold. How do you construct this using a compass?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify that a 45° angle is half of a 90° angle.
- Step 2: Place the compass point on the vertex (the corner of the paper).
- Step 3: Draw an arc across both sides of the 90° angle.
- Step 4: With the same compass width, draw two intersecting arcs starting from where the first arc touched the sides.
- Step 5: Draw a line from the vertex through the intersection point.
REASONING: By keeping the compass width constant, we create a point equidistant from the arms, ensuring the line drawn from the vertex splits the 90° angle into two equal 45° parts.
ANSWER: A 45° bisector line.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Construct an angle bisector for an obtuse angle of 120°.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Draw the 120° angle using a protractor.
- Step 2: Place compass on the vertex and draw an arc crossing both arms.
- Step 3: Keep the compass width set to the distance of the arc intersection.
- Step 4: Draw two arcs from the intersection points on the arms that meet in the middle.
- Step 5: Connect the vertex to the point where the two arcs cross.
REASONING: The intersection point serves as the target for the bisector line; because we used the same radius, this point is exactly in the middle of the rotation.
ANSWER: Two angles of 60° each.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A carpenter needs to divide a 60° corner of a wooden frame into two 30° sections. How does he mark this?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Place the compass point on the corner vertex.
- Step 2: Swing an arc across both wooden sides.
- Step 3: Place the compass needle on the mark on the first side; draw an arc in the middle.
- Step 4: Without changing the compass width, move the needle to the mark on the second side; draw an arc crossing the previous one.
- Step 5: Use a straightedge to draw a line from the corner through the intersection.
- Step 6: Verify the split is correct using a protractor.
REASONING: This geometric construction ensures precision for the frame, allowing the carpenter to cut exactly at the 30° mark.
ANSWER: Two 30° angles.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If you were given an angle already drawn on a page, how could you prove your constructed bisector is accurate without using a protractor?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the two smaller angles created by the bisector.
- Step 2: Use a piece of tracing paper to trace one of the smaller angles.
- Step 3: Place the tracing paper over the second smaller angle.
- Step 4: Observe if the arms align perfectly.
ANSWER: If the arms of the traced angle overlap exactly with the arms of the second angle, the bisector is accurate. This proves equality by superimposition.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A student claims they can bisect an angle by just using a ruler to connect the vertex to a point they "eyeball" in the middle of the arc. Why is this method mathematically inferior to using a compass?
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction
STEPS:
- Step 1: Define the requirements of an angle bisector (equal parts).
- Step 2: Analyze the "eyeballing" method (subjective, prone to human error).
- Step 3: Explain the compass advantage (uses precise geometric equidistance).
- Step 4: Conclude why the compass method is reliable.
ANSWER: The compass method is mathematically superior because it is based on geometric construction rather than visual estimation. "Eyeballing" lacks the precision required for construction, and even a 1° deviation makes the bisector incorrect. The compass ensures the intersection point is mathematically equidistant from both arms.
DIFFICULTY: hard
