## DEFINITION
An eclipse is an astronomical event where one celestial body passes into the shadow of another, or blocks the light reaching an observer from another, due to their alignment in space. Specifically, it involves the obstruction of light from a source (the Sun) by an intervening object (the Moon or the Earth).

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify the correct alignment of the Sun, Earth, and Moon for both solar and lunar eclipses and explain why light is blocked in each case.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the basic mechanical cause of solar and lunar eclipses. It excludes mathematical prediction, the saros cycle, and specific historical dates of eclipses.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever noticed your own shadow on the ground when standing in front of a bright bulb? Just like your body blocks the light from the bulb to create a shadow, celestial bodies do the same in space. To understand an eclipse, imagine the Sun, Earth, and Moon forming a straight line. If the Moon slides between the Sun and Earth, it casts a shadow on us—that’s a solar eclipse. If the Earth slips between the Sun and the Moon, it stops sunlight from hitting the Moon, creating a lunar eclipse. The key insight is that shadows are only formed when objects align perfectly in the path of a light source.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the position of the Moon during a solar eclipse?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
During a solar eclipse, the Moon is positioned between the Earth and the Sun. A student who says "the Sun is between the Earth and Moon" confuses the alignment and fails to realize that the Moon is the one casting the shadow on Earth.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why does the Moon disappear or turn dark during a lunar eclipse?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The Moon turns dark because the Earth blocks sunlight from reaching the Moon's surface. If a student thinks the Moon itself "runs out of light," they miss the concept of the Earth acting as an opaque barrier that casts a shadow into space.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If you are standing in a location experiencing a solar eclipse, what do you see when you look at the Sun?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
You see the Moon blocking part or all of the Sun's light. First, identify the order: Sun -> Moon -> Earth. Since the Moon is between you and the Sun, it acts as a screen. A wrong answer suggests the student doesn't understand that the Moon is opaque and physically obstructs our line of sight to the Sun.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the shadow-casting object in a solar eclipse versus a lunar eclipse.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
In a solar eclipse, the Moon casts the shadow; in a lunar eclipse, the Earth casts the shadow. If a student says the Sun casts the shadow, they have fundamentally misunderstood that the Sun is the light *source*, not the object being shadowed.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it possible for a solar eclipse to happen at night? Justify your answer.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, because a solar eclipse requires the Sun to be visible in the sky to be blocked by the Moon. If a student answers "yes," they are ignoring the fact that the Sun is on the opposite side of the Earth at night.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Imagine you have a torch (Sun), a small ball (Moon), and a globe (Earth). How would you arrange them to show a lunar eclipse?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The order must be Torch -> Globe -> Ball. The student must demonstrate that the Earth (globe) is in the middle, intercepting the light from the torch. A poor answer would place the ball in the middle, which would represent a solar eclipse.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe eclipses happen every month because the Moon orbits the Earth monthly. They fail to realize the Moon's orbit is tilted compared to the Earth's orbit around the Sun, so they don't always line up perfectly in a straight line every 28 days.
## ON_FAILURE
1. Take a flashlight and two balls of different sizes (one large for Earth, one small for Moon). 
2. Place them on a table and move the small ball around the large ball while keeping the flashlight stationary. 
3. Ask the student to tilt the path of the small ball up and down relative to the light beam. 
4. Explain that unless the balls are exactly at the same "height" as the light beam, no shadow falls on the other, just like in space.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A student wants to model an eclipse using a torch, a tennis ball (Earth), and a marble (Moon). The torch is 2 meters away from the tennis ball. Describe the setup for a lunar eclipse.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the light source (torch).
- Step 2: Identify the target (marble/Moon).
- Step 3: Identify the blocker (tennis ball/Earth).
- Step 4: Arrange in order: Torch -> Tennis Ball -> Marble.
- Step 5: Ensure all three are aligned on a single straight horizontal line.
REASONING: For a lunar eclipse, the Earth must cast its shadow on the Moon; therefore, the Earth must be between the Sun and the Moon.
ANSWER: Torch -> Tennis Ball -> Marble.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: If the Moon is to the left of the Earth and the Sun is to the right, which type of eclipse is occurring?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Visualize the arrangement: Sun (Right) -> Earth (Center) -> Moon (Left).
- Step 2: Identify the middle object (Earth).
- Step 3: Recall the definition: When Earth is in the middle, it blocks the Sun's light from reaching the Moon.
- Step 4: Name the event: Lunar eclipse.
REASONING: The Earth acts as the opaque obstacle blocking the light rays from the Sun to the Moon.
ANSWER: Lunar Eclipse.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A satellite orbiting Earth is 3,84,000 km away. If the Sun, Moon, and Earth align such that the Moon blocks the Sun, why does the shadow on Earth appear small?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the relative sizes of the Moon and Earth (Moon is about 1/4 the diameter of Earth).
- Step 2: Recall the property of light (it travels in straight lines).
- Step 3: Realize that a smaller object blocking a much larger/distant light source creates a narrow shadow cone.
- Step 4: Conclude that the Moon's physical size limits the area it can cover.
REASONING: The shadow's size depends on the size of the object casting it and its distance from the light source and the surface it falls upon.
ANSWER: The Moon's smaller diameter compared to Earth results in a restricted shadow area.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You are looking at a diagram of the Sun, Earth, and Moon. The Earth is in the center. A friend says, "This is a solar eclipse." Explain why they are wrong and what is actually happening.
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction
STEPS:
- Step 1: Check the positions: Sun, Earth, Moon.
- Step 2: Identify the middle body: Earth.
- Step 3: Relate middle body to eclipse type: Earth in middle = Lunar.
- Step 4: Formulate the correction: The friend is incorrect because in a solar eclipse, the Moon must be in the middle.
ANSWER: The friend is mistaken because the Earth is blocking the light to the Moon, which defines a lunar eclipse. A solar eclipse requires the Moon to be the middle body blocking light to the Earth.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: If the Moon's orbit were not tilted at all, how would the frequency of eclipses change compared to now?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand that currently, the tilt prevents the Moon from aligning perfectly every month.
- Step 2: Imagine the orbit becomes flat (no tilt).
- Step 3: Realize that every time the Moon passes between the Earth and Sun, a solar eclipse would occur.
- Step 4: Conclude that eclipses would become predictable and frequent (monthly).
ANSWER: Eclipses would occur much more frequently, specifically every month, because the Moon would pass directly between the Sun and Earth, and behind the Earth, during every cycle.
