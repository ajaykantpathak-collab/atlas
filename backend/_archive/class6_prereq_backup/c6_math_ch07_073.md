## DEFINITION
A triangle is a closed geometric figure formed by three straight line segments connected at three endpoints, creating three interior angles. The sum of these three interior angles is always exactly 180 degrees.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to classify any given triangle based on its side lengths (equilateral, isosceles, scalene) or its interior angles (acute, right, obtuse) and correctly calculate a missing third angle given the other two.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the classification of triangles by sides and angles and the angle sum property. It excludes the Pythagorean theorem, congruence criteria, and calculations of area or perimeter.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are looking at a wooden A-frame tent or a slice of pizza; notice how they both have three sides and three corners. To start exploring triangles, begin by counting how many sides have equal lengths—if all three are the same, it is equilateral; if two are, it is isosceles; if none are, it is scalene. Next, look at the corners: if one is perfectly square like the corner of your notebook, it is a right triangle; if all are smaller than that square corner, they are acute; if one corner is wider and "blunter," it is obtuse. Always remember that no matter how you stretch or shrink the triangle, the three corners inside will always add up to 180 degrees, which is our "golden rule" for triangles.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the sum of the three interior angles of any triangle?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The sum is always 180 degrees. A wrong answer here suggests the student is confusing this with the properties of a circle (360 degrees) or a square (360 degrees), showing a need to visualize a triangle as half of a rectangle.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If a triangle has one angle measuring 95 degrees, can it also be a right-angled triangle? Why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
No, it cannot. A right-angled triangle must have one 90-degree angle; if it already has a 95-degree angle, the sum would exceed 180 degrees. Students answering "yes" often fail to realize that a triangle can only have one "special" angle (either obtuse or right) because the sum is strictly limited to 180.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A triangle has angles of 50 degrees and 60 degrees. Find the third angle.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The third angle is 70 degrees (180 - 50 - 60 = 70). An incorrect answer usually results from poor arithmetic or forgetting the 180-degree rule entirely.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare an equilateral triangle and a scalene triangle. What is the main difference in their side properties?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
An equilateral triangle has all three sides equal, while a scalene triangle has all three sides of different lengths. Students often confuse "equilateral" with "isosceles," requiring a review of the prefixes "equi-" (equal) and "iso-" (two).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
If you have a triangle with sides measuring 5cm, 5cm, and 8cm, how would you classify it and why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
It is an isosceles triangle because exactly two sides are equal in length. If a student calls it scalene, they have miscounted the sides; if they call it equilateral, they have ignored the 8cm measurement.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Draw a triangle that is both obtuse and isosceles. What would be the minimum requirements for the sides and angles?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The triangle must have two equal sides and one angle greater than 90 degrees. A poor answer ignores the "isosceles" requirement (two sides equal) or draws an acute triangle instead, showing a lack of synthesis between the two classification types.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often mistakenly believe that if a triangle is "equilateral" (all sides equal), its angles must also be different sizes. They fail to realize that equal sides imply equal turns (angles) at the corners. For example, they might guess an equilateral triangle has angles of 50, 60, and 70 degrees.
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to draw an equilateral triangle by carefully measuring all sides to be the same length (e.g., 5cm). Then, have them use a protractor to measure each of the three corners. They will discover that each corner measures 60 degrees. Explain that because the sides are "balanced" or equal, the "opening" at each corner must also be balanced.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A triangular park has three fence sections. Two sections are 20 meters each, and the third is 10 meters. The first angle is 40°. Find the other two angles if they are equal.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify that it is an isosceles triangle (two sides are 20m).
- step 2: Note the sum of angles is 180°.
- step 3: Subtract the known angle: 180° - 40° = 140°.
- step 4: Divide the remainder by 2 because the other two angles are equal: 140° / 2 = 70°.
REASONING: Since the triangle is isosceles, the angles opposite the equal sides must also be equal. We use the 180° rule to isolate the remaining sum and split it evenly.
ANSWER: 70° and 70°
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A triangular piece of cardboard has angles measuring 30°, 60°, and 90°. Classify it by both angles and sides.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the angles: 30°, 60°, 90°.
- step 2: Since one angle is 90°, it is a "Right Triangle."
- step 3: Since all angles are different, all sides must be different lengths.
- step 4: A triangle with all different sides is a "Scalene Triangle."
REASONING: Classification by angle is direct from the 90° measurement. Classification by sides follows the rule that unequal angles correspond to unequal side lengths.
ANSWER: Right-angled scalene triangle.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A student has two sticks of 10cm and wants to form a triangle. The angle between them is 100°. What are the other two angles?
STEPS:
- step 1: The triangle is isosceles (two sides are 10cm).
- step 2: The sum of angles is 180°.
- step 3: Subtract the obtuse angle: 180° - 100° = 80°.
- step 4: Since it is isosceles, the remaining two angles are equal: 80° / 2 = 40°.
- step 5: Verify: 100° + 40° + 40° = 180°.
REASONING: We apply the isosceles property to ensure the base angles match, while ensuring the total rotation inside the closed shape remains 180°.
ANSWER: 40° and 40°
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Can a triangle have two obtuse angles? Explain your reasoning using the sum of angles rule.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- step 1: Define an obtuse angle (greater than 90°).
- step 2: Assume two obtuse angles exist (e.g., 91° and 91°).
- step 3: Calculate the sum: 91° + 91° = 182°.
- step 4: Compare with the 180° limit.
ANSWER: No, it is impossible. Two obtuse angles alone would sum to more than 180°, leaving no degrees for the third angle, which violates the triangle sum property.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A triangular garden bed has sides of 4m, 4m, and 4m. An architect wants to build a path that cuts the garden into two smaller triangles. If the original triangle is equilateral, what must be true about the sum of angles of the two new triangles?
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions
STEPS:
- step 1: Recognize that cutting a triangle creates two smaller triangles.
- step 2: Recall the angle sum property for any triangle.
- step 3: Apply the property: every triangle, regardless of size, must sum to 180°.
- step 4: Conclude that the sum remains unchanged.
ANSWER: The sum of angles for both new triangles will still be 180° each. The rule that the interior angles of a closed triangle sum to 180° is universal for all triangles, regardless of how they are formed.
DIFFICULTY: hard
