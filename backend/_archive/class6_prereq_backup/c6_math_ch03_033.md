## DEFINITION
Addition of fractions is the process of combining two or more fractional parts of the same whole into a single total, provided they are expressed using the same fractional unit (denominator). When denominators differ, we must find an equivalent common unit before adding the numerators.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can calculate the sum of two or more fractions with both like and unlike denominators, simplify the final result to lowest terms, and solve word problems involving fractional quantities in real-world contexts.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the addition of proper, improper, and mixed fractions with like or unlike denominators. It excludes algebraic fractions, addition of negative fractions, and formal proofs of commutative or associative properties.
## WALKTHROUGH
If your friend gives you 1/4 of a roti and your sibling gives you 1/2 of a roti, how much do you have in total? You cannot simply add 1 + 1 to get 2/6, because the pieces are different sizes. You must first "cut" the 1/2 piece into two 1/4-sized pieces so that all pieces are the same "fractional unit" (1/4). Once every piece is the same size, you just count how many of those pieces you have: one from your friend plus two from your sibling makes three pieces of 1/4, or 3/4. The key insight is that fractions can only be added when they represent the same size of "share."
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the golden rule for adding two fractions like 1/3 and 1/5?
[1-2 sentences]
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The golden rule is that the denominators must be the same (a common fractional unit) before adding the numerators. A student who says "add the numerators and add the denominators" (e.g., 2/8) is incorrectly applying addition to the denominator, which represents the size of the share, not the count.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you have 2/7 of a cake and add 3/7 of a cake, why do you not add the 7s to get 2/14?
[2-3 sentences]
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The denominator 7 tells us the "size" of the slice (it is a seventh-part). Adding 7+7 would change the slice size to a fourteenth-part, which is a different piece entirely. A wrong answer here shows the student confuses the "name" of the unit with a quantity to be summed.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Calculate the sum of 1/4 and 1/6. Show your steps.
[2-3 sentences]
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
First, find a common denominator (LCM of 4 and 6 is 12). Convert 1/4 to 3/12 and 1/6 to 2/12. Finally, add the numerators: 3/12 + 2/12 = 5/12. A wrong answer here suggests the student lacks the skill to find common multiples or correctly perform equivalent fraction conversions.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Why is it easier to add 2/5 + 1/5 than 2/5 + 1/4?
[2-3 sentences]
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Adding 2/5 + 1/5 is easier because the fractional units (fifths) are identical; we simply count the number of fifths. 2/5 + 1/4 requires the extra step of converting both to twentieths to match the share sizes. A wrong answer indicates the student does not understand the relationship between the denominator and the physical size of the fractional part.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is the sum of 1/2 and 1/3 greater than 1? Justify your answer.
[2-3 sentences]
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, the sum is 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6, which is less than 1 (or 6/6). A student who guesses "yes" or "no" without calculation lacks the ability to estimate fractional sums or perform the required conversion.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a real-world word problem where adding 1/2 and 1/4 is necessary.
[2-3 sentences]
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good answer would involve measuring something, like "I used 1/2 litre of milk for tea and 1/4 litre for coffee; how much milk did I use total?" A poor answer, such as "adding 1/2 and 1/4 apples," without context, shows difficulty in bridging abstract fractions with physical reality.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
The most common error is "denominator addition," where students add numerators and add denominators (e.g., 1/2 + 1/3 = 2/5). This happens because students apply the rules of whole number addition to both parts of the fraction without realizing that the denominator represents the "name" or "size" of the unit, not a quantity of items.
## ON_FAILURE
If a student adds denominators, stop them and draw two rectangles of the same size. Shade 1/2 of one and 1/3 of the other, then ask them to visualize those pieces inside a third, identical rectangle. Show them that 1/2 is 3 pieces of 1/6 and 1/3 is 2 pieces of 1/6, meaning they have 5 pieces of 1/6 total. Reinforce that we only add "labels" (denominators) when the labels are identical.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Meena poured 2/5 litre of water into a jug and her friend poured 1/4 litre. What is the total volume in the jug?
STEPS:
- 1: Identify the fractions 2/5 and 1/4.
- 2: Find the LCM of 5 and 4, which is 20.
- 3: Convert 2/5 to 8/20 and 1/4 to 5/20.
- 4: Add the numerators: 8 + 5 = 13.
- 5: The total is 13/20.
REASONING: We cannot add fifths and fourths directly. By converting to twentieths, we make both quantities "speak the same language" of unit size, allowing us to combine them accurately.
ANSWER: 13/20 litre
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A carpenter used 3/8 of a metre of wood for one shelf and 1/4 of a metre for another. How much wood did he use total?
STEPS:
- 1: Identify 3/8 and 1/4.
- 2: Notice 8 is a multiple of 4, so the LCM is 8.
- 3: Convert 1/4 to 2/8.
- 4: Add the numerators: 3 + 2 = 5.
- 5: The total is 5/8.
REASONING: Since 4 divides evenly into 8, we only need to adjust one fraction, simplifying the calculation while keeping the unit size consistent.
ANSWER: 5/8 metre
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Ravi walked 1/2 km to the market and 2/3 km back home. How far did he walk in total?
STEPS:
- 1: Identify 1/2 and 2/3.
- 2: Find LCM of 2 and 3, which is 6.
- 3: Convert 1/2 = 3/6 and 2/3 = 4/6.
- 4: Add: 3/6 + 4/6 = 7/6.
- 5: Convert 7/6 to a mixed number: 1 and 1/6.
REASONING: When the result is an improper fraction (numerator > denominator), it is standard to express it as a whole number plus a remainder fraction for better real-world clarity.
ANSWER: 1 and 1/6 km
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Two students are asked to add 1/3 and 1/2. Student A says the answer is 2/5. Student B says the answer is 5/6. Who is correct? Explain why Student A's method is logically flawed.
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction.
STEPS:
- 1: Calculate the correct sum: 2/6 + 3/6 = 5/6.
- 2: Analyze Student A's logic (adding numerators and denominators).
- 3: Explain that adding 3+2 in the denominator creates a unit (fifth) that doesn't exist in the original parts.
ANSWER: Student B is correct. Student A is flawed because they treated the denominator as a count rather than a unit size, failing to realize that "thirds" and "halves" must be converted to "sixths" to be combined.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: You have three containers of juice. One has 1/2 litre, one has 1/3 litre, and one has 1/6 litre. If you pour them all into a large 2-litre bottle, will the bottle overflow? Show your calculation.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions.
STEPS:
- 1: Set up the sum: 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/6.
- 2: Find LCM (6).
- 3: Convert: 3/6 + 2/6 + 1/6 = 6/6.
- 4: Simplify to 1 litre.
- 5: Compare 1 litre to 2-litre capacity.
ANSWER: The total is exactly 1 litre. Since the bottle holds 2 litres, it will not overflow; it will be half-full.
