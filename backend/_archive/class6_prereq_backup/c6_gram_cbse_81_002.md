## DEFINITION
A pronoun replaces a noun so we do not repeat ourselves: he, she, it, they, this, who, mine. But the replacement must match the original in number and gender — 'Ravi lost HIS book', and it must point clearly to ONE noun, not two.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student picks the pronoun matching the antecedent's number and gender, spots ambiguous pronouns ('Ravi told Mohan he won' — who won?), and uses possessive pronouns (mine, yours, theirs) correctly.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers personal, possessive, demonstrative pronouns and the antecedent rule. Does NOT cover reflexives in depth or relative pronouns (class 7).

## WALKTHROUGH
Show the repetition problem first: 'Ravi picked up Ravi's bag and Ravi left.' Clunky. Swap: 'Ravi picked up his bag and he left.' Then the ambiguity trap: 'Ravi told Mohan he had won.' Who won? Nobody can tell — that is the antecedent rule breaking.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students match pronouns to the NEAREST noun instead of the correct antecedent ('The girls gave the boys their ball' — whose ball?), and write 'it's' for the possessive 'its' — the apostrophe means 'it is', never possession.

## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Find which noun the pronoun stands for — the antecedent. Step 2: Match number and gender to THAT noun, not the nearest one. Step 3: its vs it's — expand to 'it is'; if the sentence still makes sense, use the apostrophe.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
'The girls gave the boys ___ ball back.' their / his / her?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
'their' — the ball belongs to the girls (antecedent), not the nearest noun 'boys'. A wrong answer 'his' or reading it as the boys' ball reveals nearest-noun matching — the classic antecedent error.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Fix: 'Its raining, so the dog lost it's bone.'

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
'It's raining' (it is) + 'its bone' (possessive, no apostrophe). A wrong answer keeping both apostrophes shows the student thinks apostrophe = possession — exactly backwards for 'its'.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
'Ravi told Mohan he had passed.' Who passed — and why is it a problem?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Cannot tell — 'he' could point to either Ravi or Mohan. Rewrite needed: 'Ravi told Mohan, "I have passed"' or 'Ravi congratulated Mohan on passing'. A wrong answer confidently picking one shows the student doesn't yet see ambiguity as an error.
