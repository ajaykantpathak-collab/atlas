## DEFINITION
Markets are categorized based on their geographical reach, where local markets serve immediate neighborhoods, national markets cover an entire country, and global markets involve trade between different nations. These divisions reflect the scale at which goods and services are exchanged between producers and consumers.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to classify a given market example (e.g., a village haat vs. an international import) into the correct category and explain the primary difference in their scale of operation.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the classification of markets by geography (local, national, global). It does not cover market failure, government regulations, stock exchanges, or formal trade agreements.
## WALKTHROUGH
Think about the fresh vegetables your mother buys from the street vendor compared to the smartphone in your father's pocket that was perhaps designed in another country. To understand markets, start by looking at how far a product travels to reach you. A local market is like your village haat, where you can walk to get goods. A national market uses trains and trucks to send goods across states, like snacks made in Mumbai sold in Delhi. A global market involves ships and planes moving products across oceans. The key insight is that as the market scale increases, the distance between the producer and consumer grows.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is a local market?
A local market is a place where goods are sold within a small area, like a neighborhood or village.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Correct. A wrong answer (like "a place where only farmers sell") reveals a misunderstanding that markets are restricted by the *type* of person selling rather than the *distance* the goods travel.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If a company sells shoes across every state in India, is this a local or national market?
It is a national market because it spans an entire country.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Correct. Choosing "local" suggests the student is confused by the size of the *shop* rather than the reach of the *market network*.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A farmer sells mangoes to a merchant who then ships them to a store in London. Which type of market is this?
This is a global market because the trade happens between different countries.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Steps: 1. Identify the origin (India). 2. Identify the destination (London/UK). 3. Recognize that crossing national borders makes it global. A wrong answer shows the student does not yet understand that "global" specifically refers to international boundaries.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Why might the price of the same brand of soap be different in a local shop versus a large city store?
Because local markets are not always directly connected, and costs like transportation and middlemen vary.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Correct. Failing to see this suggests the student thinks all markets are perfectly linked, ignoring the reality of logistics and geography.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it possible for a small village shop to participate in a global market?
Yes, if they sell their local handicrafts through an online platform to international customers.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
This requires understanding that the *platform* determines the reach. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks "global" only applies to massive factories.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
If you were to set up a business, what would determine whether it becomes a local or national market?
The decision depends on the scale of production and the ability to transport goods efficiently across states. A poor answer would ignore the role of logistics and target audience size.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
[Correct answer]
Whether a business becomes local or national depends primarily on the scale of production and the efficiency of the logistics network required to transport goods to distant regions. Additionally, a business must assess whether its target audience is concentrated in a specific community or spread widely across the country.

[Explanation]
A wrong answer reveals that the student misunderstands the relationship between **supply chain capacity and market reach**, likely believing that a business’s scale is determined solely by the owner's ambition or a simple choice, rather than the practical constraints of production volume and distribution infrastructure.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often confuse "market size" with "shop size." They might think a large supermarket in their neighborhood is a "national market" just because it is big. It is actually a local market because it serves the immediate neighborhood.
## ON_FAILURE
1. Ask the student to identify where the customers of the shop live. 2. If they only live nearby, define it as local. 3. Explain that "National" requires moving goods across state lines, regardless of how big the building is.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A weaver in a village makes baskets. She sells some to neighbors, some to a shop in the state capital, and some to an exporter who sends them to Japan. Identify the markets.
STEPS:
- Identify the customers: Neighbors (Local), State capital shop (National), Exporter to Japan (Global).
- Categorize each transaction by distance/border.
- Conclude that the weaver participates in all three.
- Interpret the result: Business scale determines the market type.
REASONING: By mapping the destination of the goods, we classify the market reach.
ANSWER: Local (neighbors), National (state capital), Global (Japan).
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A company produces laptops in Bengaluru and sells them in Mumbai, Kolkata, and Chennai. Is this a global market?
STEPS:
- Identify the manufacturing location (Bengaluru, India).
- Identify the sale locations (Mumbai, Kolkata, Chennai, all in India).
- Compare against the definition of "Global" (must cross international borders).
- Evaluate: Since it stays within India, it is a national market.
REASONING: Geography defines the market scope; national borders are the cutoff for "national" vs "global."
ANSWER: No, it is a national market.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A local bakery decides to ship its special biscuits to a cousin in the USA. Is the bakery now a global market entity?
STEPS:
- Define the primary business scope: Does it regularly trade internationally?
- Analyze the single transaction: Is it a business expansion or a personal shipment?
- Distinguish between "participating in a market" and "making a single sale."
- Determine the classification: The bakery is primarily a local market business.
REASONING: A single transaction does not change the fundamental nature of the market; consistent trade across borders is required.
ANSWER: No, it is still a local market business.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Imagine a village that produces unique pottery. How would you change it from a local market to a national market?
COMPETENCY: Strategic thinking regarding market expansion.
STEPS:
- Identify the barrier (limited reach).
- Suggest solutions: Find distributors in other states, use online shipping, or join a national cooperative.
- Explain the role of transportation.
- Conclude how reach increases.
ANSWER: By partnering with distributors in other states and utilizing national courier services, the pottery moves from local village sales to a nationwide customer base.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: If a country suddenly stopped all trade with other nations, which market type would disappear and what would happen to the price of local goods?
COMPETENCY: Analyzing economic interdependence.
STEPS:
- Identify the affected market: Global market disappears.
- Analyze the impact on supply: Goods previously imported become scarce.
- Analyze the impact on prices: Scarcity usually increases prices for imported-dependent goods.
- Synthesize the conclusion.
ANSWER: The global market would cease to exist for that country. Prices for goods previously imported would likely rise due to scarcity, forcing consumers to rely on local or national alternatives.
