## DEFINITION
Being a critical media consumer means that instead of believing everything we see on news or social media instantly, we investigate the source, the purpose, and the evidence behind the message. It is the practice of questioning information to decide if it is fair, accurate, or biased before sharing it.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
analyze
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to evaluate a news headline or social media post by identifying the author, the source of facts, and the potential intent of the creator. Success is demonstrated by the student asking at least three critical questions before deciding if the information is trustworthy.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers the evaluation of information accuracy and source credibility in media. Excludes psychological theories on media habits, specific mathematical analysis of algorithm biases, and the socioeconomic digital divide.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you see a viral video on your phone claiming that "Schools in your city will be closed for a whole month starting tomorrow." Instead of panicking or telling your friends, stop and ask: Who posted this? Is it the official government education department or a random account? Look for evidence, like a link to an official order or a quote from a known authority. If the post doesn't mention where they got the news, it is likely just a rumor. By taking these steps, you move from being a passive receiver of information to an active, critical thinker.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the first step you should take when you see a shocking piece of news online?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The first step is to identify who created or reported the information. A wrong answer, such as "immediately sharing it with friends," shows the student does not understand that information must be verified before it is spread.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why is it important to distinguish between a fact and an opinion in a news report?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Facts can be proven true with evidence, while opinions are just someone's personal belief. A student who struggles here might think everything written by a reporter is a law or absolute truth, failing to realize that reporters can have their own biases.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If a news report presents only one side of a story about a new school rule, what should you do?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
You should look for other sources to see if they report the same event from a different perspective. A wrong answer suggests the student blindly accepts the first version of the story they encounter without considering that other viewpoints might exist.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
How does knowing the motivation of a person who creates a social media post help you as a consumer?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
If you know someone is trying to sell a product or win an argument, you can guess why they might exaggerate the truth. A failure to answer this shows the student believes all information is created purely to inform, rather than to persuade or entertain.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is a news report more reliable if it cites multiple sources or if it uses anonymous sources? Why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Multiple sources are more reliable because they allow you to cross-check information. If a student chooses anonymous sources, they likely don't understand the importance of accountability and evidence in journalism.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a list of three questions you would ask if a friend sent you a message saying, "My local park is being sold to build a shopping mall."
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The student should ask: 1) Who told you this? 2) Is there a government notice or news report confirming this? 3) Why would they tell you this now? A poor answer would be simply asking "Are you sure?" because it lacks the focus on verifying sources and understanding intent.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often assume that if a piece of information has a photo or a video attached, it must be 100% true. They fail to realize that images can be taken out of context or edited to change the meaning. For example, an old photo of a flooded road from three years ago might be used today to claim a new flood is happening.
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to find two photos of the same event from different news websites. Have them compare what the photos show and what the captions say. Point out how the same event can look different depending on who is reporting it, helping them realize that visual proof requires context.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: You see a post on social media saying "The government is changing the school syllabus tomorrow." How do you verify this?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Search for the information on the official website of the Ministry of Education.
- Step 2: Check if major, trusted newspapers are reporting the same news.
- Step 3: Look for an official document or circular number mentioned in the post.
- Step 4: Compare the dates to ensure the news is current and not from years ago.
REASONING: Following these steps prevents falling for rumors by prioritizing official channels over social media posts.
ANSWER: If no official website or major newspaper confirms it, the information is likely false.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A report claims, "Our city is the best place in India to live because it has the most parks." How do you evaluate this?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify that "best" is an opinion, not a fact.
- Step 2: Look for data on other cities' park counts to check if the claim is factual.
- Step 3: Determine the creator's motive; perhaps they are a local official trying to win votes.
- Step 4: Acknowledge the positive intent but treat the "best" label as a personal viewpoint.
REASONING: Distinguishing between a fact (number of parks) and an opinion (the "best" city) helps maintain a balanced view.
ANSWER: The claim is partially factual (if the park count is high) but mostly an opinion.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A news channel reports on a protest. One channel says it's peaceful; another says it's violent. How do you form your own conclusion?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify that both channels have a specific perspective or "slant."
- Step 2: Look for third-party, neutral evidence like police reports or bystander videos.
- Step 3: Analyze if either channel is leaving out specific facts to make their side look better.
- Step 4: Recognize that there might be a middle ground.
- Step 5: Avoid picking one channel and instead look for consistent facts across both.
REASONING: By cross-referencing, you reduce the impact of individual media bias.
ANSWER: The truth is likely found in the common facts reported by both sides, disregarding the emotional language used by each.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If a video shows a politician making a promise, is that proof they will keep it?
COMPETENCY: Critical Analysis
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify that a promise is an intention, not an action.
- Step 2: Differentiate between a statement of intent and a historical record of actions.
- Step 3: Explain that a video proves the person said it, but not that the outcome is guaranteed.
- Step 4: Conclude that critical consumers distinguish between rhetoric and reality.
ANSWER: No, the video only proves the person made a statement. A critical consumer would check the person's past actions to see if they keep their promises.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Why might a media organization report only the positive results of a government scheme?
COMPETENCY: Media Literacy
STEPS:
- Step 1: Recognize that the organization might have a motive to support the government.
- Step 2: Identify the difference between reporting progress and reporting the whole truth.
- Step 3: Consider who funds or owns the media organization.
- Step 4: Explain the impact of selective reporting on public opinion.
- Step 5: Conclude why seeking independent sources is necessary.
ANSWER: The organization may be biased toward the government or trying to influence public opinion. A critical consumer must look for independent reports to see if there are also challenges or failures in the scheme.
DIFFICULTY: hard
