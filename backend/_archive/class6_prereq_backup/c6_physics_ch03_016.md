## DEFINITION
An electric cell is a device that converts stored chemical energy into electrical energy to create a voltage difference between its two terminals. A battery is a collection of two or more such cells connected together to provide power to electrical devices.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify the positive and negative terminals of a cell, explain that a battery is a combination of cells, and describe that the energy source is chemical.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the basic function and definition of cells and batteries. It excludes battery chemistry, internal resistance, and battery disposal/recycling.
## WALKTHROUGH
Think about the remote control you use for your TV; have you ever noticed the "+" and "-" signs on the batteries inside? Those marks show the two terminals of the electric cell. When you put them in, you are creating a path for energy to flow. Inside that small metal cylinder, chemicals are reacting to push electricity through your device. If your toy needs more power than one cell can give, you put two or more cells together, which is technically what we call a battery.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the primary function of an electric cell inside a device like a torch?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
An electric cell converts chemical energy into electrical energy. An answer focusing only on "making the light turn on" is incomplete, as it misses the energy transformation process which is the core scientific concept.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you have a remote that requires two cells to work, is it correct to call the pair a "battery"?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Yes, because a battery is technically a collection of two or more cells connected together. If a student says "no, those are just two cells," they are missing the distinction that a group of cells functions as a single battery unit.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A student connects a wire from one terminal of a cell to the other terminal of the same cell. What happens, and why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Current flows through the wire because the cell creates a voltage difference between the terminals. If the student answers "nothing happens," they fail to understand that a complete path (circuit) allows the chemical energy to transform into electrical current.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Why do some devices use one cell while others, like large flashlights, use three or four?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Devices use multiple cells (batteries) to provide more electrical energy or a higher voltage than a single cell can provide alone. An answer like "to make it heavier" shows a lack of understanding regarding the purpose of voltage in powering electrical components.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
If a battery-operated toy stops working, is it because the chemicals inside are "used up" or because the metal casing is broken?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The chemicals are "used up" because the conversion of chemical energy to electrical energy is a finite process. A student who guesses "the casing is broken" is ignoring the internal energy-conversion nature of the cell.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Imagine you have a device that needs 3 volts to run, but you only have 1.5-volt cells. How would you solve this using the definition of a battery?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
You would connect two 1.5-volt cells together to form a battery to reach the required 3 volts. A poor answer would suggest using only one cell or would fail to mention connecting them, showing a misunderstanding of how cells combine to form a battery.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that electricity is "stored" inside the battery as actual current, rather than as chemical potential energy. They think the battery is like a tiny tank of liquid electricity. This leads to the error of thinking a battery can be "refilled" by shaking it or that the battery is empty only when the case is physically damaged.
## ON_FAILURE
First, ask the student to imagine a ball held high above the ground; explain that the ball has "potential" to move, just like the chemicals in a battery have the "potential" to push electricity. Explain that the chemicals are like a coiled spring, and when the circuit is closed, the spring is released. Use the analogy of a water pump: the cell is the pump, not the water itself.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A torch uses two cells, each providing 1.5 volts. If the torch needs 3 volts to light up, how many cells are acting as a battery?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify that one cell provides 1.5 volts.
- step 2: Identify the total voltage needed is 3 volts.
- step 3: Divide the total voltage needed (3V) by the voltage of one cell (1.5V).
- step 4: Calculate 3 / 1.5 = 2.
- step 5: Conclude that 2 cells are required to form the battery.
REASONING: Since a battery is a collection of cells, we combine their individual voltage capacities to meet the device's requirement. This shows how multiple cells work together to supply the necessary power.
ANSWER: 2 cells.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: If a single cell provides 1.5 volts, how much voltage would a battery of 4 such cells provide?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the voltage of one cell as 1.5 volts.
- step 2: Identify the number of cells in the battery as 4.
- step 3: Recognize that the total voltage is the sum of the individual cells (1.5 + 1.5 + 1.5 + 1.5).
- step 4: Calculate 4 * 1.5 = 6.
- step 5: State the total voltage as 6 volts.
REASONING: Combining cells in a series increases the total potential difference available to the circuit. By multiplying the number of cells by the voltage of one, we find the capacity of the battery.
ANSWER: 6 volts.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A student has a toy car that needs 4.5 volts to move. Each cell has 1.5 volts. How many cells are needed to create the battery?
STEPS:
- step 1: List the requirements: Total needed = 4.5V. Each cell = 1.5V.
- step 2: Set up the equation: (Number of cells) x (Voltage per cell) = Total voltage.
- step 3: Plug in values: x * 1.5 = 4.5.
- step 4: Solve for x: x = 4.5 / 1.5.
- step 5: Compute the result: x = 3.
REASONING: This requires understanding that a battery's total voltage is the sum of its parts. By identifying the ratio of total needed voltage to individual cell voltage, we find the quantity required.
ANSWER: 3 cells.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You find a battery pack that has space for 4 cells, but only 2 are inserted. Why might the device be dim or failing to work?
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to scientific principles.
STEPS:
- step 1: Understand that the device is designed to receive power from 4 cells.
- step 2: Recognize that each cell contributes to the total voltage needed for the device to function correctly.
- step 3: Conclude that with only 2 cells, the total voltage is insufficient (half of the required amount).
- step 4: Explain that the lack of proper voltage prevents the device from working at full capacity.
ANSWER: The device is dim because it is not receiving the full voltage it was designed for. A battery of 4 cells provides a higher voltage than a battery of only 2 cells, and without the full set, the energy flow is too weak.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Compare a wind farm (as described in your chapter) to a battery. How are they similar and different in providing energy?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the energy source of a wind farm (wind energy).
- step 2: Identify the energy source of a battery (chemical energy).
- step 3: Identify the similarity: both provide electricity for devices.
- step 4: Identify the difference: wind energy is harvested from the environment (renewable), while battery energy comes from finite chemical reactions (non-renewable/stored).
ANSWER: Both are sources of energy used to produce electricity. However, they are different because wind farms convert motion into electricity, while batteries convert stored chemical energy into electricity.
DIFFICULTY: hard
