## DEFINITION
A fraction represents a part of a whole, where a single object or a collection is divided into equal shares. It is written as a numerator (top number) showing how many parts we have, and a denominator (bottom number) showing how many equal parts the whole is divided into.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify, write, and draw fractions given a real-world sharing scenario, and correctly label the numerator and denominator.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the definition of fractions, identifying parts of a whole, and representing them as a/b. It excludes improper fractions, mixed numbers, and operations like addition/subtraction.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you have one delicious paratha and you want to share it equally with your best friend. If you cut it into two equal pieces, how much does each of you get? You each get one piece out of the two total pieces, which we write as 1/2. To understand any fraction, start by asking: "How many equal pieces is the whole broken into?" That is your denominator. Then ask: "How many of those pieces am I talking about?" That is your numerator. Always remember that the pieces must be exactly the same size for it to be a fraction!
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
In the fraction 3/4, what do the numbers 3 and 4 represent?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
3 is the numerator (parts we have) and 4 is the denominator (total equal parts). If a student says 3 is the top and 4 is the bottom, they recall the position but not the meaning; if they flip them, they misunderstand the core concept of a share.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you divide a chocolate bar into 5 parts, but they are all different sizes, can you say one piece is 1/5 of the bar? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
No, because fractions require equal parts. If the pieces are different sizes, they are not "equal shares," and therefore we cannot use a single fraction to describe a part of that whole.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
You have 1 kg of apples and divide them into 4 equal bags. What fraction of the total weight is in one bag?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The fraction is 1/4. We identify the whole (1 kg) and divide by the number of equal parts (4). Answering 4/1 shows the student has reversed the numerator and denominator, which is a common error.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
If you have 1/6 of a cake and your friend has 1/3 of the same cake, who has a larger piece? Explain why.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The friend with 1/3 has a larger piece. Because 1/3 means the cake is cut into only 3 big pieces, while 1/6 means it is cut into 6 smaller pieces; dividing a whole into more parts makes each part smaller.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it possible to have a fraction like 5/0? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, the denominator cannot be zero because you cannot divide a whole into zero parts. Division by zero is undefined, and a fraction represents an actual division of a physical or numerical whole.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Draw a square. Show how you would shade 3/4 of it. If you had to create a different shape representing 3/4, how would you change your approach?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The student should divide the square into 4 equal parts and shade 3. For a different shape (like a circle), they must still ensure 4 equal parts are created. Poor answers fail to keep the parts equal in size.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think a larger denominator means a larger fraction (e.g., thinking 1/8 is bigger than 1/4 because 8 is bigger than 4). This happens because they focus on the number in the denominator rather than the size of the "share" created by division.
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to draw two identical circles. Tell them to cut one into 4 slices and the other into 8 slices. Ask them to hold one slice from each circle. Physically seeing that the 1/8 slice is much smaller than the 1/4 slice helps them realize that more pieces mean smaller individual shares.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Meena has 1 litre of juice. She pours it equally into 5 glasses. What fraction of the total juice is in one glass?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the total whole (1 litre) and the number of equal parts (5).
- step 2: The denominator is the total number of parts (5).
- step 3: The numerator is the number of parts we are looking at (1).
- step 4: Express as 1/5.
- step 5: Confirm that 5 glasses of 1/5 litre each make 5/5 or 1 whole litre.
REASONING: By dividing a whole into 5 equal shares, each share takes the identity of 1 part out of 5. This confirms that the denominator represents the total distribution.
ANSWER: 1/5 litre
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A tailor has a 1-metre long ribbon. He cuts it into 8 equal pieces to make bows. What fraction of the ribbon is one piece?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the whole as 1 metre and the number of divisions as 8.
- step 2: Since the ribbon is cut into 8 equal pieces, the denominator must be 8.
- step 3: Each piece represents 1 of those 8 parts.
- step 4: Write the fraction as 1/8.
- step 5: Since 8 pieces of 1/8 length make 8/8, this equals the original 1 metre.
REASONING: The size of the share is determined by the total number of divisions; increasing the number of divisions (denominator) reduces the size of each piece.
ANSWER: 1/8 metre
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A rectangular farm is divided into 10 equal plots. If a farmer plants crops in 7 of them, what fraction of the farm is planted?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the total number of plots (10) as the denominator.
- step 2: Identify the number of planted plots (7) as the numerator.
- step 3: Form the fraction 7/10.
- step 4: Check if the parts are equal; they are.
- step 5: Interpret 7/10 as 7 parts out of 10 equal parts.
REASONING: Fractions describe part-to-whole relationships; here, the "whole" is the 10-plot farm and the "part" is the 7-plot planted area.
ANSWER: 7/10
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You have a chocolate bar. Your friend says 2/4 is the same as 1/2. Do you agree? Use a drawing to explain your reasoning.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- step 1: Draw two identical rectangles.
- step 2: Divide the first into 4 parts and shade 2.
- step 3: Divide the second into 2 parts and shade 1.
- step 4: Compare the shaded areas.
ANSWER: Yes, I agree. When you shade 2 out of 4 parts, you have covered exactly half of the rectangle, which is the same as shading 1 out of 2 parts. Both represent the same portion of the whole.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: If you have a pizza cut into 6 equal slices and another pizza (same size) cut into 12 equal slices, and you eat 3 slices of the first and 6 slices of the second, have you eaten the same amount? Explain.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the first fraction as 3/6.
- step 2: Identify the second fraction as 6/12.
- step 3: Simplify both fractions to their relationship with the whole.
- step 4: Compare the two portions.
ANSWER: Yes, you have eaten the same amount. 3/6 is half of the pizza, and 6/12 is also half of the pizza. Even though the slices in the second pizza are smaller, eating 6 of them covers the same total area as eating 3 larger slices from the first pizza.
DIFFICULTY: hard
