## DEFINITION
Data handling is the process of collecting, organizing, and summarizing raw information (data) into a structured format like a table or graph so that we can easily understand patterns and draw conclusions. It turns a disorganized list of facts into a clear, readable story.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to convert a raw, unordered list of items into a frequency table using tally marks and interpret the resulting data to answer questions about the most or least frequent occurrences.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers data collection, tally marks, and frequency tables; it excludes statistical analysis (like mean/median/mode) and data validation.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you want to find out the favorite fruit of everyone in your class. You ask 20 friends, and they shout out different answers: "Mango," "Apple," "Mango," "Banana," etc. If you just have a messy list of these words, it is impossible to see the "winner" at a glance. To fix this, you start a table with two columns: "Fruit" and "Tally Marks." For every "Mango" heard, you draw a vertical line, and when you hit the fifth, you draw a diagonal line through the four to make a group of five. This organized method makes it easy to count the total, which is your "frequency," and instantly identify the most popular fruit.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is a frequency table, and why do we use tally marks when creating one?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
A frequency table is a way to organize data by listing categories and the number of times they occur. Tally marks are used to count items one by one as we process a list, preventing us from losing track of the total count. A wrong answer here shows the student thinks tally marks are just random decorations rather than a counting tool.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If your tally marks for "Cricket" look like `|||||`, how many students chose that game?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The answer is 5. A wrong answer (like "1" or "4") reveals the student doesn't understand the standard convention that a group of five (four vertical lines with one diagonal strike) represents the number five.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
You have a list of 10 shoe sizes: 4, 5, 4, 6, 5, 4, 3, 4, 5, 6. Create a frequency table for this data.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Steps: 1. Identify unique sizes (3, 4, 5, 6). 2. Go through the list and mark a tally for each. 3. Count the tallies to get frequencies: Size 3 (1), Size 4 (4), Size 5 (3), Size 6 (2). A wrong answer suggests the student is either skipping numbers or counting incorrectly.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Why is it better to represent 25 students using a tally system rather than just writing a long list of their names in a row?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
A list of names requires reading every single entry to find a pattern, whereas a tally system groups them, allowing for an instant visual comparison of totals. A wrong answer suggests the student doesn't see the benefit of summarization in data handling.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
If two different categories in your table have the same frequency, can you still decide which one is "most popular"?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, if frequencies are equal, they are tied for the most popular. A wrong answer suggests the student believes there must always be a single winner, ignoring the possibility of a tie.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
If you were asked to record the eye color of every student in your school, how would you design a data collection sheet to make organizing it later easier?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good design lists the possible eye colors as column headers or row labels so that you only need to add a tally mark as you walk through the school. A poor answer (like just "I would write them in a notebook") fails to recognize that pre-organizing the labels makes the actual data collection much faster and less error-prone.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often "skip" the diagonal line for the fifth tally, simply drawing five vertical lines (`|||||`). This makes the data harder to read quickly, as the human eye struggles to distinguish groups of five without that diagonal break.
## ON_FAILURE
Show the student a table with `|||||` and another with `|||||` (with the diagonal). Ask them which is faster to count. Explain that the diagonal line is a "shortcut" for our eyes to recognize a group of five instantly without counting each line again.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A shopkeeper sells 15 notebooks: 5 Red, 3 Blue, 4 Green, 3 Red. Organize this into a frequency table.
STEPS:
- Step 1: List the unique colors (Red, Blue, Green).
- Step 2: Tally the data: Red (5+3=8), Blue (3), Green (4).
- Step 3: Draw the table columns: "Notebook Color", "Tally Marks", "Frequency".
- Step 4: Fill in the tallies: Red (`||||| |||`), Blue (`|||`), Green (`||||`).
- Step 5: Total the frequency: 8 + 3 + 4 = 15.
REASONING: By grouping items by category first, we ensure no item is missed and the final count matches the total number of notebooks provided.
ANSWER: Red: 8, Blue: 3, Green: 4.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Students voted for their favorite subject: Maths, English, Maths, Science, Maths, English, Science. Create a table.
STEPS:
- Step 1: List subjects: Maths, English, Science.
- Step 2: Tally: Maths (|||), English (||), Science (||).
- Step 3: Count tallies: Maths = 3, English = 2, Science = 2.
- Step 4: Verify: 3 + 2 + 2 = 7 total votes.
REASONING: Converting raw votes into a frequency count allows us to immediately see that Maths is the most popular subject.
ANSWER: Maths: 3, English: 2, Science: 2.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: You measured the height of 10 plants in cm: 12, 15, 12, 14, 15, 12, 16, 14, 15, 12. Create a table.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Sort the unique heights: 12, 14, 15, 16.
- Step 2: Count frequencies: 12 appears 4 times; 14 appears 2 times; 15 appears 3 times; 16 appears 1 time.
- Step 3: Total the frequencies: 4 + 2 + 3 + 1 = 10.
- Step 4: Match the total with the number of plants recorded.
REASONING: Sorting unique values before tallying prevents us from adding new columns later if we find a height we missed.
ANSWER: 12cm: 4, 14cm: 2, 15cm: 3, 16cm: 1.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You have a list of favorite colors from 20 students. If your frequency table shows the total count as 18 instead of 20, what should you do?
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Re-count the tallies in the table.
- Step 2: Re-read the original list of 20 items.
- Step 3: Identify which tally group is missing a mark.
- Step 4: Update the table to reflect the missing data.
ANSWER: You must audit your work by checking the original data against the table to find the missing entries, as the sum of frequencies must equal the total number of items collected.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Why does a frequency table make it easier to compare the popularity of different items than a simple paragraph describing the data?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Explain the limitation of a paragraph (hard to scan for numbers).
- Step 2: Explain the strength of a table (aligned columns, visual summary).
- Step 3: Give an example of how "most" or "least" is found in a table.
ANSWER: A table aligns all the numbers in one column, making it visually simple to identify the highest or lowest value, whereas a paragraph requires the reader to search through text to find and memorize each value individually.
DIFFICULTY: hard
