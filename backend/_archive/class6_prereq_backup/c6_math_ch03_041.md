## DEFINITION
Simplifying complex fraction problems is the process of solving expressions containing multiple operations (like addition, subtraction, and multiplication) by following the standard Order of Operations (often called BODMAS or GEMDAS) to reach a single simplified fraction.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to correctly identify the order of operations for a given multi-step fraction problem and perform the calculation, including finding common denominators where necessary, to reach a simplified result.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers multi-step arithmetic operations with positive fractions. It excludes algebraic expressions, variables, advanced partial fractions, or negative fractions.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine your school is organizing a snack event where you have 1/2 a packet of biscuits, and you need to add 1/4 of another packet, but first, you must multiply 1/3 of your existing portion by 2/5 to give to a friend. Where do you even start? To solve this, always follow the "Multiplication First" rule: handle all multiplication parts of your expression before attempting any addition or subtraction. Once you have simplified those parts, bring all your remaining fractions into the same "language" by finding a common denominator (the LCM). Finally, add or subtract the numerators while keeping the denominator the same, ensuring your final answer is reduced to its simplest form.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
In the expression 2/3 + 1/4 × 3/5, which operation should you perform first?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
You must perform the multiplication (1/4 × 3/5) first. A wrong answer here suggests the student is trying to solve the problem strictly from left to right, ignoring the priority order of operations.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why do we find a common denominator before adding or subtracting fractions like 1/3 and 1/4?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
We need a common denominator because fractions represent shares of different sizes; we can only add them if we express them as the same "fractional unit" (the same number of total parts). A wrong answer indicates the student thinks fractions are just numbers to be added like whole numbers (e.g., adding numerators and denominators separately).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Simplify: 1/2 + (1/3 × 1/2). Show your steps.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Step 1: Multiply 1/3 × 1/2 = 1/6. Step 2: Find common denominator for 1/2 and 1/6, which is 6. Step 3: Convert 1/2 to 3/6. Step 4: Add 3/6 + 1/6 = 4/6. Step 5: Simplify to 2/3. A wrong answer suggests a failure in either the order of operations or the process of finding equivalent fractions.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
If you have 1/2 of a cake and you subtract 1/3 of the total, why can't you just subtract 3 from 2?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Subtracting 3 from 2 ignores the fact that the fractions represent different sizes of pieces; 1/2 is 3/6 and 1/3 is 2/6. A wrong answer reveals a deep-seated misunderstanding that fractions are just pairs of whole numbers rather than parts of a whole.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it easier to solve (1/2 + 1/4) × 1/3 or (1/2) + (1/4 × 1/3)? Which one results in a larger value?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
(1/2 + 1/4) × 1/3 = 1/4. (1/2) + (1/4 × 1/3) = 7/12. Since 7/12 is larger, the second expression is larger. A wrong answer suggests the student struggles to apply parentheses to change the priority of operations.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a multi-step problem using the fractions 1/5 and 2/3 that results in an answer greater than 1.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
An example would be (2/3 × 2) + 1/5. A poor answer indicates an inability to combine operations to reach a specific goal, showing a lack of fluency with fractional values.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often add or subtract numerators and denominators separately (e.g., 1/2 + 1/3 = 2/5). This happens because they treat fractions as two independent whole numbers instead of a single value representing a part of a whole.
## ON_FAILURE
If a student adds numerators and denominators, take two paper strips of equal length. Fold one into halves and one into thirds. Show them that 1/2 + 1/3 is physically larger than 2/5. Then, guide them to fold both into 6ths to show that 1/2 = 3/6 and 1/3 = 2/6, leading to 5/6, not 2/5.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Meena has 1/2 kg of sugar. She uses 1/4 of the sugar to make tea and then buys an additional 2/3 kg. How much sugar does she have now?
STEPS:
- Identify: Start with 1/2, subtract 1/4 of 1/2, then add 2/3.
- Method: Perform multiplication (1/4 of 1/2) first, then addition/subtraction.
- Compute: 1/4 × 1/2 = 1/8. Remaining sugar = 1/2 - 1/8 = 4/8 - 1/8 = 3/8.
- Final step: 3/8 + 2/3. LCM of 8 and 3 is 24.
- Convert: 3/8 = 9/24; 2/3 = 16/24. 9/24 + 16/24 = 25/24 kg.
REASONING: We prioritize the "of" (multiplication) to find out how much sugar was consumed before updating the total balance. We use LCM to make the denominators match so we can combine the quantities accurately.
ANSWER: 25/24 kg (or 1 and 1/24 kg)
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A bucket contains 5 litres of water. You remove 2/5 of the water, then pour in 1/3 of the original 5 litres. How much water is in the bucket now?
STEPS:
- Identify: Start with 5, subtract (2/5 × 5), add (1/3 × 5).
- Method: Multiplication first, then combine.
- Compute: (2/5 × 5) = 2 litres removed. 5 - 2 = 3 litres remaining.
- Add: (1/3 × 5) = 5/3 litres.
- Combine: 3 + 5/3 = 9/3 + 5/3 = 14/3 litres.
REASONING: Multiplying the fraction by the total quantity tells us the exact amount of litres to remove or add. We convert whole numbers to fractions to make the final addition consistent.
ANSWER: 14/3 litres (or 4 and 2/3 litres)
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A tailor has 10 metres of cloth. He uses 1/2 of the cloth for a shirt, and then uses 2/5 of the *remaining* cloth for trousers. How many metres of cloth are left?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Shirt use = 1/2 × 10 = 5 metres.
- Step 2: Remaining cloth = 10 - 5 = 5 metres.
- Step 3: Trouser use = 2/5 of remaining (5 metres) = 2/5 × 5 = 2 metres.
- Step 4: Leftover cloth = 5 (remaining) - 2 (trouser use) = 3 metres.
REASONING: This requires tracking the "remaining" amount. We must calculate the shirt usage first to find the new total before calculating the trouser usage, as the fraction applies to a changing whole.
ANSWER: 3 metres
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You have two expressions: A = 1/2 + 1/3 × 1/4 and B = (1/2 + 1/3) × 1/4. Without calculating the final result, explain which expression will be larger and why.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- Understand: A adds a small fraction (1/12) to 1/2. B multiplies the sum of 1/2 and 1/3 by 1/4.
- Analyze: Expression A is 1/2 + something small. Expression B is a fraction (less than 1) multiplied by a value less than 1.
- Compare: 1/2 is already larger than 1/4 of (1/2 + 1/3).
- Conclude: A must be larger.
ANSWER: Expression A is larger because in B, the entire sum (which is less than 1) is being reduced significantly by multiplying by 1/4, whereas in A, 1/2 remains mostly intact.
DIFFICULTY: hard
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A student solves 1/2 + 1/4 × 1/3 and gets 1/4. Identify the mistake and provide the correct calculation.
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction
STEPS:
- Identify mistake: The student likely added 1/2 + 1/4 = 3/4 first, then multiplied by 1/3.
- Explain: This violates the order of operations (multiplication must come first).
- Correct: 1/4 × 1/3 = 1/12.
- Solve: 1/2 + 1/12 = 6/12 + 1/12 = 7/12.
ANSWER: The student incorrectly prioritized addition. The correct process requires multiplying 1/4 and 1/3 first, then adding 1/2, resulting in 7/12.
DIFFICULTY: medium
