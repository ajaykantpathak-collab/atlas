## DEFINITION
A food chain is a linear sequence showing how energy flows from one organism to another, starting from plants that capture sunlight and passing through consumers that eat them.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to construct a 3-link food chain and correctly identify the producer, primary consumer, and secondary consumer.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This covers the basic structure of a food chain and energy flow; it does not cover complex food webs, nutrient cycling, or ecosystem management policies.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever wondered why you feel energized after eating a lunch of rice and dal? That energy actually started in the sun, which the plants captured to make food. To begin understanding this, imagine a simple path: the Sun feeds the plant, the plant feeds a grasshopper, and the grasshopper feeds a frog. Think of each organism as a "link" in a chain where energy is passed along like a relay race baton. By identifying who eats whom, you are mapping the energy path of nature.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What do we call the first organism in any food chain that makes its own food using sunlight?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
They are called producers. A wrong answer (like "consumer") reveals the student does not understand that only plants (producers) start the energy flow by capturing sunlight.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If a deer eats grass and a lion eats the deer, explain why the grass is not a consumer.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The grass is a producer because it creates its own food, whereas consumers must eat other organisms to survive. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks anything living that is eaten is a consumer.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
In a chain: Plant -> Rabbit -> Fox, if the number of plants suddenly decreases, what happens to the energy available for the fox?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The energy available for the fox decreases. This is because the rabbit has less food, leading to fewer rabbits, which reduces the energy source for the fox; a wrong answer shows the student doesn't see the chain as a dependent sequence.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Analyze the role of a carnivore in a food chain. Why can't a carnivore be the first link?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
A carnivore cannot be the first link because it cannot create its own energy from the sun and requires other animals to provide the energy it needs. A wrong answer indicates the student does not understand the difference between primary energy production and energy transfer.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate this statement: "Every food chain must eventually end because energy is lost at every step." Do you agree?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, because as energy moves from plants to consumers, much of it is used for life processes or lost as heat, leaving less for the next level. A wrong answer suggests the student believes energy remains infinite or does not transfer between levels.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a food chain involving a common Indian bird (like a sparrow) and explain the role of each organism.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A correct response would be: Grain (Producer) -> Sparrow (Primary Consumer) -> Hawk (Secondary Consumer). A poor response lacks the correct order or confuses the roles (e.g., placing the hawk as the producer).
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think the food chain is a circular loop or that humans are always the top link. They miss the "linear" nature of the flow, often drawing arrows pointing toward the plant instead of toward the animal eating the plant.
## ON_FAILURE
First, ask the student to draw a single line on paper. Tell them the arrow represents the "flow of energy." Instruct them to draw a plant at the start and point an arrow from the plant to an animal, explaining "The plant gives its energy to the animal." Repeat this for the next animal.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A farmer grows wheat. A rat eats the wheat. A snake eats the rat. Identify the links and the direction of energy flow.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the producer (Wheat).
- Step 2: Identify the primary consumer (Rat).
- Step 3: Identify the secondary consumer (Snake).
- Step 4: Draw arrows showing energy flow: Wheat -> Rat -> Snake.
REASONING: Energy flows from the source (plant) to the herbivore (rat) and finally to the predator (snake).
ANSWER: Wheat (Producer) -> Rat (Primary Consumer) -> Snake (Secondary Consumer).
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: In a garden, a caterpillar eats a leaf, and a bird eats the caterpillar. How many trophic levels are there?
STEPS:
- Step 1: List the organisms: Leaf, Caterpillar, Bird.
- Step 2: Assign level 1 to the producer (Leaf).
- Step 3: Assign level 2 to the first consumer (Caterpillar).
- Step 4: Assign level 3 to the second consumer (Bird).
REASONING: Each step in the sequence is a trophic level, moving sequentially from the producer upward.
ANSWER: There are 3 trophic levels.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: If a field has 100 kg of grain, and a mouse eats 5 kg, how much energy is theoretically passed to the owl that eats the mouse?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Define the chain: Grain -> Mouse -> Owl.
- Step 2: Recognize the mouse is the link between grain and owl.
- Step 3: Note that energy transfer is not 100% efficient.
- Step 4: Conclude that the owl receives significantly less than the 5 kg consumed by the mouse.
REASONING: Energy is lost at each step due to metabolic processes.
ANSWER: The owl receives much less than 5 kg worth of energy because the mouse used most of it.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If all the plants in a forest were removed, what would happen to the food chain? Explain your reasoning.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Define the role of plants as producers.
- Step 2: Analyze the impact on primary consumers (herbivores).
- Step 3: Analyze the impact on secondary consumers (carnivores).
- Step 4: Conclude the collapse of the chain.
ANSWER: The entire chain would collapse because the primary source of energy (plants) is gone, meaning herbivores would starve, and subsequently, carnivores would have no food.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Compare the energy source of a plant to the energy source of a tiger. How are they different?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the plant's energy source (Sunlight).
- Step 2: Identify the tiger's energy source (Other animals/food).
- Step 3: Explain the difference (Production vs. Consumption).
ANSWER: Plants produce their own energy by capturing sunlight (producers), while tigers must consume other animals (carnivores) to get the energy previously stored by those animals.
DIFFICULTY: hard
