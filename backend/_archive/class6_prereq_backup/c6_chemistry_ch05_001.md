## DEFINITION
A physical change is a change in the form, shape, size, or state of a substance where no new material is created. The original substance remains the same, even though it may look different.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to classify various common changes as "physical" by demonstrating that the substance remains unchanged in its identity. They should be able to provide at least two examples where a material's appearance changes but its composition does not.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers identifying and defining physical changes through observable characteristics like shape and state. It does not cover chemical reactions, molecular bonding, or thermodynamics.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you have a crisp, clean sheet of paper. If you crumple it into a ball or tear it into tiny confetti pieces, has the paper suddenly turned into wood or plastic? Of course not; it is still just paper, even though its shape and size are totally different. Now, think about an ice cube melting in a glass of water; the solid ice turns into liquid water, but it is still water—not a new substance. To understand a physical change, always ask yourself: "Did the material turn into something else, or is it still the same stuff I started with?" If it is still the same, you are looking at a physical change.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
If you stretch a rubber band, is it still a rubber band?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Yes, it is still a rubber band. A wrong answer suggests the student confuses "shape change" with "substance change," failing to realize the material identity remains constant.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
When you dissolve salt in water, does the salt disappear and become a new substance, or does it stay as salt?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The salt stays as salt, it just changes its state (by mixing/dissolving). A wrong answer reveals the misconception that if you cannot see the solid particles, they must have vanished or changed into something else.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
You have a metal wire. You bend it into a circle. Has a new substance been formed?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
No. Step 1: Identify the material (metal wire). Step 2: Observe the change (bending). Step 3: Check if the material identity changed (it is still metal). Step 4: Conclude it is a physical change. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks physical manipulation equals a chemical creation.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare tearing a piece of paper to cutting a piece of wood. How are they similar in terms of physical change?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Both involve changing the size and shape of the original material without creating a new substance. A wrong answer indicates difficulty in generalizing the concept of "physical change" across different types of materials.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
If a boy breaks a glass bottle, is this a physical change? Justify your answer.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, it is a physical change because the glass shards are still glass. The substance has not changed its identity; only its size and shape have changed. A wrong answer implies the student equates "destruction" or "damage" with "chemical change."
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Think of a real-world object in your kitchen. Describe how you could perform a physical change on it.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
An example would be crushing a dry biscuit into crumbs. The answer is valid if the student identifies that while the biscuit looks different, it is still the same material. A poor answer would suggest something like "burning a match," which is a chemical change.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that if a change is "permanent" or "drastic" (like breaking an object), it cannot be physical. They mistakenly assume that physical changes must be easily "reversible," like melting ice. This leads them to think that breaking a glass or cutting wood is a chemical change because they cannot "un-break" the items.
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to name the material before and after the change (e.g., "Glass before, glass after"). Remind them that a physical change is about the *identity* of the stuff, not whether it is easy to put back together. Use the example of a jigsaw puzzle: cutting the picture into pieces is a physical change because it is still the same picture, regardless of whether you glue it back.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A shopkeeper has a 5 kg block of ice. He cuts it into 5 smaller pieces, each weighing 1 kg. Has the substance changed?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify initial state (5 kg ice).
- Step 2: Identify process (cutting).
- Step 3: Identify final state (five 1 kg pieces of ice).
- Step 4: Compare substance identity (ice remains ice).
- Step 5: Conclude it is a physical change.
REASONING: The mass is conserved, and the molecular identity of the ice has not been altered; only the spatial distribution of the mass has changed.
ANSWER: No, it is still ice; therefore, it is a physical change.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A student takes a 2-metre long cotton cloth and cuts it into 10 squares of 20 cm each. Is this a physical change?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify initial material (cotton cloth).
- Step 2: Identify process (cutting).
- Step 3: Compare identity (cotton fibers remain cotton fibers).
- Step 4: Evaluate if new matter was created (no).
- Step 5: Conclude classification.
REASONING: Cutting changes only the dimensions (area/shape) of the cotton fabric, not the chemical makeup of the cloth itself.
ANSWER: Yes, it is a physical change.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: You have a 100g lump of clay. You mold it into a bird shape. Later, you squash it back into a lump. Are both actions physical changes?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Analyze action 1 (molding clay into bird).
- Step 2: Analyze substance (still clay).
- Step 3: Analyze action 2 (squashing bird into lump).
- Step 4: Analyze substance (still clay).
- Step 5: Compare both actions against the definition.
REASONING: Both actions only alter the physical shape of the clay. Since the identity of the clay does not change in either step, both are physical changes.
ANSWER: Yes, both are physical changes.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If you grind a piece of chalk into powder to write on the board, explain why this is a physical change even though you cannot turn the powder back into a solid piece of chalk easily.
COMPETENCY: Justifying and comparing
STEPS:
- Step 1: Define what the material is before (chalk).
- Step 2: Define what the material is after (chalk powder).
- Step 3: Check if the material identity changed (no).
- Step 4: Conclude.
ANSWER: It is a physical change because the powder is still made of the same chalk material. The difficulty of reversing the action does not change the fact that no new substance was created.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A friend says that burning a piece of paper is a physical change because "it's still ash, just smaller." Critique this statement.
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the claim (burning is a physical change).
- Step 2: Analyze the result of burning (paper becomes smoke, ash, and heat).
- Step 3: Determine if paper identity is preserved (no, new substances were created).
- Step 4: Correct the misconception.
ANSWER: The friend is incorrect. Burning creates new substances (ash and gases) that were not present in the original paper. A physical change would only happen if the paper were torn, not burned.
DIFFICULTY: hard
