## DEFINITION
Character analysis is the process of examining a character's traits, motivations, and behaviors to understand their role and personality within a story. It involves looking at what a character says, does, and how they interact with others to form a complete picture of who they are.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
analyze
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify at least three specific personality traits of a character and provide one piece of evidence from the story for each trait.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers identifying and analyzing character traits based on actions and speech. It does not cover character arcs (how a character changes over time) or the analysis of minor, background characters.
## WALKTHROUGH
Think of your favorite cartoon or movie hero—how do you know they are "brave" or "funny"? You probably point to a specific moment where they helped someone or told a joke. To analyze a character like Dr. Dolittle, start by asking: "What is this character's main goal?" Then, look for "clues" in the story: What do they do when things get hard? How do they treat others? By connecting these clues to a trait name (like 'compassionate'), you move from just reading to truly understanding the person inside the story.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What are 'character traits'?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Character traits are the specific qualities or personality features that describe a character, such as being kind, curious, or funny. If a student answers that traits are just "what the character looks like," it reveals they are confusing physical appearance with personality.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If Dr. Dolittle decides to learn animal languages instead of treating humans, what does this tell us about his character?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
It shows he is curious and open-minded because he is willing to try a new, unconventional path. If a student says he is "just weird," it shows they are judging the character without analyzing the motivation behind his actions.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A character gives away their last piece of bread to a hungry stray dog. How would you label this character?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
You would label this character as 'compassionate' or 'kind.' A student who guesses "hungry" is focusing on the situation rather than the character's internal quality.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the character of a doctor who loves animals to a person who ignores them. What is the difference in their 'trait'?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The first is 'compassionate' or 'empathetic,' while the second is 'indifferent' or 'unconcerned.' A wrong answer shows the student is not yet able to contrast opposing personality types.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it enough to say "Dr. Dolittle is nice"? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, because "nice" is too general; a deep analysis requires specific traits like 'compassionate' or 'curious' backed by evidence. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks simple labels are sufficient for analysis.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a list of three traits for a character you know, and for each, write one action that proves it.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The answer should include a trait (e.g., 'brave') and an action (e.g., 'standing up to a bully'). A poor answer would list traits without any supporting evidence from a story.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often confuse 'feelings' with 'traits.' A feeling is temporary (like feeling sad because of a rainstorm), but a trait is a lasting part of a personality (like being 'cheerful' even when it rains). For example, a student might say, "Dr. Dolittle is happy because he is talking to a parrot," instead of identifying his 'curiosity' as a permanent trait.
## ON_FAILURE
If the student confuses feelings with traits, ask them: "Does the character feel this way for one minute, or is this how they act every day?" Help them identify the permanent behavior by asking, "What word describes a person who always wants to learn new things?" Then, guide them to label that as a 'trait' rather than a temporary 'feeling.'

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Analyze the character of the mahout from the text.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify what the mahout does (e.g., gives Gajaraj a bath, feeds him).
- step 2: Think of a word for this action (e.g., 'kind' or 'responsible').
- step 3: Check if the text supports this (e.g., he served food daily).
- step 4: Formulate the analysis: "The mahout is responsible because he cares for Gajaraj's daily needs."
REASONING: These steps move from observation to labeling to evidence, ensuring the analysis is grounded in the story.
ANSWER: The mahout is a 'kind caretaker' because he consistently attends to the elephant's physical needs and notices when he is unhappy.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Identify one trait for the farmer from the text.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the farmer's final action regarding the dog.
- step 2: Note that he lets the dog go to his friend.
- step 3: Choose a trait word: 'understanding' or 'kind'.
- step 4: State the evidence: He removed the rope because he saw the dog was sad.
REASONING: By linking the action of removing the rope to the motivation of empathy, we define his character.
ANSWER: The farmer is 'understanding' because he prioritizes the dog's happiness over his own possession of the pet.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Compare Gajaraj and Buntee as friends.
STEPS:
- step 1: List Gajaraj's actions (playing, splashing, waiting).
- step 2: List Buntee's actions (staying, licking the farmer, running back).
- step 3: Identify the shared trait (loyalty).
- step 4: Verify with evidence (neither would eat when apart).
- step 5: Conclude why they are 'best friends'.
REASONING: Comparing two characters helps see the shared trait of 'loyalty' that defines their relationship.
ANSWER: Both are 'loyal' because they refused to eat when separated, proving their bond was essential to their well-being.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If a new character enters a story and immediately shares their toys with everyone, what trait would you assign to them?
COMPETENCY: Inferencing
STEPS:
- step 1: Look at the action (sharing toys).
- step 2: Determine the intent (making others happy).
- step 3: Match intent to a trait (generous).
ANSWER: The trait is 'generous' because the action shows a willingness to put others' needs before their own belongings.
DIFFICULTY: easy
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Why is "Polynesia" considered an intelligent character in the Dr. Dolittle story?
COMPETENCY: Evidence-based reasoning
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify her role in the story (she suggests the career change).
- step 2: Analyze the impact of her suggestion (she guides the Doctor).
- step 3: Determine if the action requires intelligence (yes, she sees a better path).
- step 4: Explain that her ability to offer advice makes her 'intelligent'.
ANSWER: She is intelligent because she recognizes the Doctor's true passion and offers a solution that changes his life, showing she thinks ahead and understands others' needs.
DIFFICULTY: medium
