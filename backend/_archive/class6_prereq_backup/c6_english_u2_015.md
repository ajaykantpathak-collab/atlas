## DEFINITION
Poetic devices are creative tools used by poets to make language more interesting, musical, or humorous. They include repetition (re-using words for effect), rhyme (matching end sounds), and word play (using puns or double meanings).
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify examples of repetition, rhyme, and word play in a short verse and explain why the poet chose these specific tools to create humor.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the identification and simple application of repetition, rhyme, and word play in humorous poetry. It excludes free verse, complex meter, and other literary devices like metaphors or personification.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever noticed how a song gets stuck in your head because it keeps repeating a catchy chorus? Poets do the same thing with words to make us laugh or feel the rhythm. Start by reading a poem out loud—if it sounds like a melody, that’s rhyme. If a word or a funny sound pops up twice or three times, that’s repetition. Finally, look for words that could mean two things at once; that’s word play. By finding these patterns, you stop seeing a poem as just "lines on a page" and start seeing it as a puzzle designed to make you smile.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What do we call the technique where a poet uses the same word or phrase multiple times to emphasize a point or make the reader laugh?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
It is called repetition. A wrong answer suggests the student is confusing it with rhyme, which involves sound, or word play, which involves meaning, rather than the act of repeating the actual text.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If a poem uses the words "cat" and "hat" at the end of two lines, what device is the poet using? Explain why this makes the poem feel "light."
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The poet is using rhyme. This creates a musical or bouncy quality, which makes the poem feel playful rather than like a serious textbook passage. A wrong answer indicates the student does not understand how sound patterns affect the mood of a poem.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Look at this line: "The baker was a kneady person because he always needed dough." Identify the word play (pun) and explain the double meaning of "kneady/needy" or "dough."
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The word play is on "kneady" (sounds like "needy") and "dough" (means both bread mixture and money). The student must link the baker's job to these double meanings. A wrong answer shows an inability to distinguish between standard definitions and clever word associations.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Analyze this: "He ran fast, he ran far, he ran until he hit a star." Why does the author repeat the phrase "he ran"?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The author repeats "he ran" to show the character's exhaustion or to create a comedic, panting rhythm. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks it is just a "mistake" or an accident, ignoring the deliberate nature of poetic craft.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate this statement: "Rhyme is the most important part of every poem." Do you agree? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, because while rhyme creates music, humor often comes from word play or the surprise of repetition. A wrong answer shows the student believes all poems must rhyme, failing to recognize that devices serve different purposes.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a one-line phrase using word play about a gardener.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Example: "The gardener was outstanding because he stood out in his field." A poor answer would be a simple sentence without a double meaning, showing the student did not grasp the "play" part of word play.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think "rhyme" just means any two words that sound vaguely similar, like "cat" and "car," or they think "repetition" must happen in every single line. This stems from a lack of focus on the *exact* ending sound or the *deliberate* choice of the poet.
## ON_FAILURE
If a student struggles, ask them to clap out the syllables of a line. Then, ask them to isolate the very last sound of the line. Explain that "rhyme" requires the *final* vowel and consonant sounds to match perfectly. Practice with simple pairs like "ball/tall" before moving to more complex poetry.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Identify the devices in this verse: "The frog was green, the frog was lean, he sat on a log and was never seen."
STEPS:
- step 1: Read the verse aloud to find the rhythm and matches.
- step 2: Look for repeated phrases. "The frog was" is repeated. This is repetition.
- step 3: Look for end sounds. "Green/lean/seen" match. This is rhyme.
- step 4: Check for word play. There are no puns or double meanings here.
- step 5: Conclude that the poem uses repetition and rhyme.
REASONING: By isolating each device, we see how the poet builds the poem's structure.
ANSWER: The poem uses repetition ("the frog was") and rhyme ("green/lean/seen").
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Create a funny line about a school teacher using word play.
STEPS:
- step 1: Pick a subject related to a teacher (books, grades, rulers).
- step 2: Think of a word with two meanings (e.g., "ruler" as a tool and a leader).
- step 3: Write a sentence using both meanings: "My teacher is a great ruler because she carries a wooden one!"
- step 4: Analyze if it fits the "humor" goal. Yes, it plays on the two definitions of ruler.
REASONING: Connecting the literal object to a personality trait creates the "clever" effect required for word play.
ANSWER: "My teacher is a great ruler because she always carries a wooden one!"
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Compare how repetition in a serious story differs from a funny poem.
STEPS:
- step 1: Consider a serious story. Repetition there adds gravity or sadness.
- step 2: Consider a funny poem. Repetition there adds energy or absurdity.
- step 3: Compare the effect on the reader. Serious = tension; Funny = amusement.
- step 4: Write the comparison.
REASONING: Poetic devices are not fixed; their impact changes based on the author's intent.
ANSWER: In a serious story, repetition makes things feel heavy or important. In a funny poem, repeating an action (like a frog hopping) makes it feel silly and exaggerated.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If a poem says, "The clock talked to the sock," is this word play? Why?
COMPETENCY: Critical Thinking
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the key words (clock/talked/sock).
- step 2: Check for rhyme (clock/sock). That is rhyme, not word play.
- step 3: Check for puns or double meanings. There is none.
- step 4: Conclude it is rhyme, not word play.
ANSWER: No, it is not word play. It uses rhyme ("clock" and "sock"), but it does not use double meanings or clever word usage to create a pun.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Why might a poet choose to repeat a word five times instead of just twice in a poem about a clown?
COMPETENCY: Analytical Reasoning
STEPS:
- step 1: Analyze the subject (clown). Clowns are associated with silliness and exaggeration.
- step 2: Analyze the effect of repetition. Repeating a word creates a rhythm.
- step 3: Relate the two. Repeating five times is "over the top," which fits a clown's personality.
- step 4: Formulate the conclusion.
ANSWER: The poet uses five repetitions to make the action feel silly and exaggerated, fitting the theme of a clown. Repeating it only twice might be too subtle; five times creates a comedic, chaotic effect.
DIFFICULTY: hard
