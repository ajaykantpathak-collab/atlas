## DEFINITION
A pinhole camera is a simple light-tight box with a tiny hole on one side that forms an inverted, real image of an object on the opposite screen by allowing light to travel in straight lines. It demonstrates the principle of rectilinear propagation of light without the use of lenses.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to draw a ray diagram showing how light from the top and bottom of an object crosses at the pinhole to form an inverted image. They must also be able to explain that the image is inverted because light travels in straight lines.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the basic structure and image formation mechanics of a pinhole camera. It excludes lens optics, aperture adjustments, exposure times, and digital sensor technology.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever wondered how a tiny hole in a dark box can "capture" a picture of the world outside? Start by imagining a dark room with a small hole in the window curtain; you might notice a faint, upside-down image of the outside scene on the opposite wall. To build a pinhole camera, you essentially create a miniature version of this room using a light-tight box. The key insight is that light travels in straight lines: light from the top of an object goes through the hole and hits the bottom of the screen, while light from the bottom goes through and hits the top. This crossing path is exactly why the resulting image appears flipped or "inverted."
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What happens to the image if you make the pinhole very large?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The image becomes blurry or disappears entirely because too much light enters from different angles, causing the rays to overlap. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks a larger hole acts like a "bigger window," not realizing that the sharpness of the image depends on the pinhole restricting light to a single, straight path.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you hold a pinhole camera to look at a tree, why is the image of the top of the tree at the bottom of the screen?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Because light travels in a straight line from the top of the tree, through the pinhole, and continues in that same straight path to the bottom of the screen. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks the "camera flips it," missing the physical fact that the path of the light ray itself is responsible for the inversion.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If an object is moved closer to the pinhole, what happens to the size of the image on the screen?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The image becomes larger. As the object moves closer, the angle of the light rays passing through the pinhole increases, causing the projection to spread over a larger area on the screen. A wrong answer reveals the student hasn't visualized the geometry of the light paths converging at the hole.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Why is the image formed by a pinhole camera called a "real" image?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
It is real because the light rays actually meet on the screen to form the image, rather than just appearing to originate from behind a mirror. A wrong answer shows confusion between "real images" (which can be caught on a screen) and "virtual images" (which cannot).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Can a pinhole camera work if the box is not perfectly light-tight?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, because extra light entering from other gaps would wash out the faint image formed by the pinhole. Students who say "yes" do not understand that the image is a low-intensity projection that requires a dark environment to be visible.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple experiment to prove that light travels in straight lines using a pinhole camera.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Place two objects of different colors at different heights in front of the pinhole; if the images appear inverted and in the same relative order, it proves the rays crossed. A poor answer lacks a clear way to track individual light paths or fails to connect the inversion to the straight-line path.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe the pinhole "turns" the light around or that there is a mirror inside the box to flip the image. This occurs because they are accustomed to how lenses in our eyes or magnifying glasses work and find it counter-intuitive that a simple hole can perform such a complex task.
## ON_FAILURE
Take a cardboard tube and hold a burning candle in front of it in a dark room. Ask the student to trace the path of the light from the candle flame to the back of the tube with a string. Show them that the string must pass through the center to reach the opposite side, physically demonstrating the straight-line "crossing" effect.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A student builds a box 20 cm long. A candle is placed 20 cm in front of the pinhole. If the candle is 10 cm tall, how tall will the image be?
STEPS:
- Identify: Object distance (u) = 20 cm, Screen distance (v) = 20 cm, Object height (h) = 10 cm.
- Method: Use the principle of similar triangles. The ratio of image height to object height equals the ratio of screen distance to object distance.
- Substitute: Image height / 10 cm = 20 cm / 20 cm.
- Compute: Image height / 10 = 1. Therefore, Image height = 10 cm.
- Interpret: Because the distances are equal, the image is the same size as the object.
REASONING: Since the light rays travel in straight lines, the triangle formed by the object and the pinhole is identical to the triangle formed by the pinhole and the image. This proportional relationship allows us to calculate image size without complex optics.
ANSWER: 10 cm
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: The same student moves the candle to 40 cm from the pinhole. The box remains 20 cm long. How tall is the image now?
STEPS:
- Identify: Object distance (u) = 40 cm, Screen distance (v) = 20 cm, Object height (h) = 10 cm.
- Method: Similar triangles ratio (Image height / Object height = v / u).
- Substitute: Image height / 10 = 20 / 40.
- Compute: Image height / 10 = 0.5. Image height = 5 cm.
- Interpret: Doubling the distance of the object while keeping the screen distance constant halves the image size.
REASONING: By changing the object distance, the angle of the light rays entering the pinhole changes, effectively compressing the image on the screen.
ANSWER: 5 cm
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A student sees a tree 10 meters away. Their pinhole camera is 0.5 meters deep. If the tree is 20 meters tall, how tall is the image?
STEPS:
- Identify: Tree distance (u) = 10 m, Box depth (v) = 0.5 m, Tree height (h) = 20 m.
- Method: Ratio calculation (Image height = Object height * (v / u)).
- Substitute: Image height = 20 * (0.5 / 10).
- Compute: 0.5 / 10 = 0.05.
- Compute: 20 * 0.05 = 1.0 m.
- Verify: The image is significantly smaller because the object is very far away compared to the depth of the camera.
REASONING: This applies the same geometric logic as the previous examples, showing how a camera captures a massive object into a small, portable box.
ANSWER: 1 meter
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You are looking at a flag waving in the wind. In your pinhole camera, the flag is upside down. If you cut a second hole near the first one, what would you see on the screen?
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction
STEPS:
- Understand: A pinhole camera relies on a single point of light to form a distinct image.
- Method: Consider how multiple light sources from the same object would interact.
- Apply: A second hole would act as a second pinhole, projecting a second, overlapping, and slightly shifted image.
- Interpret: This would make the final image appear blurry or doubled.
ANSWER: You would see two overlapping images, making the picture appear blurry or "ghosted" because light from the same part of the flag is now hitting two different spots on the screen.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Why does a pinhole camera not work effectively in a very brightly lit room, even if the object is also bright?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- Understand: The contrast between the image and the background is key.
- Method: Analyze light interference.
- Apply: Light from the room would enter the box through the walls or around the edges, filling the box with light.
- Interpret: The "real" image formed by the pinhole is very faint; bright light from the surroundings would drown it out.
ANSWER: A pinhole camera requires a dark environment to function because the image produced by the tiny hole is very low-intensity. If the room is bright, the "noise" of the ambient light will overpower the faint light rays passing through the pinhole, making the image invisible.
DIFFICULTY: hard
