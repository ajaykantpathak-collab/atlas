## DEFINITION
An angle is the amount of rotation between two rays that share a common starting point, called the vertex. Angles are measured in degrees (°), where a full turn is 360°.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can identify the vertex and arms of an angle, classify it by type (acute, right, obtuse, straight, reflex), and measure it accurately using a protractor.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the identification, classification, and measurement of angles using a protractor. It excludes radian measures, trigonometric calculations, and angle bisector constructions beyond basic paper folding.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are opening a pair of scissors; as the blades move apart, the space between them grows—that "opening" is an angle. To start, find the point where the two rays join (the vertex) and the two lines extending from it (the arms). To measure this, place the center of your protractor exactly on the vertex and align one arm with the 0° line. Read the number where the second arm crosses the scale, making sure you use the correct scale based on which side the 0° line is on. The key insight is that the size of the angle depends on the amount of rotation, not the length of the arms.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What do we call the common starting point where two rays meet to form an angle?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
It is called the vertex. A wrong answer (like "corner" or "point") shows the student is not using the correct geometric vocabulary needed for formal construction.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If two different angles have the same degree measure, but one has very long arms and the other has very short arms, which angle is larger?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
They are equal. A wrong answer reveals the common misconception that longer arms make an angle "bigger" because the student is confusing physical length with the measure of rotation.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If an angle measures 95°, how would you classify it: acute, right, or obtuse?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
It is an obtuse angle. A wrong answer suggests the student hasn't memorized the ranges (Acute < 90°, Right = 90°, Obtuse > 90° and < 180°).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Why do we have two sets of numbers (scales) on a standard protractor?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
To measure angles opening in either direction (left or right). A wrong answer shows the student doesn't understand how to align the protractor with the base ray.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Can a reflex angle be measured using a standard semi-circle protractor? If so, how?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, by measuring the interior angle and subtracting it from 360°, or by measuring the "gap" and adding 180°. A wrong answer indicates difficulty in visualizing the full 360° rotation.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Draw a rough sketch of an angle that is exactly 45°. How would you explain to a friend that this is half of a right angle?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The answer should describe that since a right angle is 90°, and 45° is 90° divided by 2, they represent the same "turn." An answer that focuses only on drawing without explaining the relationship lacks conceptual depth.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think the size of an angle changes if you extend the length of the arms. For example, they might claim that a 30° angle with 5cm arms is smaller than a 30° angle with 10cm arms. This happens because they perceive the visual space between the endpoints of the arms rather than the amount of rotation at the vertex.
## ON_FAILURE
If a student thinks longer arms make a larger angle, have them draw two 30° angles using a protractor: one with short arms and one with long arms. Then, ask them to place the vertex of the smaller one exactly over the vertex of the larger one. Show them that the arms overlap perfectly, which proves the "opening" or rotation is identical regardless of how far the lines extend.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Vidya opens a book on her desk such that the cover makes an angle of 60°. If she opens it further by another 25°, what is the final angle measure?
STEPS:
- Identify initial angle: 60°.
- Identify additional rotation: 25°.
- Determine operation: Since she is opening it "further," we add the rotations.
- Calculate: 60° + 25° = 85°.
- Interpret: The new angle is 85°, which is still an acute angle.
REASONING: Angles represent cumulative rotation; therefore, adding the degrees represents the total turn from the starting position.
ANSWER: 85°
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A carpenter needs to cut a wooden slab to create a right angle corner. He has already marked a 35° angle. How many more degrees does he need to add to reach a right angle?
STEPS:
- Identify the goal: Right angle = 90°.
- Identify the current mark: 35°.
- Determine operation: Find the difference between the goal and current mark (90° - 35°).
- Calculate: 90 - 35 = 55.
- Interpret: He needs to add 55° to reach exactly 90°.
REASONING: A right angle is a fixed reference of 90°; subtracting the existing part leaves the missing rotation required to complete the right angle.
ANSWER: 55°
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A clock shows the time 3:00. What is the angle between the hands? If the minute hand moves to 4:00, what is the new angle?
STEPS:
- Identify total degrees in a circle: 360°.
- Calculate degrees per hour (12 hours): 360° / 12 = 30° per hour.
- Identify angle at 3:00: 3 hours × 30° = 90°.
- Identify angle at 4:00: 4 hours × 30° = 120°.
- Interpret: The angle increased from 90° (right angle) to 120° (obtuse angle).
REASONING: The clock face is a full circle; dividing 360° by the 12 hour-marks gives the unit rotation for each hour.
ANSWER: 120°
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You are given two angles, ∠AOB and ∠PQR. If you superimpose ∠AOB onto ∠PQR and the arms overlap perfectly, what can you say about their measures? What if they don't overlap?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- Understand superimposition: Placing one object directly over another.
- Compare: If they match perfectly, they are equal in size.
- Analyze non-match: If one is wider, it is larger; if narrower, it is smaller.
- Interpret: The measure is independent of the arm length, only the rotation matters.
ANSWER: If they overlap perfectly, the angles are equal in size. If they do not, the one with the wider opening (greater rotation) is the larger angle.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A student measures an angle and says it is 150°. Another student says it is 30° because they read the other scale. Is it possible for both to be correct? How do you check which one is the correct measure?
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction
STEPS:
- Analyze the angle: Look at the visual opening.
- Classify: 150° is obtuse (wide); 30° is acute (sharp).
- Compare with visual: If the angle looks "blunt" (wider than a corner), it must be 150°.
- Correct: If the angle is clearly wide, the 30° reading is an error caused by using the wrong scale on the protractor.
ANSWER: No, only one is correct. You must classify the angle visually first. If the angle is clearly wider than a right angle (90°), the 150° reading is correct, and the student who read 30° used the wrong scale.
DIFFICULTY: hard
