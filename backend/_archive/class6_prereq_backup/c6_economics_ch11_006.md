## DEFINITION
Money in daily life refers to the cycle of earning (gaining income through work), spending (using money for goods and services), and saving (keeping money aside for future needs). It is the practical way we manage resources to meet our daily requirements and long-term goals.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to describe how their family earns, spends, and saves money, and explain why setting aside a portion of income is necessary for future security.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers the basic cycle of personal finance (earning, spending, saving) and simple budgeting. It does not cover complex investment strategies, interest rates, taxation, or institutional credit systems.
## WALKTHROUGH
Think about the last time you bought a pencil or a snack; where did that money come from, and why didn't you spend all your money at once? Start by identifying how the adults in your household bring money home—is it a salary, a daily wage, or profit from a shop? Next, distinguish between 'needs' (like food) and 'wants' (like extra toys), and see how your family manages these through spending. Finally, understand that 'saving' is like a rainy-day fund that helps you when you have an emergency or a big future goal, like school fees. The key insight is that money is a limited resource that we must manage carefully to balance our needs today with our safety tomorrow.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What are the three main ways money flows through our daily lives?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The three ways are earning, spending, and saving. A wrong answer suggests the student hasn't grasped the basic cycle of personal finance and may be confusing "money" with just "wealth" or "coins."
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If a person spends every single rupee they earn each month, what problem might they face if they have a sudden medical emergency?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
They would have no savings to pay for the emergency, leading to financial distress. This shows the student understands the purpose of saving as a buffer for future uncertainty.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A shopkeeper earns profit from sales, while a teacher earns a monthly salary. How are these two types of earnings similar?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Both are forms of "earning" resulting from economic activity. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks only one type of work counts as "earning" money.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the difference between saving money for a bicycle (a specific goal) and saving money for an emergency (a general buffer).
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Saving for a bike is for a "want" or a planned purchase, while an emergency fund is for unpredictable events. A wrong answer shows the student does not distinguish between planned expenditure and contingency planning.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it better to save money only after spending on all your wants? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, it is better to prioritize savings first, because if you wait until after spending on wants, you might have nothing left. This tests the student's ability to evaluate financial habits critically.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a simple "budget" plan for a student who receives 100 rupees as pocket money for the week.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good plan allocates specific amounts for needs (e.g., pen), small wants (e.g., one chocolate), and a fixed portion for savings. A poor answer ignores saving entirely or spends more than the 100-rupee limit.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that "saving" is only for rich people who have "extra" money. They assume that if you earn just enough for food and rent, you cannot save. This is a misconception because saving is a habit of setting aside a small percentage, regardless of the income level, to build security over time.
## ON_FAILURE
1. Ask the student to list three small "wants" they bought last week. 2. Ask how much that cost in total. 3. Explain that even setting aside 1 rupee out of that total is a form of saving. 4. Emphasize that saving is about the "habit" of keeping a part, not the size of the amount.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A farmer sells his harvest for 5000 rupees. He spends 2000 on seeds for next season, 1000 on food/clothes, and keeps 2000 in a box. Identify the income, spending, and savings.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify total income (5000).
- step 2: Identify total spending (seeds + food/clothes = 3000).
- step 3: Identify savings (money kept in box = 2000).
- step 4: Verify that 2000 (savings) + 3000 (spending) = 5000 (income).
REASONING: Income must equal the sum of spending and savings. This demonstrates the basic balance of personal finance.
ANSWER: Income is 5000; Spending is 3000; Savings is 2000.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Riya gets 50 rupees a day. She spends 30 on lunch and 10 on a pen. How much does she save per week (7 days)?
STEPS:
- step 1: Calculate daily spending (30 + 10 = 40).
- step 2: Calculate daily savings (50 - 40 = 10).
- step 3: Multiply daily savings by 7 days (10 * 7).
- step 4: Calculate total weekly savings (70).
REASONING: By calculating daily habits first, we can predict long-term savings.
ANSWER: Riya saves 70 rupees per week.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A tailor earns 300 rupees a day. He wants to buy a new machine that costs 3000 rupees in 20 days. How much must he save daily?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the goal (3000 rupees).
- step 2: Identify the timeframe (20 days).
- step 3: Divide the goal by the timeframe (3000 / 20).
- step 4: Calculate the result (150).
- step 5: Check if 150 is less than his daily income of 300.
REASONING: Planning for a large purchase requires dividing the total cost by the available time to set a daily savings target.
ANSWER: He must save 150 rupees every day.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If a family stops "spending" entirely, what happens to the shopkeeper who sells them food?
COMPETENCY: Interdependence
STEPS:
- step 1: Define the family's spending as the shopkeeper's earning.
- step 2: Explain the connection between the two.
- step 3: Conclude that if spending stops, the shopkeeper cannot earn.
- step 4: Explain the impact on the shopkeeper's own cycle of saving/spending.
ANSWER: The shopkeeper would lose their income because one person's spending is another person's earning. This shows that all economic activities are linked.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Why is it risky to keep all your "savings" in a box at home instead of using a bank (if available)?
COMPETENCY: Critical Thinking
STEPS:
- step 1: List risks of home storage (theft, damage, fire).
- step 2: Identify the purpose of the bank (security).
- step 3: Compare safety of home vs. institution.
- step 4: Conclude why institutions are safer.
- step 5: Acknowledge that even saving at home is better than not saving at all.
ANSWER: Keeping money at home is risky because it can be stolen or lost. A bank provides a secure place, ensuring the money is there when you need it for emergencies.
DIFFICULTY: hard
