## DEFINITION
A bar graph is a visual display of data using rectangular bars of equal width, where the height or length of each bar corresponds to the frequency of a specific category. It allows for quick comparison of different groups within a set of data.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can create a correctly labeled bar graph from a frequency table using an appropriate scale and can extract specific values or make comparisons between categories from an existing bar graph.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the construction and interpretation of single vertical or horizontal bar graphs. It excludes histograms, stacked bar graphs, and double bar graphs.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine your teacher wants to know the favourite fruits of everyone in your class—how would you show this quickly without just listing names? You would count how many students like mangoes, apples, and bananas, then draw a bar for each fruit where the height matches the number of students who chose it. Start by drawing two lines (axes) that meet at a corner, labeling the bottom line with the fruits and the side line with numbers. Always use a consistent scale, like counting by 2s or 5s, to ensure your graph is accurate. By looking at the heights, you can instantly see which fruit is the most popular, turning a messy list into a clear picture.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What do the heights of the bars in a bar graph represent?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The height represents the frequency or the total count of the category it describes. A wrong answer like "the size of the object" reveals that the student is confusing the physical drawing with the mathematical data it represents.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you have data ranging from 0 to 50, why might you choose a scale where 1 unit equals 5 instead of 1 unit equals 1?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Using 1 unit = 5 makes the graph smaller and easier to draw on a single page, whereas 1 unit = 1 would require a paper 50 units tall. A wrong answer suggests the student does not understand that the scale is a tool for fitting data into a manageable space.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
In a bar graph showing the number of books read by 5 students, if the bar for 'Rohan' is twice as high as the bar for 'Meera', what does this tell you?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
It means Rohan read exactly twice as many books as Meera. If a student says "Rohan read more," they are missing the quantitative relationship that the scale provides.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Why must the bars in a bar graph have equal widths and equal spacing?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
To ensure the comparison is fair; if one bar is wider, it might visually mislead the viewer into thinking that category is "larger" or "more important." A student who misses this likely views the graph as an artistic drawing rather than a precise mathematical tool.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
If a bar graph's vertical axis starts at 0 and goes up by intervals of 10, but the highest value is 45, where should the top of the bar for 45 stop?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
It should stop exactly halfway between the marks for 40 and 50. A student failing this likely struggles with understanding how to represent values that fall between marked intervals on an axis.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a plan for a bar graph showing the number of students who walk, cycle, or take a bus to school. What are the three essential parts you must include?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The essential parts are: labels on the horizontal axis (categories), labels on the vertical axis (frequency/scale), and a clear title. A poor answer that omits labels indicates the student does not understand that a graph is a communication tool, not just a set of bars.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often start the vertical axis at a number other than zero or use inconsistent spacing between numbers (e.g., 0, 5, 20, 25). This makes the graph look "stretched" or "shrunken" in parts, which distorts the data and makes it impossible to compare the bars accurately.
## ON_FAILURE
If a student makes this error, have them redraw the axis using a ruler to ensure gaps between tick marks (e.g., 0, 5, 10, 15) are exactly equal in length. Tell them: "The distance between 0 and 5 must be the same length as the distance between 5 and 10, otherwise your 'ruler' is broken." Check if the first line starts at zero to ensure the full height of the bar is captured.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A shopkeeper sold the following number of pens over 5 days: Monday (20), Tuesday (35), Wednesday (25), Thursday (40), Friday (30). Create a scale and determine the height of each bar.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the range (20 to 40) and choose a scale where 1 unit = 5 pens.
- Step 2: Calculate heights: Monday: 20/5 = 4 units; Tuesday: 35/5 = 7 units; Wednesday: 25/5 = 5 units; Thursday: 40/5 = 8 units; Friday: 30/5 = 6 units.
- Step 3: Draw the horizontal axis with 5 categories (days) and vertical axis with a 0-8 unit scale.
- Step 4: Draw bars for each day to their calculated height.
- Step 5: Verify that the gaps between bars are equal and labels are clear.
REASONING: By dividing the total count by the scale, we translate the "real world" amount into a "drawing" length that fits perfectly on the graph paper.
ANSWER: Heights are 4, 7, 5, 8, and 6 units respectively.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: In a classroom, 12 students like tea, 16 like coffee, and 8 like milk. Using a scale of 1 unit = 2 students, draw the bars.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Divide each frequency by the scale (2): Tea (12/2 = 6), Coffee (16/2 = 8), Milk (8/2 = 4).
- Step 2: Draw the axes, labeling "Drinks" on the bottom and "Number of Students" on the side.
- Step 3: Mark the vertical axis with 0, 2, 4, 6, 8.
- Step 4: Draw the bars: Tea (height 6), Coffee (height 8), Milk (height 4).
- Step 5: Check that all bars have the same width.
REASONING: The scale allows us to represent larger numbers using smaller, manageable units while keeping the ratio between them accurate.
ANSWER: Bars of height 6, 8, and 4 units.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A student recorded the heights of 4 plants as 10cm, 25cm, 35cm, and 45cm. They want to use a scale of 1 unit = 5cm. How many units high should each bar be, and what is the total height of all bars combined?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Convert each height to units: 10/5 = 2, 25/5 = 5, 35/5 = 7, 45/5 = 9.
- Step 2: Sum the units: 2 + 5 + 7 + 9 = 23 units.
- Step 3: Draw the graph with these specific unit heights.
- Step 4: Total height represents the sum of the frequencies.
REASONING: Converting raw measurements into units allows us to use a consistent scale for different data points, and summing units helps in comparing the cumulative growth.
ANSWER: Heights: 2, 5, 7, 9 units; Total: 23 units.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You are given a bar graph where the vertical axis has no numbers, but you see that the bar for "Cricket" is twice as high as the bar for "Hockey". If 10 students like Hockey, how many students like Cricket? Explain your reasoning.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the relationship (Cricket = 2 x Hockey).
- Step 2: Substitute the known value (Hockey = 10).
- Step 3: Calculate (2 x 10 = 20).
ANSWER: 20 students. Since the bar graph shows that the frequency of Cricket is twice that of Hockey based on the relative heights, and we know 10 students like Hockey, the calculation 2 * 10 gives us 20.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Look at a bar graph comparing the price of 1kg of onions in four different cities: City A (₹40), City B (₹60), City C (₹30), and City D (₹50). If you want to draw this, why might a scale of 1 unit = ₹10 be better than a scale of 1 unit = ₹20?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate unit heights for 1 unit = ₹10: (4, 6, 3, 5).
- Step 2: Calculate unit heights for 1 unit = ₹20: (2, 3, 1.5, 2.5).
- Step 3: Compare clarity: 1.5 units is harder to draw and read accurately than 3 units.
ANSWER: A scale of 1 unit = ₹10 is better because it results in whole numbers for all values (4, 6, 3, 5 units), whereas 1 unit = ₹20 results in "half-units" (1.5 and 2.5) which are prone to drawing errors and make the graph harder to read precisely.
DIFFICULTY: hard
