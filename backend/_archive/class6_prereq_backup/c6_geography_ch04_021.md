## DEFINITION
Subsistence farming is an agricultural method where families grow food primarily to feed themselves, while commercial farming is a large-scale business activity focused on producing crops or animal products for profit in national or international markets.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify key differences between subsistence and commercial farming based on the purpose of production, the scale of land, and the destination of the harvest.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the distinction between subsistence and commercial farming systems. It excludes plantation farming, organic farming methods, and contract farming agreements.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever wondered why some farmers in your village have a small patch of land with many different vegetables, while others have huge fields filled with only one type of crop like cotton? To understand this, look at the farmer's "goal." If a family grows rice, wheat, and pulses just to make sure they have enough dinner for the whole year, they are doing subsistence farming. If they plant a massive field of sugarcane to sell to a factory for money, they are doing commercial farming. Start by asking: "Who is this food for—the table or the market?" Once you answer that, you can see how the farmer decides what to grow and how to manage their land.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the primary goal of a farmer practicing subsistence farming?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The primary goal is to produce enough food to meet the needs of the farmer's own family. A wrong answer suggests the student confuses "self-sufficiency" with "profit-making," revealing a failure to distinguish between survival and business goals.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why do farmers practicing subsistence farming usually grow a "mix" of crops rather than just one?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
They grow a variety of crops like rice, pulses, and vegetables to ensure they have a complete, balanced diet for their family throughout the year. A wrong answer shows the student hasn't grasped that subsistence is about household security, not just market efficiency.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If a farmer sells their small "surplus" of wheat at the local market, does this change their farming type to commercial? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
No, it remains subsistence farming because the main goal was still family consumption, and the sale of surplus is secondary. A wrong answer reveals the misconception that *any* sale of produce makes a farm "commercial."
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the land size usually associated with subsistence farming versus commercial farming.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Subsistence farming is typically done on small landholdings (often one or two acres), while commercial farming requires large tracts of land for high-volume production. A wrong answer indicates the student doesn't understand the relationship between land scale and the purpose of farming.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it possible for a commercial farmer to also consume some of the crops they produce? Explain.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, but the *primary* purpose of their farming activity is to generate profit, not family self-sufficiency. A wrong answer reveals a rigid, binary thinking that fails to account for the primary business motivation in commercial models.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple table or chart that would help a farmer decide whether to switch a small portion of their land to commercial crops. What factors should they include?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good answer would include columns for "Daily Food Needs" (subsistence) and "Market Demand/Profit" (commercial). A poor answer ignores the family's basic survival needs, indicating a failure to prioritize household security in the subsistence model.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that commercial farming only exists in Western countries or that it must involve modern machinery. They fail to realize that commercial farming is defined by the *intent* (selling for profit) and *scale* (large surplus), which is a common practice in many parts of India, such as sugarcane or tea farming.
## ON_FAILURE
First, ask the student to define "profit." Then, show them two photos: a small kitchen garden and a vast, single-crop field. Ask them to point to the one that would be "risky" if the market price dropped, and explain that commercial farming is tied to market prices, while subsistence farming is tied to family hunger.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A farmer named Ravi has 2 acres of land and grows rice, dal, and spinach. He keeps most of it for his house but sells 5 bags of rice at the local shop to buy seeds for next year. What type of farming is this?
STEPS:
- 1: Analyze the land size (small, 2 acres).
- 2: Identify the crop variety (diverse: rice, dal, spinach).
- 3: Determine the primary goal (family needs).
- 4: Evaluate the sale (small surplus for household supplies).
- 5: Conclude the type.
REASONING: The focus is clearly on the family's survival, and the sale is secondary (a surplus).
ANSWER: This is subsistence farming.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: An organization owns 500 acres of land and grows only high-quality cotton. They sell it to international garment companies to make clothes. What type of farming is this?
STEPS:
- 1: Analyze the land size (massive, 500 acres).
- 2: Identify the crop variety (single, specialized).
- 3: Determine the primary goal (profit).
- 4: Determine the destination (international market).
- 5: Conclude the type.
REASONING: The scale and the profit-driven goal define this as a business enterprise.
ANSWER: This is commercial farming.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A farmer shifts from growing 5 different crops (for food) to growing only 1 crop (for the market) but realizes the market price is unstable. Why is this a major change?
STEPS:
- 1: Identify the shift from subsistence to commercial.
- 2: Evaluate the risk of losing variety (loss of family food supply).
- 3: Evaluate the risk of market dependence (unstable income).
- 4: Consider the scale change (likely needed for the single crop).
- 5: Conclude the impact on the family's security.
REASONING: This shows the tradeoff between self-sufficiency and market-based profit.
ANSWER: It is a change from a secure, self-sufficient lifestyle to a high-risk, profit-dependent business model.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If a village has a drought, which farmer is likely to be more secure: the one practicing subsistence or the one practicing commercial? Why?
COMPETENCY: Critical Analysis
STEPS:
- 1: Analyze the resource dependency of both.
- 2: Note that subsistence farmers grow diverse crops, some of which might survive.
- 3: Note that commercial farmers depend on a single cash crop that needs specific conditions.
- 4: Compare stability.
ANSWER: The subsistence farmer is likely more secure because their diverse crops serve as an insurance policy, whereas the commercial farmer relies on one crop for their entire income, which could fail completely.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Why is "surplus" the key word in distinguishing between these two types of farming?
COMPETENCY: Conceptual Synthesis
STEPS:
- 1: Define what surplus means (what is left over).
- 2: Explain that in subsistence farming, surplus is an accident or extra.
- 3: Explain that in commercial farming, the "surplus" is the actual target.
- 4: Synthesize the relationship between purpose and the product.
ANSWER: Surplus is key because for a subsistence farmer, it represents "extra" food after the family is fed, whereas for a commercial farmer, the entire harvest is intended to be surplus to be sold for profit.
DIFFICULTY: hard
