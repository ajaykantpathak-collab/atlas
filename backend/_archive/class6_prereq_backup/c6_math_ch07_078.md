## DEFINITION
Symmetry exists when a shape can be divided into two identical halves that mirror each other perfectly along a specific line. A line of symmetry is the imaginary line where you could fold a shape so that both sides match exactly.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify lines of symmetry in basic geometric shapes and determine if a given shape has no symmetry, one line of symmetry, or multiple lines of symmetry through visual inspection or paper folding.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers line symmetry in 2D shapes (polygons and circles) and identifies shapes with no symmetry. It excludes point symmetry, rotational transformation geometry, and tessellation patterns.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever noticed how a butterfly's wings look like a perfect reflection of each other? To find symmetry in shapes, imagine you are holding a piece of paper cut into the shape you are studying. If you fold it along a straight line and both sides overlap perfectly, you have found a line of symmetry. Start by drawing a square and trying to fold it horizontally, vertically, and diagonally; you will find that it matches in all four ways. If you try this with an irregular shape, like a scalene triangle, you will see that no matter how you fold it, the sides never match, meaning it has no line symmetry.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is a line of symmetry in a 2D shape?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
It is an imaginary line that divides a shape into two identical mirror-image halves. A wrong answer like "any line drawn through a shape" shows the student doesn't understand that the two halves must match perfectly upon folding.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Does a circle have a limited number of lines of symmetry? Explain your reasoning.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
No, a circle has infinitely many lines of symmetry because any straight line passing through its center acts as a line of symmetry. Students who answer "one" or "zero" likely confuse a line of symmetry with a diagonal of a polygon.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If you draw a rectangle that is not a square, how many lines of symmetry can you find?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
A rectangle has exactly two lines of symmetry: one horizontal and one vertical. If a student answers "four," they are incorrectly assuming all four-sided shapes (quadrilaterals) have the same symmetry as a square.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Analyze the letter "A" and the letter "B." Which one has a line of symmetry? Where is it located?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The letter "A" has one vertical line of symmetry down its center, while the letter "B" has one horizontal line of symmetry through its middle. Students failing this often struggle to visualize the shape reflected across an axis versus just looking at the shape as a whole.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Critique this statement: "Every triangle has at least one line of symmetry." Is this true?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
This is false; a scalene triangle has no lines of symmetry because all three sides have different lengths, making it impossible to fold into identical halves. If a student says "true," they are over-generalizing from equilateral or isosceles triangles.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Draw a shape that has exactly three lines of symmetry. Label where these lines would be.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
An equilateral triangle is the correct shape. If a student draws a shape with two or four lines, they have failed to identify the unique geometric properties of the equilateral triangle.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think a shape only has symmetry if it is "cut in half" by a vertical line. They fail to recognize horizontal or diagonal lines of symmetry because they are fixed on a top-to-bottom perspective. For example, they might say a rectangle has only one line of symmetry instead of two.
## ON_FAILURE
Take a physical sheet of paper cut into the shape. Ask the student to fold it vertically; if it doesn't match, ask them to rotate the paper 90 degrees and try folding again. By physically manipulating the object, they learn that symmetry is a property of the shape's geometry, not the orientation of the viewer.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A rectangular piece of chart paper measures 10 cm by 6 cm. How many lines of symmetry does it have and where are they?
STEPS:
- step 1: Visualize the rectangle as a four-sided shape with two pairs of equal sides.
- step 2: Attempt a fold along the horizontal axis (bisecting the 6 cm sides).
- step 3: Attempt a fold along the vertical axis (bisecting the 10 cm sides).
- step 4: Attempt a diagonal fold. Observe that the corners do not touch.
- step 5: Conclude that there are 2 lines of symmetry.
REASONING: Symmetry requires perfect overlap. Since the length and width differ, diagonals create non-matching triangles, leaving only the two mid-line folds.
ANSWER: 2 lines of symmetry (one horizontal, one vertical).
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: An equilateral triangle has three sides of 5 cm each. How many lines of symmetry can be drawn?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify that an equilateral triangle has equal sides and equal angles.
- step 2: Draw a line from the top vertex to the midpoint of the opposite base.
- step 3: Observe that this folds the triangle into two identical right-angled triangles.
- step 4: Repeat this process for the other two vertices.
- step 5: Count the three distinct lines.
REASONING: In a regular polygon, lines of symmetry correspond to the number of vertices when the number of sides is odd.
ANSWER: 3 lines of symmetry.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A student has a square frame with side length 8 cm. They want to decorate it with lines of symmetry. How many can they draw?
STEPS:
- step 1: Recognize the square as a regular polygon with 4 equal sides and 90-degree corners.
- step 2: Draw a vertical line through the center (1 line).
- step 3: Draw a horizontal line through the center (1 line).
- step 4: Draw a diagonal line connecting one corner to the opposite corner (1 line).
- step 5: Draw the second diagonal connecting the other two corners (1 line).
- step 6: Total the lines (1+1+1+1 = 4).
REASONING: Because all sides and angles are equal, any line connecting opposite midpoints or opposite corners results in a perfect mirror reflection.
ANSWER: 4 lines of symmetry.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You are given a shape of a heart. If you draw a line down the center, the left side matches the right side perfectly. Is this a line of symmetry? Can you find any other lines of symmetry in a standard heart shape?
COMPETENCY: Identifying and justifying lines of symmetry.
STEPS:
- step 1: Apply the definition of symmetry (identical halves upon folding).
- step 2: Test the vertical line by "folding" the heart in the mind.
- step 3: Test other angles (horizontal or diagonal) to see if they create identical halves.
- step 4: Conclude that the heart only has one vertical line of symmetry.
ANSWER: Yes, the vertical line is a line of symmetry. A standard heart has no other lines of symmetry because folding it horizontally or diagonally results in unequal top and bottom portions.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Compare a square and a circle. Both have symmetry. Which one has more? Why?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying geometric properties.
STEPS:
- step 1: List the lines of symmetry for a square (4).
- step 2: Consider the definition of a circle (all points equidistant from a center).
- step 3: Test different lines through the center of the circle.
- step 4: Compare the count (4 vs infinite).
ANSWER: The circle has more lines of symmetry. A square is limited to 4 specific lines (midpoints and corners), but because every point on the edge of a circle is identical in relation to its center, any diameter acts as a line of symmetry, leading to an infinite number.
DIFFICULTY: hard
