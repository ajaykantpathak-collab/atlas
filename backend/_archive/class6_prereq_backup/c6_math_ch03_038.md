## DEFINITION
Problem solving with fractions is the application of fraction operations—like identifying parts of a whole, comparing sizes, or calculating quantities—to find solutions for real-world situations. It involves translating word problems into mathematical steps by determining which fraction represents the share or portion of the total quantity.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to translate a word problem into a correct mathematical expression involving fractions and compute the result accurately. They must also demonstrate understanding by explaining whether their final answer makes sense in the context of the problem.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers basic arithmetic problem solving (addition, subtraction, and multiplication by a whole number) involving fractions within the Class 6 curriculum. It excludes algebraic equations, complex multi-step word problems involving variables, and optimization problems.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you have a 12-litre bucket of water, and you use 2/3 of it to water your garden. How do you find out how much you used? First, identify the "whole" amount (12 litres) and the fraction you are working with (2/3). Think of the fraction as a set of instructions: "Divide the whole into 3 equal parts, then take 2 of those parts." By dividing 12 by 3, you find one part is 4 litres; multiplying that by 2 gives you 8 litres used. Finally, look at the question again—did it ask for the amount used or the amount left? If it asked for what is left, subtract your result from the total. The key insight is that fractions are just shares of a whole; always find the value of one "share" first.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
If you have a 20 kg bag of rice and you use 1/4 of it, what does the denominator '4' tell you about the rice?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The denominator '4' tells you that the 20 kg bag has been divided into 4 equal parts. A wrong answer like "it means you take 4 kg" shows the student is confusing the total number of parts with the value of one part.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you have 1/2 of a roti and your friend has 1/3 of a similar roti, who has a larger piece and why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Your friend has a smaller piece because 1/3 is less than 1/2. A wrong answer (saying 1/3 is bigger because 3 > 2) reveals the student ignores that the denominator represents the number of people sharing the whole; more people sharing means each person gets less.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A rope is 15 metres long. If you cut off 1/3 of it, how many metres did you cut off?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
You cut off 5 metres. Steps: (1) Total = 15m, (2) Fraction = 1/3, (3) 15 ÷ 3 = 5, (4) 5 × 1 = 5. A wrong answer (like 1/3 m) shows the student thinks the fraction is a length rather than a relative share.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
If a tank holds 20 litres and is 3/4 full, how much water is in the tank? Compare this to a tank of 20 litres that is 1/4 full.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The first tank has 15 litres, and the second has 5 litres. A student who struggles here may not realize that the numerator (3 vs 1) indicates how many of those equal 'shares' are present, not just the size of the share.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Your teacher says 2/5 of the class are girls. If there are 30 students, is it possible to have 20 girls? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, it is not possible, because 1/5 of 30 is 6, so 2/5 is 12 girls. A wrong answer suggests the student is guessing or not performing the division/multiplication correctly to find the value of the fractional unit.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a word problem that requires you to find 3/4 of 16.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good answer would be: "I have 16 sweets and I give 3/4 of them to my friends. How many sweets did I give away?" A poor answer (e.g., "I have 16 sweets, I add 3/4") shows the student does not understand that "of" in fractions indicates multiplication/scaling.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often confuse the "share" with the "whole." For example, if asked to find 2/3 of 12, they might answer 2 or 3 because they see those numbers in the fraction, rather than understanding that 12 must be partitioned into 3 parts. This happens because they try to perform operations on the numbers they see without visualizing the "part of a whole" concept.
## ON_FAILURE
If a student fails, have them draw a rectangle and divide it into the number of parts shown by the denominator. Ask them to shade the number of parts shown by the numerator. Then, ask them to write the total number (e.g., 12) inside the rectangle and ask, "If the whole rectangle is 12, what number belongs in just one of these shaded boxes?" This visual "partitioning" helps them see the denominator as a divisor.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A shopkeeper has 40 kg of sugar. He sells 3/5 of it in the morning. How many kilograms of sugar are left for the afternoon?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify total (40 kg) and fraction sold (3/5).
- step 2: Find the value of one part by dividing 40 by the denominator 5 (40 ÷ 5 = 8 kg).
- step 3: Find the amount sold by multiplying the value of one part by the numerator 3 (8 × 3 = 24 kg).
- step 4: Subtract the amount sold from the total to find the amount left (40 - 24 = 16 kg).
- step 5: Verify: 2/5 of 40 is 16. Since 3/5 were sold, 2/5 remain.
REASONING: By finding the value of one "share" (1/5) first, we can easily calculate any number of shares (3/5 or 2/5). This method prevents confusion between the total and the portion sold.
ANSWER: 16 kg
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A school bus route is 20 kilometres long. If the bus has completed 4/5 of its route, how many kilometres has it travelled?
STEPS:
- step 1: Total distance = 20 km; portion travelled = 4/5.
- step 2: Divide the total by the denominator to find the length of one fifth (20 ÷ 5 = 4 km).
- step 3: Multiply by the numerator to find the total distance for four fifths (4 × 4 = 16 km).
- step 4: State the answer clearly with the correct unit.
- step 5: Interpret: 16 km is 4/5 of 20, which is correct.
REASONING: The denominator tells us the route is split into 5 equal segments; each segment is 4 km. Travelling 4 of these segments equals 16 km.
ANSWER: 16 km
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Rani has 60 rupees. She spends 1/3 on a pen and 1/4 of the remaining money on a notebook. How much money does she have left now?
STEPS:
- step 1: Initial total = 60 rupees.
- step 2: Calculate pen cost: 1/3 of 60 = 20 rupees.
- step 3: Calculate remaining money after pen: 60 - 20 = 40 rupees.
- step 4: Calculate notebook cost: 1/4 of 40 = 10 rupees.
- step 5: Subtract notebook cost from current balance: 40 - 10 = 30 rupees.
REASONING: This requires two stages because the second fraction (1/4) applies to the *remaining* amount, not the *initial* total. Sequential subtraction ensures each step is based on the updated balance.
ANSWER: 30 rupees
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You have a pizza cut into 8 equal slices. If you eat 3/8 and your friend eats 2/8, what fraction of the pizza is left? Explain your reasoning.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify total parts (8/8).
- step 2: Add portions consumed (3/8 + 2/8 = 5/8).
- step 3: Subtract consumed portion from total (8/8 - 5/8).
- step 4: Result is 3/8.
ANSWER: 3/8 of the pizza is left. My reasoning is that the whole pizza is 8/8, and together we ate 5/8, leaving 3 parts out of the original 8.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Two friends, A and B, both have a 100-page book. A reads 3/4 of their book, and B reads 4/5 of their book. Who read more pages and how many more?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying.
STEPS:
- step 1: Calculate pages for A: (100 ÷ 4) × 3 = 75 pages.
- step 2: Calculate pages for B: (100 ÷ 5) × 4 = 80 pages.
- step 3: Compare results (80 > 75).
- step 4: Find difference (80 - 75 = 5).
ANSWER: B read more pages. B read 80 pages and A read 75 pages, so B read 5 pages more than A.
DIFFICULTY: hard
