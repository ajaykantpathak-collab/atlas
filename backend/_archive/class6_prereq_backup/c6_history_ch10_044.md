## DEFINITION
The British Raj refers to the period of direct rule by the British Crown over the Indian subcontinent, which fundamentally changed India’s economy by prioritizing British industrial needs over local production. It transformed India into a source of raw materials and a market for British factory-made goods.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to describe how British policies turned India from a manufacturer into a supplier of raw materials and explain one negative impact on Indian farmers or weavers.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the economic transition and hardships (textiles, cash crops, land revenue) under British rule. It excludes social reform movements, specific economic statistics (like GDP figures), and nationalist historiography.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine your family makes beautiful handmade sweaters that are famous in the whole city. Suddenly, a big factory opens nearby and sells cheap, machine-made sweaters, forcing your family to stop weaving and instead sell the wool to the factory at a low price. This is exactly what happened to India's textile industry when British cloth flooded the markets. We start by looking at how local weavers lost their jobs, then we look at how farmers were forced to stop growing food to grow cash crops like indigo for the British. By connecting this to the idea of losing control over one’s own work, students realize that "impact" in history often means a major change in daily life.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What happened to the Indian textile industry when cheap British cloth arrived?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The Indian textile industry, once one of the most advanced, was destroyed. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks it grew or stayed the same, indicating they don't understand the concept of "competition" between handmade and factory goods.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why did British policies force farmers to grow crops like indigo instead of food crops?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
They were forced to grow "cash crops" to supply raw materials for British industries. If a student says "to feed more people," they are missing the point that the British prioritized their own industrial profit over the food security of the local population.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If a farmer is forced to grow indigo instead of rice, what happens to their local village?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
1. Identify the shift: Food crop (rice) to cash crop (indigo). 2. Identify the consequence: Less food available. 3. Conclude: Famines or food shortages occur because the village can no longer feed itself. A wrong answer shows a failure to link agricultural choices to food security.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the position of an Indian weaver before and after the influx of British machine-made cloth.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Before, they were skilled independent producers; after, they lost their livelihood because they could not compete with the low cost of mass-produced British cloth. If the student focuses only on "cheaper clothes," they fail to analyze the impact on the artisan's life.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Was the British economic policy beneficial to the Indian common person? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, because it prioritized British industrial wealth at the expense of local manufacturing and food security. A wrong answer suggests the student is being influenced by myths of "progress" without looking at the cost to the actual population.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a scenario showing how a local market would look before and after the arrival of British goods.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good answer describes a vibrant bazaar of local crafts before, and a market dominated by uniform British imports after. Poor answers will just list random items without showing the change in the source or the economic shift.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often assume that "cheap cloth" was a "good deal" for Indians without realizing that the destruction of the local textile industry caused massive unemployment. They focus on the price of the product rather than the loss of the worker's income and skill.
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student: "If you buy a cheap shirt but lose your job because the factory that made it put your workshop out of business, are you richer or poorer?" Guide them to see that economic impact is about the whole cycle of production, not just the store price.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A village used to grow wheat to feed everyone, but the British demanded they grow cotton for export. Trace the steps of how this changes the village.
STEPS:
- 1. Identify the original state: Wheat (food) production.
- 2. Identify the intervention: Forced shift to Cotton (cash crop).
- 3. Identify the economic shift: From local consumption to external export.
- 4. Analyze the result: Less wheat means potential hunger.
- 5. Conclude: The village loses its independence to feed itself.
REASONING: This shows that economic policies directly dictate the survival and daily habits of rural communities.
ANSWER: The village is now dependent on external trade for food, making it vulnerable to famine and poverty.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Why did the Indian textile industry collapse?
STEPS:
- 1. Identify the initial state: High-quality handmade textiles.
- 2. Identify the external factor: Massive supply of cheap British factory cloth.
- 3. Compare: Handmade is slower and costlier than factory-made.
- 4. Analyze the outcome: People bought cheaper cloth, local weavers lost their work.
- 5. Verify: The industry was systematically dismantled by this price competition.
REASONING: Economic survival depends on the ability to compete; when a policy favors foreign factories, local artisans suffer.
ANSWER: The industry collapsed because local artisans could not compete with the low prices of mass-produced British goods.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: How did land revenue demands (taxes) impact farmers already struggling with cash crops?
STEPS:
- 1. Identify the pressure: High tax (revenue) must be paid in money.
- 2. Identify the limitation: Farmers only have crops, not cash.
- 3. Analyze the constraint: To get cash, they MUST grow cash crops (like indigo).
- 4. Analyze the risk: If the crop fails, they have no food AND no money for taxes.
- 5. Synthesize: The combination of high taxes and cash crop requirements creates a cycle of debt.
REASONING: This combines the impact of taxation with agricultural policy to show the "squeeze" on farmers.
ANSWER: Farmers were pushed into deep debt because high taxes forced them to grow cash crops, which, if unsuccessful, left them with neither food nor money.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If you were a weaver in 1850, how would the arrival of British cloth change your daily routine?
COMPETENCY: Empathy and Historical Analysis
STEPS:
- 1. Describe the normal day (weaving).
- 2. Identify the change (no one buying your cloth).
- 3. Describe the result (need to find other work or starve).
- 4. Connect to the policy (British imports).
ANSWER: My day would go from being a respected artisan to struggling to find labor work, because my handmade cloth is now too expensive compared to the new factory imports.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Why might a famine occur even if a region is growing plenty of plants?
COMPETENCY: Critical Thinking
STEPS:
- 1. Identify the shift: Growing cash crops (indigo/cotton) instead of food.
- 2. Identify the purpose: These are for profit/export, not eating.
- 3. Analyze the consequence: The local food supply drops.
- 4. Verify: High taxes force farmers to sell all their harvest.
- 5. Conclude: The land produces wealth, but not food for the people living there.
ANSWER: A famine occurs because the land is used to grow cash crops for profit rather than food for the local population, and high taxes force farmers to sell whatever they have, leaving nothing to eat.
DIFFICULTY: hard
