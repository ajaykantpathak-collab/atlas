## DEFINITION
Poetic devices are specific tools—like imagery, contrast, and sound words—that writers use to make their poems more vivid, emotional, and memorable for the reader. They turn simple descriptions into sensory experiences.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify an example of imagery, contrast, or sound words in a short poem and explain how that specific device changes their experience of the text.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers identifying and explaining imagery, contrast, and onomatopoeia (sound words). It excludes meter, rhyme schemes, and free verse structure.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever closed your eyes and felt like you could "hear" the rain or "see" the dark shadows in a story? That happens because the writer used poetic devices. Start by picking one line from a poem and asking: "What does this make me see, hear, or feel?" If you find words like 'crash' or 'hiss', you've found sound words; if you find two opposite ideas (like hot sun and cold night) side-by-side, you've found contrast; if it paints a picture in your mind, it's imagery. By connecting these devices to your own five senses, you can unlock the "hidden" meaning in any poem.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What do we call words that imitate the natural sound of an object or action, like 'rumble' or 'hiss'?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
These are called sound words or onomatopoeia. A wrong answer here suggests the student is confusing these with literal descriptions or adjectives, which do not imitate sounds.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If a poem mentions a "bright, sunny morning" followed immediately by a "dark, lonely night," what poetic device is the author using?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The author is using contrast. A student answering "imagery" may be focusing on the visual aspect, but missing the deliberate placement of opposites to create impact.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Find a line in your mind that describes a hot cup of tea. How would you rewrite it using imagery to make the reader "feel" the warmth and "smell" the tea?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Example: "The steaming cup radiated heat, carrying the earthy scent of fresh leaves to my nose." A wrong answer shows a lack of sensory detail, often relying on simple labels like "the tea was hot."
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Analyze this: "The train roared through the silent jungle." Why does the poet use the word 'roared' and the word 'silent' in the same sentence?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
'Roared' is a sound word emphasizing the train's power, while 'silent' provides contrast to highlight how loud the train actually is. A wrong answer indicates the student is not seeing the relationship between the two distinct devices.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
"The sun was a golden coin in the sky." Is this imagery? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, it is imagery because it creates a clear visual picture for the reader. A wrong answer suggests the student only thinks imagery is about "sound" or "touch," forgetting the importance of sight.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Write one sentence about a busy school playground using a sound word.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Acceptable: "The playground buzzed with laughter and shouting." A poor answer lacks the sound-word element (e.g., "The playground was very noisy"), which fails to use the specific tool requested.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think "imagery" must include all five senses at once. They struggle to realize that even one strong visual description is a form of imagery. For example, if a poem says "the blue sky," they might say "that's not imagery because I can't smell or hear it," failing to recognize sight as a sensory experience.
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to close their eyes and describe a single object (like a mango) without naming it. Focus on what it looks like (color, shape), then what it feels like (texture). Explain that imagery is simply "word-painting"—if you can visualize it, it's imagery.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Analyze the line: "The icy wind whistled through the gaps."
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify sensory words (icy, whistled).
- Step 2: Categorize 'icy' as a touch-based imagery word.
- Step 3: Categorize 'whistled' as a sound word (onomatopoeia).
- Step 4: Combine the findings to explain the effect (the cold is felt, and the sound is heard).
REASONING: Breaking the sentence into sensory parts helps identify multiple devices at once.
ANSWER: This line uses touch-based imagery ('icy') and onomatopoeia ('whistled') to create a cold, haunting atmosphere.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Identify contrast in: "The city never sleeps; the forest knows no time."
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the two locations (city vs. forest).
- Step 2: Identify the actions linked to each (never sleeps vs. knows no time).
- Step 3: Compare the speed/intent of these actions.
- Step 4: Conclude that the contrast highlights the difference between human haste and natural stillness.
REASONING: Comparing the qualities of two subjects reveals the writer's intent.
ANSWER: The contrast is between the busy, endless activity of the city and the timeless, quiet nature of the forest.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Write a short paragraph about a storm using imagery, contrast, and sound words.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Use a sound word for the thunder (e.g., 'crackle').
- Step 2: Use visual imagery for the lightning (e.g., 'jagged white veins').
- Step 3: Use contrast between the loud thunder and the quiet fear of the speaker.
- Step 4: Combine into a coherent narrative.
REASONING: Integrating multiple devices creates a richer descriptive scene.
ANSWER: "The sky cracked with a sudden boom, the lightning painting jagged white veins across the dark. Inside, I held my breath, the silence of my room contrasting sharply with the roar of the storm outside."
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: In a poem about a race, the author writes: "The crowd cheered; the runner was silent." What devices are used here?
COMPETENCY: Identification and analysis of contrast.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Note the two groups (crowd and runner).
- Step 2: Note the opposing actions (cheered vs. silent).
- Step 3: Identify the relationship as contrast.
- Step 4: Explain that this highlights the runner's intense focus.
ANSWER: This uses contrast to show the difference between the loud external support and the quiet internal focus of the athlete.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Why might a poet choose to use the word 'chug' instead of 'moved' when describing a train?
COMPETENCY: Evaluation of poetic choices.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Define 'chug' as a sound word.
- Step 2: Define 'moved' as a neutral verb.
- Step 3: Compare the sensory effect of both words.
- Step 4: Conclude that 'chug' provides a rhythmic, auditory experience.
- Step 5: Explain that this helps the reader "hear" the train's engine.
ANSWER: A poet chooses 'chug' to give the reader an auditory experience, making the poem feel alive compared to the flat description of 'moved.'
DIFFICULTY: hard
