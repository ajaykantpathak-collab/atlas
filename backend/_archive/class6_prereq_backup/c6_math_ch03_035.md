## DEFINITION
Multiplying fractions is the process of finding a part of a part; it involves multiplying the numerators to form the new numerator and multiplying the denominators to form the new denominator.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to calculate the product of two fractions correctly and explain why the result is typically smaller than the original fractions when using proper fractions.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the multiplication of two proper or improper fractions. It excludes algebraic fractions, complex fractions, and properties proofs.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you have half a bar of chikki and you want to give one-third of that half to your friend. How much of the whole chikki does your friend get? To solve this, you don't need a common denominator; you simply multiply the numerators together and the denominators together. Think of it as "of" — one-third "of" one-half. By multiplying 1/3 by 1/2, you get 1/6, which shows how the piece becomes smaller as you take a fraction of a fraction.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the rule for multiplying two fractions?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
To multiply two fractions, you multiply the numerators together to get the new numerator and the denominators together to get the new denominator. A wrong answer like "add the denominators" reveals a confusion between the rules for addition/subtraction and multiplication.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you multiply 1/2 by 1/4, will the result be larger or smaller than 1/2? Why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The result will be smaller (1/8) because you are taking a fraction of a fraction. Answering "larger" shows the student is incorrectly applying whole-number multiplication logic where products are always larger than factors.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Calculate the product: 2/5 × 3/4.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
(2 × 3) / (5 × 4) = 6/20. Simplified, this is 3/10. Answering 5/9 or 6/9 shows the student is attempting to cross-multiply or add instead of multiplying directly across.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the calculation 1/3 × 1/5 with the addition 1/3 + 1/5. Why are the results different?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The product is 1/15, while the sum is 8/15. The results differ because multiplication finds a portion of a portion, while addition combines two distinct quantities, requiring different operational rules. Failing to see this distinction suggests the student views "fractions" as just numbers and not as parts of a whole.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
If a recipe requires 2/3 of a cup of milk, and you decide to make only 1/2 of the recipe, how much milk do you need? Justify your calculation.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
You need 1/2 × 2/3 = 2/6 = 1/3 cup of milk. The student must justify that taking half of two-thirds means multiplying them. Answering 2/3 shows a failure to recognize the need for multiplication in a "part of a part" scenario.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a real-life word problem where you would need to multiply 3/4 by 1/2.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good answer would involve a physical quantity: "If you have 3/4 of a litre of juice and drink half of it, how much juice did you drink?" A poor answer would just be a string of numbers or an addition-based scenario, showing they haven't grasped that multiplication implies taking a fraction "of" a quantity.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often try to find a common denominator before multiplying, just as they do with addition and subtraction. For example, in 1/2 × 1/3, they might change it to 3/6 × 2/6 and then mistakenly multiply the numerators to get 6/6 (or 1), ignoring that the denominator should also be multiplied.
## ON_FAILURE
Explain that multiplication is not about combining shares (which is addition), but about scaling one share by another. Use a paper strip: show 1/2 of the strip, then fold that piece into 3 parts and shade one of them. Ask them to count how many of those small pieces would fit in the original whole (6), leading them to see the denominator becomes 6, not 2 or 3.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A container holds 4/5 of a litre of milk. If you use 1/3 of the milk for a recipe, how many litres of milk did you use?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the given amount (4/5 litre) and the portion being used (1/3).
- step 2: Recognize that "1/3 of 4/5" requires multiplication: 1/3 × 4/5.
- step 3: Multiply the numerators: 1 × 4 = 4.
- step 4: Multiply the denominators: 3 × 5 = 15.
- step 5: Result is 4/15 litre.
REASONING: Multiplying the fractions gives us the specific portion of the whole litre that was used.
ANSWER: 4/15 litre
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A garden bed is 5/6 of a metre long. If a path covers 2/3 of this length, how long is the path?
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the total length (5/6 m) and the portion covered (2/3).
- step 2: Set up the multiplication: 2/3 × 5/6.
- step 3: Multiply numerators: 2 × 5 = 10.
- step 4: Multiply denominators: 3 × 6 = 18.
- step 5: Simplify 10/18 by dividing both by 2 to get 5/9 m.
REASONING: We scale the length of the garden bed by the fraction covered by the path.
ANSWER: 5/9 metre
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A ribbon is 3/4 of a metre long. A student cuts off 2/5 of the ribbon. How much ribbon is left?
STEPS:
- step 1: Calculate the piece cut off: 2/5 × 3/4 = 6/20 = 3/10 m.
- step 2: Understand the remaining ribbon is (Total length) - (Piece cut off).
- step 3: Subtract: 3/4 - 3/10.
- step 4: Find common denominator (20): 15/20 - 6/20 = 9/20.
- step 5: The remaining ribbon is 9/20 metre.
REASONING: This combines multiplication (to find the part removed) with subtraction (to find what is left).
ANSWER: 9/20 metre
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Is 1/2 × 1/4 larger or smaller than 1/2 × 1/8? Explain your reasoning without just calculating the final answers.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- step 1: Analyze the meaning of the fractions.
- step 2: Recognize that 1/4 is a larger portion than 1/8.
- step 3: Understand that multiplying 1/2 by a larger number results in a larger product.
- step 4: Conclude 1/2 × 1/4 > 1/2 × 1/8.
ANSWER: It is larger because 1/4 is a larger fractional unit than 1/8. When you take a larger portion of the same starting amount (1/2), the result must be greater.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A bag contains 2 kg of sugar. If you take 3/4 of the bag, and then from that portion, you use 1/5, what fraction of the original 2 kg did you use?
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the two stages: 3/4 of the bag, then 1/5 of that part.
- step 2: Multiply the fractions: 1/5 × 3/4.
- step 3: Calculate the product: (1 × 3) / (5 × 4) = 3/20.
- step 4: Verify the question asks for the fraction of the original 2 kg.
ANSWER: You used 3/20 of the original bag. Even though the bag is 2 kg, the question asks for the fraction of the "original amount," which is 3/20 of the whole.
DIFFICULTY: hard
