## DEFINITION
Ashoka's edicts are formal, public messages carved onto stone rocks and pillars by Emperor Ashoka to communicate his policies, his regret over war, and his commitment to the moral principles of *dhamma* (righteous living) to the citizens of his vast empire.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify that the edicts served as a public communication tool and explain at least two reasons why Ashoka chose to carve them on stone rather than writing on paper or palm leaves.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the purpose and nature of Ashoka's edicts and the content of his messages. It excludes linguistic decipherment, detailed grammar, or comparison with inscriptions from other civilizations.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are the leader of a massive school and want to make sure every single student follows the same rules, but you don't have WhatsApp or notice boards. How would you tell everyone your rules? Ashoka faced this problem: he ruled a huge area with many languages and needed his message to be permanent and visible to everyone. He chose to carve his thoughts on huge rocks and tall pillars, which were like the "billboards" of the ancient world. Because these rocks don't rot like paper, his messages survived for over 2,000 years. The key insight is that the edicts were not just history records, but active tools used by a king to shape how people lived together.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What materials did Ashoka use to create his edicts, and why were these choices important?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
He used rock surfaces and sandstone pillars. These materials were chosen because they are durable and permanent, ensuring his messages would last for generations. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks these were books or scrolls, missing the point of public visibility and longevity.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why did Ashoka use different languages like Prakrit, Greek, and Aramaic for his inscriptions?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
He used multiple languages so that people living in different parts of his diverse empire could actually read and understand his messages in their own local language. A wrong answer suggests students think it was just to show off his power, failing to realize the practical purpose of effective communication.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If Ashoka wanted to reach villagers who could not travel to the capital, how did his choice of placing edicts on rocks help him?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Steps: 1. Identify the location (public spaces/routes). 2. Recognize the durability. 3. Connect to the reader. 4. Conclusion: By placing them in public view, he brought his laws to the people's doorstep. A wrong answer shows the student assumes messages were only for royal officials.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
How does the existence of the Kalinga War in the edicts change our view of Ashoka as a king?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
It shows he was a leader who could change his mind and reflect on his mistakes, moving from a conqueror to a teacher of peace. A wrong answer treats the edicts as just "laws," missing the personal transformation revealed by the text.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it more accurate to call the edicts "advertisements" or "monuments"? Why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
They are more like "public notices" or "social advertisements." They weren't built just to look pretty (like a monument), but to actively persuade people to adopt *dhamma*. If a student says "monument," they are ignoring the *content* and *purpose* of the writing.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
If you were a ruler today, what one "moral rule" would you carve on a pillar in your city square to make it a better place?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good answer provides a rule related to community well-being (e.g., "Respect all living beings") and explains *why* it is important for the public to see it daily. A poor answer is trivial (e.g., "Eat ice cream") or lacks a reason, showing a failure to understand the social goal of Ashoka's *dhamma*.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think Ashoka carved these edicts only to brag about his victories. In reality, the edicts focus heavily on his regret over the Kalinga War and his desire for peace. Students confuse the edicts with a "trophy room" of a king, failing to understand they were a revolutionary way to promote social ethics.
## ON_FAILURE
If a student thinks the edicts are just bragging, ask them: "If you win a game but then feel bad about how you played, would you write that on a wall for everyone to see?" Guide them to see that by admitting to the Kalinga War's destruction, Ashoka was doing the opposite of bragging—he was showing humility and a desire for moral change.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A traveler in 250 BCE sees an inscription on a pillar in a language they don't recognize. Why might this happen?
STEPS:
- 1: Identify the region where the pillar is located.
- 2: Recall that Ashoka used different languages (Prakrit, Greek, Aramaic) based on the region.
- 3: Determine the traveler's origin relative to that region.
- 4: Conclude that the king chose the language of the local majority, not a universal one.
REASONING: The edicts were practical tools, not meant to be universal in a single language, but localized for the residents of that specific area.
ANSWER: The traveler is likely from a different linguistic region of the empire, where a different local language was used for the edicts.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Why did Ashoka stop using soldiers to enforce his will and start using edicts to guide his people?
STEPS:
- 1: Recognize the shift from war (Kalinga) to peace (Dhamma).
- 2: Analyze the limitation of physical force versus moral influence.
- 3: Contrast "commanding" with "persuading."
- 4: Conclude that moral education (dhamma) creates long-term stability.
REASONING: Force changes behavior temporarily, but teaching values through public edicts aims to change how people think and treat others.
ANSWER: Ashoka realized that physical force, like that used in the Kalinga War, caused only suffering; he shifted to edicts to promote *dhamma* because he wanted to lead through moral influence rather than fear.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: How do we know the extent of the Mauryan Empire's influence by looking at where the edicts are found?
STEPS:
- 1: Locate the edicts on a map of the Indian subcontinent.
- 2: Observe the geographical spread (from the northwest to the south).
- 3: Correlate the placement of stone markers with government control.
- 4: Infer that the king only placed expensive stone edicts where his authority was recognized.
- 5: Verify the spread covers diverse terrains.
REASONING: The placement of permanent structures shows the physical limits of the king's reach and his desire to stamp his policies in those specific territories.
ANSWER: The wide dispersal of these edicts across mountains and plains acts as a map of the Mauryan Empire, showing where Ashoka's administrative influence and laws were in effect.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If you were an archaeologist finding a new inscription, what detail would prove it is likely from Ashoka's time?
COMPETENCY: Evidence-based reasoning.
STEPS:
- 1: List features of Ashoka's edicts (stone, specific languages, message of dhamma).
- 2: Compare with typical royal decrees of the time.
- 3: Select the most unique marker (the tone of moral guidance/dhamma).
ANSWER: The focus on *dhamma* and personal reform, rather than just lists of taxes or military victories, is the strongest marker of an Ashokan edict.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Why might a king choose to carve edicts in a public place rather than keeping them inside his palace?
COMPETENCY: Analyzing historical intent.
STEPS:
- 1: Analyze the purpose of a palace (private) vs. a public square (shared).
- 2: Determine who the target audience is (citizens vs. royal family).
- 3: Connect the location to the goal of "dhamma."
- 4: Formulate the reasoning based on accessibility.
- 5: Conclude why public space is essential for social change.
ANSWER: A king keeps palace records for his own administration, but he places edicts in public squares to ensure they act as a "public conscience," forcing every passerby to engage with his moral message.
DIFFICULTY: hard
