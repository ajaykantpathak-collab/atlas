## DEFINITION
Rusting is a chemical change where iron reacts with oxygen and moisture from the air to form a new substance called iron oxide (rust). This process permanently changes the original properties of the iron into a brittle, reddish-brown, non-magnetic material.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify rusting as a chemical change by contrasting the physical properties of iron (grey, hard, magnetic) with those of rust (reddish-brown, brittle, non-magnetic) and listing the two necessary conditions (oxygen and water).
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the identification of rusting as a chemical change and the basic environmental conditions required. It does not cover the electrochemical mechanism, chemical equations (stoichiometry), or industrial corrosion prevention methods.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever noticed how the iron gate in your garden or a bicycle left in the rain turns a reddish-brown color over time? To understand why this happens, think of the iron "breathing" in oxygen and "drinking" water from the humid air. Unlike a piece of paper being torn, which is still paper, iron undergoes a transformation where it becomes an entirely new substance called rust. You begin by observing that the new substance (rust) cannot be turned back into the original iron, which is the hallmark of a chemical change. The key insight is that the properties—color, hardness, and magnetic behavior—have been permanently altered by a reaction with the environment.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What are the two specific substances present in the environment that must react with iron to cause rusting?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The two substances are oxygen (from air) and water (from moisture). A wrong answer suggests the student is guessing common environmental factors (like dust or sunlight) rather than identifying the specific chemical reactants needed for oxidation.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why is the formation of rust considered a "chemical change" rather than a "physical change"?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
It is a chemical change because a new substance (iron oxide) is formed with different properties, and the process cannot be reversed by simple means. A wrong answer suggests the student believes the change is merely in shape or appearance, missing the fact that the material itself has changed composition.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If you have a block of pure iron and it turns into rust, what happens to its ability to stick to a magnet?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The block will lose its ability to stick to a magnet because rust is non-magnetic. A wrong answer indicates the student has not grasped that the physical properties of the material (magnetism) change when the chemical structure changes.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the physical appearance of iron and rust.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Iron is grey, hard, and strong, whereas rust is reddish-brown, brittle, and weak. A wrong answer reveals confusion between the original metal and the product of the reaction.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
If an iron rod is kept in a vacuum (an area with no air or moisture), will it rust? Explain why.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, it will not rust because the necessary reactants (oxygen and water) are absent. This tests the student's ability to apply the conditions of the chemical change to a hypothetical scenario.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design an experiment to prove that moisture is necessary for iron to rust.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Place one iron nail in a dry container and one in a container with water/moisture, then observe which one changes over a week. A poor answer fails to include a "control" (the dry nail) to compare against, showing a lack of scientific methodology.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that rusting is just "dirt" or "stains" that can be wiped off, or that it is a physical change like melting ice. This happens because they confuse the surface layer of rust with an external coating rather than recognizing it as a transformed portion of the metal itself.
## ON_FAILURE
First, show the student a magnet attracting a clean iron nail but failing to attract a heavily rusted one. Second, ask them to scrape the rust off—point out that the metal beneath is thinner, proving the iron was consumed to make the rust. Explain that because the iron disappeared to become something else, it is a permanent chemical change.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A student leaves two 100g iron bars outside. Bar A is coated with oil (protecting it from air/water), and Bar B is left bare in the rain. Describe the outcome after a month.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify that Bar A is protected by an oil layer preventing contact with oxygen and moisture.
- Step 2: Identify that Bar B is exposed to both oxygen and water.
- Step 3: Apply the rule that iron + oxygen + water = rust.
- Step 4: Conclude that Bar B will have converted some of its iron mass into reddish-brown, brittle iron oxide.
- Step 5: Compare: Bar A remains strong and magnetic; Bar B is weakened and loses magnetic properties.
REASONING: This illustrates that without the chemical reactants, the change cannot occur, highlighting the necessity of oxygen and water.
ANSWER: Bar A remains unchanged; Bar B turns into rust (iron oxide).
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: An iron key is dropped into a cup of water and left for a week. What physical changes will occur to the key?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the reactants: Iron (key), Oxygen (dissolved in water/air), and Water.
- Step 2: Note the conditions: The key is fully exposed to moisture and oxygen.
- Step 3: Predict the change: The surface of the iron will undergo a chemical reaction.
- Step 4: Describe the visual result: A reddish-brown coating will appear.
- Step 5: Describe the physical change: The key will become weaker and less magnetic.
REASONING: Exposure to water provides the moisture needed, and air exposure provides the oxygen, fulfilling the requirements for rusting.
ANSWER: The key will develop a reddish-brown, brittle coating of iron oxide.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A mechanic finds a magnetic screwdriver and a rusted iron bolt. He cannot get the bolt to stick to the screwdriver. Why?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the properties of the screwdriver (magnetic).
- Step 2: Identify the state of the bolt (rusted).
- Step 3: Recall that rust is a different substance from iron.
- Step 4: Apply the property rule: Rust is non-magnetic.
- Step 5: Determine the interaction: The magnetic force cannot attract the non-magnetic rust layer.
- Step 6: Conclusion: The chemical change has destroyed the magnetic property required for the bolt to be picked up.
REASONING: This demonstrates how chemical changes alter the fundamental properties (magnetism) of materials.
ANSWER: The bolt does not stick because the iron has turned into non-magnetic iron oxide.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You are given three samples: a grey iron nail, a piece of wood, and a reddish-brown object that was once an iron nail. Which one is a result of a chemical change and why?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Analyze the origin of the three samples.
- Step 2: Identify the "reddish-brown" object as the one that underwent a change.
- Step 3: Contrast its current properties (brittle, non-magnetic) with the original iron nail (hard, magnetic).
- Step 4: Justify that the change is chemical because the material's identity changed.
ANSWER: The reddish-brown object is the result of a chemical change because it is no longer the original grey iron. It has transformed into iron oxide, a new substance with different properties.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: If you have a toy made of iron, suggest two ways to prevent it from rusting based on the conditions required for rusting.
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction / Application.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Recall the two requirements for rusting: Oxygen and Water.
- Step 2: Devise a method to block oxygen (e.g., paint, oil).
- Step 3: Devise a method to keep it away from water (e.g., store in a dry place).
- Step 4: Explain why these prevent the chemical reaction.
ANSWER: To prevent rusting, one could paint the toy to block oxygen from touching the iron, or keep the toy in a completely dry, moisture-free container to ensure no water is available for the reaction.
DIFFICULTY: hard
