## DEFINITION
A composite shape is a complex figure formed by joining two or more basic shapes (like rectangles, squares, or triangles) together. The perimeter is the total length of the outer boundary, while the area is the sum of the areas of the individual basic shapes that make up the whole.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to correctly decompose a complex polygon into simpler rectangles or triangles and perform the arithmetic to calculate the total area and perimeter. Mastery is shown by correctly identifying missing side lengths and summing the individual areas without overlap.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the calculation of perimeter and area for composite shapes made by joining rectangles and triangles. It excludes 3D shapes, integration, and complex curved boundaries.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are building a custom garden fence that looks like an 'L' shape. You have the total length of the long sides, but how do you find the length of the shorter "cut-in" sides or the total space inside? To begin, trace your finger along the outside edge of the shape to visualize the perimeter; count only the segments that face outward. Next, use a ruler or imaginary line to "slice" the L-shape into two separate, simple rectangles. Calculate the area of each smaller piece individually using the formula (length × breadth) and add them together to find the total area. The key insight is that even the most complicated shape is just a collection of simple shapes waiting to be broken apart.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the difference between the perimeter and the area of a composite shape?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Perimeter is the total length of the outer boundary, while area is the total space enclosed inside the shape. A wrong answer here suggests the student confuses "distance around" with "space inside," which is a foundational error in spatial reasoning.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you split an L-shaped figure into two rectangles, does the sum of their perimeters equal the perimeter of the L-shape? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
No, it does not, because the inner sides where the shapes are joined are no longer part of the outer boundary. A wrong answer shows the student does not realize that "perimeter" only counts the outside edges, not the internal lines used for measurement.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A composite shape is made of a 4m × 3m rectangle joined to a 2m × 2m square. What is the total area?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The area is (4 × 3) + (2 × 2) = 12 + 4 = 16 sq metres. A wrong answer suggests the student failed to calculate the areas of the parts individually before summing them.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
If a composite shape has two rectangles joined, how do you find the length of a missing side?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
You look at the parallel sides; if the total length is 10m and one part is 6m, the missing part must be 10 - 6 = 4m. A wrong answer indicates difficulty in visualizing the geometry of parallel line segments.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it possible for two different composite shapes to have the same area but different perimeters? Give a reason.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes. You can rearrange the same pieces (like two rectangles) into a long thin shape or a compact square-like shape; the area stays the same, but the outer boundary length changes. A wrong answer reveals a lack of understanding of how shape configuration affects boundary length.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Draw an L-shape and explain how you would calculate its area.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The student should describe splitting it into two rectangles, measuring the length and breadth of each, finding both areas, and adding them. A poor answer fails to mention the "split" strategy or tries to use a single formula for the whole irregular shape.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often try to include the "hidden" internal line where two shapes meet when calculating the perimeter. For example, if a 5x2 rectangle is joined to a 3x3 square, they might add all sides of both shapes (5+2+5+2 + 3+3+3+3), forgetting that the touching boundary is no longer part of the "outside."
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to take a crayon and trace only the path an ant would walk if it were walking along the very outside of the shape. Tell them: "If your crayon touches an internal line that is inside the figure, stop—that line is not part of the fence." Then, have them list only the segments they traced and add those lengths.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A floor plan is made of a living room (6m x 4m) and a kitchen (3m x 2m) joined together. Find the total area.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the two shapes: a 6x4 rectangle and a 3x2 rectangle.
- step 2: Calculate Area of living room: 6m × 4m = 24 sq m.
- step 3: Calculate Area of kitchen: 3m × 2m = 6 sq m.
- step 4: Add the two areas: 24 + 6 = 30 sq m.
REASONING: Since the total area is the sum of the non-overlapping parts, adding individual areas gives the total space occupied.
ANSWER: 30 sq metres.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A garden fence consists of a 5m x 5m square and a 4m x 3m rectangle attached to one side. Find the perimeter of the combined shape.
STEPS:
- step 1: Sketch the shape and identify all outer segments.
- step 2: Determine which sides are "internal" (where they join) and exclude them.
- step 3: Add the lengths of all remaining external sides.
- step 4: If the shapes share a 3m side, subtract that from the total boundary.
- step 5: Total = 5+5+5+2+4+3+4 = 28m.
REASONING: By excluding the shared boundary, we ensure we are only measuring the "fence" that keeps the garden in, not the internal lines.
ANSWER: 28 metres.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A piece of cardboard is 10cm x 10cm. A corner of 2cm x 2cm is cut out. Find the area of the remaining cardboard.
STEPS:
- step 1: Find the area of the original large square: 10cm × 10cm = 100 sq cm.
- step 2: Find the area of the piece removed: 2cm × 2cm = 4 sq cm.
- step 3: Subtract the removed area from the original total area.
- step 4: 100 sq cm - 4 sq cm = 96 sq cm.
REASONING: Sometimes composite shapes involve "missing" parts. Subtracting the empty space from the total rectangle is often easier than splitting it into multiple smaller rectangles.
ANSWER: 96 sq centimetres.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You have two rectangles, one 5m x 2m and one 3m x 2m. If you join them along their 2m sides, what is the new perimeter? Explain your steps.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions.
STEPS:
- step 1: Visualize the joined shape.
- step 2: Determine the new length (5+3=8m) and breadth (2m).
- step 3: Apply perimeter formula for the new, larger rectangle: 2 × (8 + 2).
- step 4: Calculate result: 2 × 10 = 20m.
ANSWER: The new perimeter is 20m. By joining them on the 2m side, that side is now inside the shape, so we only count the new outer boundary formed by the 8m length and 2m width.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A farmer wants to cover a field with grass. The field is an L-shape made of a 10m x 10m square and a 5m x 10m rectangle. If grass costs 50 rupees per square metre, how much will it cost to cover the entire field?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying (cost analysis).
STEPS:
- step 1: Calculate area of square (10x10 = 100).
- step 2: Calculate area of rectangle (5x10 = 50).
- step 3: Sum the areas (100 + 50 = 150 sq m).
- step 4: Multiply by cost per unit (150 × 50).
- step 5: Compute final cost.
ANSWER: The total area is 150 sq m. At 50 rupees per sq m, the total cost is 7500 rupees.
DIFFICULTY: hard
