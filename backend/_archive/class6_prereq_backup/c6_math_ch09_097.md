## DEFINITION
A pictograph is a visual tool that uses small pictures or symbols to represent data, where each symbol stands for a specific number of items. It allows us to compare quantities at a glance by looking at the number of icons in each category.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to interpret a given pictograph with a legend (key) to answer specific questions and correctly construct a basic pictograph from a provided frequency table.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the interpretation and creation of simple pictographs using a key. It excludes comparative pictographs (side-by-side comparison of two sets of data) and digital/interactive graphing tools.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you want to tell your friends how many mangoes everyone in your group ate, but you don't want to just write a list of numbers. To make it interesting, you draw a small mango icon for every 5 fruits eaten! To begin, you look at the "key" or "scale" at the bottom of the chart, which tells you what one icon is worth. If you have a total of 15 mangoes, you simply draw 3 icons because $3 \times 5 = 15$. The key insight is that the picture is a shortcut for counting, so always check the value of one symbol first before you start counting the rows.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
If a pictograph uses one star symbol to represent 10 books, how many books does a row with 4 stars represent?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The row represents 40 books. A wrong answer (like 4) suggests the student is counting the symbols rather than multiplying by the scale, ignoring the "key" entirely.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why do we use a key or scale in a pictograph instead of just drawing one picture for every single item?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
We use a scale to save space and make the graph easier to read when the data numbers are very large. An incorrect answer indicates the student thinks symbols are just for decoration rather than a tool for representing quantities.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A canteen sells samosas. If the key says one triangle = 2 samosas, and you need to show that 7 samosas were sold, how do you represent the last one?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
You draw 3 full triangles ($3 \times 2 = 6$) and one half-triangle to represent the remaining 1. A wrong answer shows the student does not understand how to represent "parts" of the scale value.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
If a pictograph shows 6 symbols for "Monday" and 3 symbols for "Tuesday" (with 1 symbol = 5 visitors), how can you determine which day had more visitors?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
You compare the number of symbols; since 6 is greater than 3, Monday had more visitors. A wrong answer reveals the student struggles to compare frequencies visually without calculating the totals first.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it possible to use a pictograph to represent data where the counts are 12, 18, and 24? Suggest a suitable scale.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, a scale of 1 symbol = 6 units would work (2, 3, and 4 symbols respectively). A wrong answer suggests the student hasn't grasped that the scale must be a common factor of the data values for an exact representation.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a plan for a pictograph to show the number of bicycles owned by 5 friends: 4, 8, 2, 6, and 10. What scale would you choose and why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A scale of 1 symbol = 2 bicycles is ideal because all these numbers are multiples of 2. An answer using 1 symbol = 1 bicycle is acceptable but less efficient for larger sets.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often assume every symbol always represents exactly "1" unit, regardless of what the key says. For example, if the key says one symbol = 10, they will still count the symbols as "1, 2, 3..." instead of "10, 20, 30...". This happens because they are conditioned to count objects one-by-one in early primary school.
## ON_FAILURE
If a student counts symbols as "1", ask them to point to the key/legend at the bottom of the graph. Ask: "If this one icon is worth 10, does it mean we have 1 item or 10 items?" Write the multiplication sentence (e.g., $3 \times 10 = 30$) next to the symbols so they see the link between the icon count and the total value.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A library records books borrowed: Monday (15), Tuesday (25), Wednesday (20). Draw a pictograph using 1 book-icon = 5 books.
STEPS:
- 1: Identify the scale (1 icon = 5 books).
- 2: Calculate icons for Monday: 15 / 5 = 3 icons.
- 3: Calculate icons for Tuesday: 25 / 5 = 5 icons.
- 4: Calculate icons for Wednesday: 20 / 5 = 4 icons.
- 5: Draw the icons in rows labeled Mon, Tue, Wed.
REASONING: We divide the total by the scale to find out how many icons are needed to represent the total quantity.
ANSWER: Mon: 3 icons, Tue: 5 icons, Wed: 4 icons.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A farmer packs mangoes in crates. 1 crate-icon = 10 mangoes. If there are 45 mangoes, how to represent this?
STEPS:
- 1: Identify the scale: 1 icon = 10 mangoes.
- 2: Divide 45 by 10 to see how many full crates: 4 full crates.
- 3: Identify the remainder: 45 - (4 * 10) = 5 mangoes.
- 4: Represent the remainder: 5 is half of 10, so draw half a crate-icon.
- 5: Draw 4.5 crate-icons.
REASONING: When the total isn't a perfect multiple of the scale, we represent the remainder as a fraction of the symbol.
ANSWER: 4.5 icons.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A class has 60 students. 20 prefer cricket, 30 prefer football, 10 prefer hockey. Create a pictograph using 1 symbol = 10 students. How many symbols total?
STEPS:
- 1: Divide each category by 10 (Cricket: 2, Football: 3, Hockey: 1).
- 2: Add the symbol counts: 2 + 3 + 1 = 6 symbols.
- 3: Verify against total: 6 symbols * 10 students = 60 students.
- 4: Ensure the total matches the class size.
REASONING: The sum of the symbols multiplied by the scale must equal the total population of the data set.
ANSWER: 6 symbols total.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You see a pictograph where 1 symbol represents 100 rupees. If there are 3.5 symbols in the "Savings" row, how many rupees were saved? Explain your steps.
COMPETENCY: Translating visual data to numerical values.
STEPS:
- 1: Identify scale (1 = 100).
- 2: Count full symbols (3 = 300).
- 3: Identify partial symbol (0.5 = 50).
- 4: Add them together (300 + 50 = 350).
ANSWER: The total savings are 350 rupees. 3 full symbols equal 300, and half a symbol is half of 100, which is 50.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Two students make a pictograph for 40 apples. Student A uses 1 symbol = 5 apples. Student B uses 1 symbol = 10 apples. Which graph is better for a small notebook page? Why?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying visual presentations.
STEPS:
- 1: Calculate symbols for A: 40 / 5 = 8 symbols.
- 2: Calculate symbols for B: 40 / 10 = 4 symbols.
- 3: Compare size: 4 symbols take less space than 8.
- 4: Conclude: Student B's graph is better for a small space.
ANSWER: Student B's graph is better because it uses fewer symbols (4 icons vs 8 icons), making it more compact for a small space while still representing the data accurately.
DIFFICULTY: hard
