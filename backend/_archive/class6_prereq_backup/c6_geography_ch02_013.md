## DEFINITION
A weather diary is a personal, ongoing log used to record daily atmospheric observations, such as temperature, rainfall, wind, and cloud cover, to help identify long-term weather patterns. It serves as a simple, manual tool for students to track how local weather changes over days, weeks, and months.

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to maintain a daily observation log for one week, correctly recording temperature, wind direction, and cloud cover, and identify at least one simple pattern from their collected data.

## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever noticed that you need a sweater in the morning but feel hot by the afternoon, or that the wind feels different right before it rains? To start your weather diary, pick a fixed time each day—like right before you head to school—to observe the sky and check a thermometer. Write down the temperature, describe the clouds (e.g., fluffy, grey, none), and note the direction the wind is blowing. By doing this every day, you move from just "feeling" the weather to "seeing" how it changes. The key insight is that even small, simple observations recorded regularly reveal how weather behaves in your own neighborhood.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What are two examples of observations you should record in your weather diary?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Temperature and wind direction (or rainfall/cloud cover). A wrong answer suggests the student confuses weather with general climate or forgets the specific measurable elements mentioned in the definition.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why is it important to record weather at the same time every day?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
It ensures the data is consistent and comparable, allowing you to see true changes rather than differences caused by the time of day. If a student thinks it doesn't matter, they fail to understand the concept of "fair comparison" in data collection.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If you notice that the temperature drops every time the wind starts blowing from the north, what have you successfully created?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
You have identified a weather pattern. A wrong answer shows the student views the diary as a list of random events rather than a tool for logical connection.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
How does keeping a diary over three months change your understanding compared to just looking at the sky for one day?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
A single day shows current conditions, while three months reveal trends and patterns that repeat. A wrong answer suggests the student struggles to distinguish between a snapshot of weather and a series of observations.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
If your diary shows it rained every day for a week, is it accurate to say the "climate" of your city is rainy? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, because climate represents long-term averages (years), whereas a week is just a temporary weather event. A wrong answer indicates confusion between weather (short-term) and climate (long-term).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Imagine you want to know if mornings are usually windier than afternoons; how would you design your diary entry to test this?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The student should propose recording wind observations at two specific times (morning and afternoon) consistently for several days. A poor answer fails to incorporate the "consistency" or "time-variable" needed for a fair comparison.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think that "weather" is just whether it is sunny or rainy. They fail to realize that factors like wind direction, cloud type, and temperature ranges are equally important parts of a complete weather picture. They tend to record only the most obvious event (rain) and ignore the subtle changes that precede it.
## ON_FAILURE
Start by reviewing the components of weather. Ask the student to look out the window and list everything they see (e.g., leaves moving = wind, bright sun = temperature, white shapes = clouds). Then, help them create a table with these categories to use for their next three entries.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: You notice that on three different days when you recorded "High Clouds," it rained the following day. How do you record this to analyze the pattern?
STEPS:
- 1: Create a table with columns for "Date," "Cloud Type," and "Next Day Weather."
- 2: Fill in the data for those three specific days.
- 3: Compare the "Cloud Type" column to the "Next Day Weather" column.
- 4: Conclude if the observation supports your hypothesis.
REASONING: Organizing data in a table allows the student to see links between events that happen at different times.
ANSWER: A table shows that "High Clouds" acts as a potential indicator for rain, proving the diary helps predict patterns.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: You are in Delhi and the wind is coming from the Himalayas (North). How do you record this and what might it imply?
STEPS:
- 1: Identify the compass direction of the wind.
- 2: Record "North" in the "Wind Direction" column of the diary.
- 3: Note the current temperature.
- 4: Compare this with entries where wind came from the South.
REASONING: Recording the source of the wind helps link geography to temperature changes.
ANSWER: Recording "North" wind often correlates with lower temperatures, demonstrating how geography affects local weather.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Over a week, you record temperatures at 8 AM and 4 PM. You find the 4 PM temperature is consistently 5 degrees higher. What does this tell you?
STEPS:
- 1: List all 8 AM temperatures.
- 2: List all 4 PM temperatures.
- 3: Calculate the difference for each day.
- 4: Identify the consistent trend (the 5-degree rise).
- 5: Conclude that the daily cycle of solar heating impacts the temperature.
REASONING: Applying simple arithmetic to diary entries transforms raw data into a clear daily pattern.
ANSWER: The pattern shows that afternoons are consistently warmer due to the accumulation of heat throughout the day.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If you were moving to a new city, why would a 1-year weather diary from that city be more useful than a single weather report from today?
COMPETENCY: Critical Thinking / Data Analysis
STEPS:
- 1: Identify the difference between a single point of data and a collection of data.
- 2: Explain how a year-long record captures seasonal changes.
- 3: Explain why a single day represents only one state of the atmosphere.
ANSWER: A single day only shows today's weather; a year-long diary shows how the weather cycles through different seasons, providing a better understanding of what to expect.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Suppose your diary shows it was cloudy for 5 days but never rained. Does this mean your cloud observations were wrong? Explain.
COMPETENCY: Scientific Reasoning
STEPS:
- 1: Acknowledge that clouds do not always lead to rain.
- 2: Differentiate between weather observation and weather prediction.
- 3: Explain that recording the "cloudy but no rain" observation is just as important as recording rain.
ANSWER: No, the observations were not wrong. Clouds are a specific condition, while rain is a specific event; recording "no rain" is accurate data that helps refine future pattern predictions.
DIFFICULTY: hard
