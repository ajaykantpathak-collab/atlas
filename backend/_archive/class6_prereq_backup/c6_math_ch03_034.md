## DEFINITION
Subtraction of fractions is the process of finding the difference between two fractional parts, which requires them to be expressed in the same "fractional unit" (denominator) before the numerators can be subtracted.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to correctly subtract two fractions with different denominators by finding a common denominator, performing the subtraction, and simplifying the final result.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the subtraction of proper and improper fractions, including mixed numbers with different denominators. It does not cover algebraic variables in fractions or complex fractions with fractions inside fractions.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you have 3/4 of a litre of milk and you use 1/3 of a litre for your tea; how much is left? To find the answer, you cannot simply subtract 3 minus 1, because the "size" of the parts (the denominators 4 and 3) are different. You must first use equivalent fractions to make the denominators the same, like changing both to 12ths, so that you are comparing parts of the same size. Once they share the same denominator, you subtract the numerators while keeping the denominator the same. The key insight is that fractions can only be combined or separated when they refer to the same fractional unit.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the first step you must take when subtracting two fractions that have different denominators?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The first step is to find a common denominator by converting the fractions into equivalent fractions. A student who says "subtract the smaller denominator from the larger" is incorrectly applying whole-number logic to fractions.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you have 5/6 and 1/6, can you subtract them directly? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Yes, you can subtract them directly because they already have the same denominator (the same fractional unit). A student who attempts to find a common denominator here shows they do not understand that the denominator already represents the same sized part.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Solve: 3/4 - 1/5.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
1. Find LCM of 4 and 5, which is 20. 2. Convert 3/4 to 15/20 and 1/5 to 4/20. 3. Subtract numerators: 15 - 4 = 11. 4. Result is 11/20. A student who gets 2/1 (or similar) is subtracting denominators, which is a major error.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Why does 2/3 - 1/3 = 1/3, but 2/3 - 1/2 is not 1/1?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The first works because the unit is the same (thirds). The second fails because 2/3 and 1/2 represent different sized pieces; you must convert them to 4/6 and 3/6 to see the true difference is 1/6. A student who thinks the result is 1/1 ignores the physical meaning of the denominator.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Jaya says that to subtract 5/8 from 7/8, she must change the denominator to 16. Is she correct?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, she is not correct because the denominators are already the same. Changing them to 16 is unnecessary work that makes the calculation more prone to error; 7/8 - 5/8 = 2/8, which simplifies to 1/4.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a real-world problem where you would need to subtract 1/4 from 1/2.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
"If I have 1/2 kg of sugar and use 1/4 kg for a cake, how much is left?" A poor answer would just be a string of numbers like "What is 1/2 - 1/4?" without a real-world context.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often subtract the numerators and the denominators separately (e.g., 3/4 - 1/2 = 2/2). This happens because they treat fractions as two independent whole numbers rather than a single value representing a part of a whole.
## ON_FAILURE
If a student subtracts denominators, provide a paper strip. Ask them to fold it into 4 and shade 3 parts, then ask them to show where "subtracting 2 from 4" parts would leave them. Show that 3/4 is 6/8 and 1/2 is 4/8, so 6/8 - 4/8 leaves 2/8. Use the visual to explain that the denominator defines the size of the piece, not a number to be subtracted.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A tailor has 5/6 metres of cloth and uses 2/3 metres for a shirt. How much cloth is left?
STEPS:
- identify: have 5/6, use 2/3.
- method: find common denominator for 6 and 3. LCM is 6.
- convert: 2/3 = (2x2)/(3x2) = 4/6.
- compute: 5/6 - 4/6 = 1/6.
- verify: 1/6 + 4/6 = 5/6.
REASONING: Since the pieces were different sizes (6ths vs 3rds), we converted the 3rds into 6ths so we could compare them directly.
ANSWER: 1/6 metres.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Ritu had 3/4 kg of rice. She gave 1/5 kg to her neighbour. How much rice does she have now?
STEPS:
- identify: initial amount 3/4, given away 1/5.
- method: find common denominator for 4 and 5. LCM is 20.
- convert: 3/4 = 15/20 and 1/5 = 4/20.
- compute: 15/20 - 4/20 = 11/20.
- verify: 11/20 + 4/20 = 15/20 which is 3/4.
REASONING: We need a common unit to subtract pieces of different fractional sizes.
ANSWER: 11/20 kg.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A water tank is 7/8 full. If 3/4 of the tank's capacity is used in a day, what fraction of the tank remains?
STEPS:
- identify: current level 7/8, amount used 3/4.
- method: denominators 8 and 4. LCM is 8.
- convert: 3/4 = 6/8.
- compute: 7/8 - 6/8 = 1/8.
- verify: 1/8 + 6/8 = 7/8.
REASONING: We convert 3/4 to 6/8 because the tank is measured in 8ths; once both values are in 8ths, we simply subtract the number of parts.
ANSWER: 1/8 of the tank.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If you have two different fractions, a/b and c/d, why is it mathematically wrong to calculate (a-c) / (b-d)? Provide a simple example with 1/2 and 1/4 to justify your answer.
COMPETENCY: Error identification and justification.
STEPS:
- understand: the student must explain why subtraction of denominators is invalid.
- identify: use the visual of 1/2 and 1/4.
- apply: 1/2 = 2/4. 2/4 - 1/4 = 1/4.
- verify: compare 1/4 with the wrong method result: (1-1)/(2-4) = 0/-2.
ANSWER: The wrong method gives 0, but we know 1/2 is more than 1/4, so the answer must be 1/4. Subtracting denominators changes the "size" of the pieces, which is not how fractions work.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A recipe calls for 5/6 cup of sugar. You have 2/3 cup. How much more sugar do you need to reach the requirement? Explain your steps.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions.
STEPS:
- understand: need to find the difference between 5/6 and 2/3.
- identify: 5/6 - 2/3.
- apply: 2/3 = 4/6. 5/6 - 4/6 = 1/6.
- verify: 1/6 + 4/6 = 5/6.
ANSWER: You need 1/6 cup more sugar. You find the common denominator (6) to make the fractions comparable, convert 2/3 to 4/6, then subtract the numerators (5-4=1).
DIFFICULTY: medium
