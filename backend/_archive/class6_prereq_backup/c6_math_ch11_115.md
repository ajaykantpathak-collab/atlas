## DEFINITION
Constructing an angle is the process of using a protractor and a straightedge to create a specific opening between two rays that share a common endpoint. The resulting measure, expressed in degrees, represents the amount of rotation needed to turn one ray onto the other.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to draw a ray, correctly position a protractor’s centre and base line, accurately mark a degree measure, and connect the points to form an angle of the requested size.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers constructing acute, obtuse, and right angles using a protractor. It does not cover angle bisectors, reflex angle construction, or geometric constructions using only a compass.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are building a wooden ramp for your toy car and you need to ensure the slope is exactly 30 degrees so the car doesn't go too fast. To start, draw a straight line with your pencil to represent the ground—this is your first ray. Place the centre point (the origin) of your protractor on the starting point of your line and make sure the bottom line of the protractor perfectly covers your drawn line. Look at the degree markings, find 30, and make a small, neat dot on your paper. Finally, remove the protractor and use your ruler to draw a straight line from your starting point through that dot. The insight here is that the protractor is simply a tool to measure "how much" you have turned from the first line.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What are the two essential components that define an angle when you are drawing it on paper?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
An angle is defined by a common starting point (the vertex) and two rays (the arms). A wrong answer (like saying "two lines") suggests the student confuses a line segment intersection with a formal angle construction.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you align your protractor and see your ray passing through both 0° and 180°, which scale should you look at to mark an angle of 40°?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
You must look at the scale that begins at 0° on the ray you are using. A wrong answer shows the student does not understand that the protractor has two scales for different ray orientations and is just picking a random number.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
You are asked to draw a 70° angle. After drawing your first ray and placing the protractor, you mark the point at 70°. If you connect the vertex to this mark, what is the most important thing to check to ensure your construction is correct?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Check that the angle is acute (less than 90°) by looking at it—it should look "sharper" than a corner of a page. If the student draws an obtuse angle, it means they used the wrong scale on the protractor.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Why is it incorrect to place the edge of the protractor on the ray instead of the centre mark (the origin) when measuring or drawing an angle?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The centre mark is the vertex of the protractor's scale; if you use the edge, your starting point for the turn is shifted, leading to an inaccurate measurement. This reveals if the student understands the geometric "origin" of an angle.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
If a student marks 110° on the inner scale but uses the outer scale to read the position, why will the resulting angle be incorrect?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The scales on a protractor are complementary (they add up to 180°). Reading the wrong scale results in an angle of 70° (180-110) instead of 110°, proving the student is not paying attention to which "0" mark the ray is touching.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a quick "check" to tell if your classmate's 130° angle is likely correct without using a protractor.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good answer mentions that 130° is obtuse, so it must be wider than a square corner (90°) but narrower than a straight line (180°). A poor answer suggests "it looks big," which is too vague and lacks geometric reasoning.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often mix up the inner and outer scales of the protractor, especially when the ray points to the left. They consistently use the scale that starts at 180° instead of the one starting at 0°, resulting in an angle that is the supplement of the intended one (e.g., drawing 150° when 30° was required).
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to place their finger on the "0" mark where the ray starts. Ask, "Does the 0 start on the inner scale or the outer scale?" Remind them that they must follow that same scale all the way to the desired number. Have them trace the ray with a highlighter to visually lock in the correct scale before marking the point.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Vidya needs to construct an angle of 50° to design a stand for her photo frame.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Draw a ray AB using a ruler, where A is the vertex.
- Step 2: Place the protractor’s centre on point A and align the base line with ray AB.
- Step 3: Identify the 0° mark on the protractor that sits on ray AB.
- Step 4: Follow that scale to the 50° mark and place a small dot.
- Step 5: Remove the protractor and use the ruler to draw a line from A through the dot, naming the new point C.
REASONING: This process ensures the vertex is fixed at A and the rotation is measured accurately from the reference ray AB. We verify by checking that 50° is an acute angle.
ANSWER: ∠BAC = 50°
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A carpenter needs to cut a wooden plank at an angle of 120° for a shelf corner.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Draw a baseline ray XY, with X as the vertex.
- Step 2: Align the protractor’s centre exactly on X, making sure the baseline matches the ray.
- Step 3: Check which scale starts at 0° on ray XY.
- Step 4: Count along that scale until reaching 120° and make a mark.
- Step 5: Draw a line from X through the mark.
REASONING: Since 120° is obtuse, we expect it to be wider than a right angle. The alignment step is critical to prevent the angle from being skewed.
ANSWER: 120° angle constructed.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Construct an angle of 90° for a square corner, then bisect it (visually) to estimate 45°.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Draw ray PQ with P as the vertex.
- Step 2: Align the protractor and mark 90°.
- Step 3: Join P to the 90° mark to form a right angle.
- Step 4: Look at the space between the two arms and place a small tick at the halfway point (45°).
- Step 5: Join P to the new tick.
REASONING: This combines the construction of a standard right angle with the logical estimation of a division. Understanding that 90° is a square corner helps verify the accuracy of the first part.
ANSWER: 90° angle drawn and estimated 45° line created.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You are drawing an angle of 110°. Your friend draws an angle that looks like a "V" shape (very sharp). Explain why their angle is incorrect based on the properties of an obtuse angle.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- Step 1: Define an obtuse angle as being greater than 90°.
- Step 2: Compare the "V" shape to a right angle.
- Step 3: Conclude that the "V" shape is acute, not obtuse.
- Step 4: Explain that 110° must be wider than a corner.
ANSWER: The friend's angle is incorrect because 110° is an obtuse angle, which means it must be wider than a 90° right angle. A "V" shape is an acute angle (less than 90°), so it cannot be 110°.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: If you have a ray pointing to the left, explain the specific steps you must take to measure 60° correctly, focusing on the choice of protractor scale.
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction
STEPS:
- Step 1: Place the protractor centre on the vertex.
- Step 2: Align the baseline with the left-pointing ray.
- Step 3: Identify which "0" mark lies on the ray.
- Step 4: Use that scale (the one starting at that 0) to find 60°.
- Step 5: Verify the angle is acute.
ANSWER: Since the ray points left, you must find the "0" mark on the left side of the protractor scale. You must use the scale that starts at that left-hand 0 and follow it until you reach 60°. If you use the other scale, you will end up measuring 120° instead of 60°.
