## DEFINITION
Ratio is a mathematical way to compare two quantities of the same kind by division, showing how many times one quantity contains the other. Proportion is a statement that two ratios are equal, indicating that the relationship between the quantities remains consistent.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify, set up, and solve problems involving scaling quantities (like ingredients in a recipe or distances on a map) using the concept of equivalent ratios. They must consistently demonstrate how a change in one quantity necessitates a proportional change in the other.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers basic real-life scaling using ratios and proportions, such as cooking, map reading, and simple design dimensions. It excludes financial ratios (profit/investment), scientific ratios (speed/density), and advanced algebraic proportions.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are baking a cake for your family of 4, and the recipe calls for 2 cups of sugar. If 8 friends come over and you now need to feed 12 people in total, how much sugar do you need? To solve this, first find the ratio of people (4:12), which simplifies to 1:3. Because you have 3 times as many people, you must apply that same "3 times" multiplier to your sugar (2 cups × 3 = 6 cups). The key insight is that in a proportion, whatever you multiply or divide the first quantity by, you must do the same to the second to keep the relationship balanced.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
If a recipe for 2 people uses 3 spoons of salt, what is the ratio of people to salt?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The ratio is 2:3. A student might incorrectly write 3:2, which shows they are confusing the order of the quantities being compared.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you have a map with a scale of 1:500, what does that "1" represent compared to the "500"?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The "1" represents a unit on the map (like 1 cm), while the "500" represents 500 of that same unit on the ground. A student answering "1 cm is 500 meters" shows they have not yet understood that the units must be the same before conversion.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A juice mix is made with 2 parts orange to 5 parts water. If you use 6 cups of orange juice, how many cups of water do you need?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
You need 15 cups of water. Step 1: Identify ratio 2:5. Step 2: Notice 6 is 3 times 2. Step 3: Multiply 5 by 3 to get 15. A student answering 9 might have added 3 to the 5 instead of multiplying, missing the multiplicative nature of ratios.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Are the ratios 3:4 and 9:12 equivalent? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Yes, they are equivalent. If you multiply both terms of 3:4 by 3, you get 9:12. A student saying "no" might be looking at the numbers as individual values rather than as a relationship that can be scaled.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
If a building design has a length-to-width ratio of 4:2, is a building of 8m by 6m proportional to this design?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No. The design ratio simplifies to 2:1, but 8:6 simplifies to 4:3. A student saying "yes" is likely just looking for "bigger numbers" without checking if the multiplier is the same for both dimensions.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a real-life scenario where a ratio of 1:10 is used and explain why.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good answer would be a drink concentrate (1 part syrup to 10 parts water) or a scale drawing (1 cm on paper to 10 cm in real life). A poor answer would just provide two numbers without a context or a clear "part-to-part" relationship.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often use addition instead of multiplication when solving proportions. For example, in the juice problem (2:5), they might think "I added 4 to the 2, so I add 4 to the 5," resulting in 2:9 instead of 6:15. This happens because they see the numbers as a sequence rather than a multiplicative relationship.
## ON_FAILURE
If the student adds, ask them to draw the ratio using blocks: "Show me 2 blocks of orange juice and 5 blocks of water. Now, show me 6 blocks of orange juice. How many groups of 2-blocks did you make?" This forces them to see that they have created 3 identical sets of the original ratio, confirming they must have 3 sets of the water as well.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A local bakery makes a special batter using 3 cups of flour for every 2 cups of milk. If the baker decides to use 12 cups of flour, how much milk is needed?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the ratio: 3 parts flour to 2 parts milk.
- Step 2: Determine the multiplier: 12 (new flour) ÷ 3 (original flour) = 4.
- Step 3: Apply the multiplier to the milk: 2 (original milk) × 4 = 8.
- Step 4: Verify: 12:8 simplifies by dividing by 4 to get 3:2, which matches the original.
REASONING: Since the relationship between flour and milk must stay the same, the change in flour determines the change in milk. We use the multiplier to ensure the ratio remains constant.
ANSWER: 8 cups of milk.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: On a map, 1 cm represents 500 meters in real life. If two cities are 8 cm apart on the map, what is the actual distance between them in meters?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the scale: 1 cm : 500 m.
- Step 2: Identify the given map distance: 8 cm.
- Step 3: Apply the proportion: 8 cm is 8 times the map unit (1 cm × 8).
- Step 4: Multiply the real-life distance by the same factor: 500 m × 8 = 4000 m.
REASONING: Maps are scale models; the proportion between paper and ground is fixed. Scaling the map distance by 8 requires scaling the ground distance by 8.
ANSWER: 4000 meters.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A painting is designed to have a length-to-width ratio of 5:3. If the artist wants the painting to be 15 cm long, what must the width be to maintain the design?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Ratio is 5 (length) : 3 (width).
- Step 2: The goal is a length of 15 cm.
- Step 3: Find the multiplier: 15 ÷ 5 = 3.
- Step 4: Apply the multiplier to the width: 3 (original width) × 3 = 9.
- Step 5: Final dimension: 15 cm by 9 cm.
REASONING: To maintain the aspect ratio, the length and width must grow by the same factor. By finding how many times the length increased, we know how many times the width must increase.
ANSWER: 9 cm.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You are making a salad dressing with a ratio of 3 parts oil to 1 part vinegar. If you accidentally put in 4 parts vinegar, how much oil do you need to add to keep the original taste?
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Note the required ratio 3:1.
- Step 2: Note the current amount of vinegar is 4 parts.
- Step 3: Calculate oil required: 3 oil per 1 vinegar means for 4 vinegar, you need 3 × 4 = 12 parts oil.
- Step 4: Since you only had 3 oil (assuming 1:1 base), you need 9 more.
ANSWER: You need 12 parts of oil total. Since you already have 3 (based on the original 3:1), you need to add 9 more parts of oil to balance the 4 parts of vinegar.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A rectangular garden has a perimeter of 40m and a length-to-width ratio of 3:1. Find the actual length and width of the garden.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Length is 3 parts, width is 1 part.
- Step 2: Perimeter formula is 2 × (L + W) = 40.
- Step 3: L + W = 20.
- Step 4: 3 parts + 1 part = 4 parts. 4 parts = 20, so 1 part = 5.
- Step 5: Length = 3 × 5 = 15m, Width = 1 × 5 = 5m.
ANSWER: The garden is 15m long and 5m wide. This satisfies the 3:1 ratio (15:5) and the 40m perimeter (2 × (15+5) = 40).
DIFFICULTY: hard
