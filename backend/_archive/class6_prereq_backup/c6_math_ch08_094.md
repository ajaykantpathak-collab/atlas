## DEFINITION
Problem-solving in mensuration involves identifying whether a real-world scenario requires finding a boundary distance (perimeter) or an enclosed surface space (area) and applying the correct geometric formulas to solve it. It is the process of translating descriptive language into mathematical models using shapes like squares, rectangles, and triangles.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to distinguish between perimeter and area problems by reading keywords, select the correct formula, perform the calculation, and assign the appropriate unit of measurement (units for perimeter, square units for area).
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers identifying and solving basic 2D perimeter and area problems for rectangles, squares, and triangles. It excludes complex multi-step optimization, volume of 3D shapes, and non-standard geometric figures.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are asked to buy a lace for a rectangular table cover versus buying paint for the top surface of the table. To start, ask yourself: "Am I walking around the edge or covering the flat surface?" If it is the edge, you are dealing with a perimeter problem—sum up all the sides or use the rectangle formula $2 \times (\text{length} + \text{breadth})$. If you are covering the flat surface, it is an area problem—multiply the dimensions like $\text{length} \times \text{breadth}$. Always label your answer clearly: cm or m for perimeter, and sq cm or sq m for area.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the difference between a perimeter and an area in terms of the units used?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Perimeter is measured in linear units (like cm or m), while area is measured in square units (like sq cm or sq m). A student who confuses these often thinks both describe length, failing to realize area represents a two-dimensional surface space.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you have a wire of length 20 cm, why does bending it into different rectangular shapes change the area but keep the perimeter the same?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The perimeter remains constant because the total length of the wire (the boundary) does not change. The area changes because the arrangement of those boundary lines encloses a different amount of internal space. An incorrect answer suggests the student thinks area and perimeter are always linked proportionally.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A rectangular garden is 10 m long and 5 m wide. If you want to put a fence around it, how much wire do you need?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
1. Identify the shape: rectangle. 2. Identify the goal: fencing the edge, which is perimeter. 3. Use formula: $2 \times (10 + 5) = 2 \times 15 = 30$. 4. Final answer: 30 m. A student who calculates $10 \times 5 = 50$ is confusing area with perimeter.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
If a square park has a perimeter of 40 m, how can you determine the area of the park?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
1. Find the side: $40 \div 4 = 10$ m. 2. Use area formula: $10 \times 10 = 100$ sq m. A student who tries to divide the perimeter by something other than 4 or multiplies the perimeter by itself shows a lack of understanding of the relationship between side length and square properties.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it possible for two different rectangles to have the same area but different perimeters? Explain.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes. For example, a $2 \times 6$ rectangle and a $3 \times 4$ rectangle both have an area of 12 sq units, but their perimeters are 16 and 14 units respectively. Justifying this shows the student understands that dimensions, not just area, dictate the boundary length.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a problem where someone needs to calculate the area of a floor but must subtract a portion for a rug. What are the steps to solve it?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good problem involves calculating the total floor area, calculating the rug area, and subtracting the rug area from the floor area. A poor response would simply add the two areas, showing a failure to visualize the "subtraction" of space.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often confuse "perimeter" and "area" because they both involve length and width. For example, when asked to find how much fence is needed, they might multiply the length and width (area) instead of adding them and doubling (perimeter). This happens because they focus on the numbers provided rather than the physical meaning of the task (lining the edge vs. filling the space).
## ON_FAILURE
If a student fails, have them draw the shape on a piece of graph paper. Ask them to trace the boundary with a red pen (perimeter) and shade the interior with a blue pencil (area). Ask: "If you were an ant walking, which line would you follow?" and "If you were a painter, which part would you color?" This visual separation helps them distinguish the two concepts.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A rectangular room is 8 m long and 6 m wide. If the floor is to be tiled and the walls are to be fenced with a decorative strip, find the total area of the floor and the total length of the strip needed for the floor's edge.
STEPS:
- Identify: $l = 8$ m, $w = 6$ m.
- Formula: Area = $l \times w$; Perimeter = $2(l + w)$.
- Area: $8 \times 6 = 48$ sq m.
- Perimeter: $2 \times (8 + 6) = 2 \times 14 = 28$ m.
- Result: 48 sq m for tiles, 28 m for the strip.
REASONING: The floor is a 2D surface requiring area calculation, while the edge is a 1D boundary requiring perimeter. Multiplying gives the surface space, while the sum of edges gives the boundary length.
ANSWER: Area = 48 sq m, Perimeter = 28 m.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A square park has a side of 50 m. A runner goes around the park 4 times. Calculate the total distance covered.
STEPS:
- Identify: side = 50 m.
- Formula: Perimeter = $4 \times \text{side}$.
- Calculate perimeter: $4 \times 50 = 200$ m.
- Total distance: $4 \text{ rounds} \times 200 \text{ m/round} = 800$ m.
REASONING: One full round is the perimeter. Since the runner completes multiple rounds, the perimeter must be multiplied by the number of rounds to find total distance.
ANSWER: 800 m.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A rectangular plot of land is 20 m by 15 m. A square pond with a side of 4 m is built inside the plot. What is the remaining area available for a garden?
STEPS:
- Calculate total area: $20 \times 15 = 300$ sq m.
- Calculate pond area: $4 \times 4 = 16$ sq m.
- Identify operation: Subtract pond area from total area.
- Compute: $300 - 16 = 284$ sq m.
REASONING: The land area minus the space taken by the pond equals the remaining garden area. This requires calculating two separate areas before subtraction.
ANSWER: 284 sq m.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You have a piece of string 24 cm long. Can you form a rectangle with this string that has an area of 35 sq cm? Explain your reasoning.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions.
STEPS:
- Understand: String length = Perimeter = 24 cm.
- Method: $2 \times (l + w) = 24 \implies l + w = 12$.
- Test: Find two numbers that add to 12 and multiply to 35.
- Solve: $7 + 5 = 12$ and $7 \times 5 = 35$.
ANSWER: Yes, a $7 \times 5$ rectangle works. The reasoning is that the semi-perimeter is 12, and factors of 35 that sum to 12 are 7 and 5.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Two farmers have fields of equal area. Farmer A has a $10 \times 10$ m square field, and Farmer B has a $20 \times 5$ m rectangular field. Who needs more fencing material?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying.
STEPS:
- Calculate Farmer A perimeter: $4 \times 10 = 40$ m.
- Calculate Farmer B perimeter: $2 \times (20 + 5) = 50$ m.
- Compare: $50 > 40$.
- Conclusion: Farmer B needs more fencing.
ANSWER: Farmer B needs more fencing because his field is more elongated, resulting in a larger perimeter despite having the same area as Farmer A's field.
DIFFICULTY: hard
