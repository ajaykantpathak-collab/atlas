## DEFINITION
A shadow is a dark region created when an opaque object prevents light from reaching a surface. It requires three components: a source of light, an opaque object that blocks the light, and a surface (like a wall or ground) to capture the shadow.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to draw a diagram illustrating how an opaque object creates a shadow on a surface and correctly label the umbra and penumbra when a large light source is used.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the fundamental conditions for shadow formation and the distinction between umbra and penumbra. It excludes diffraction, complex soft-shadow light physics, and coloured shadows.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever noticed your shadow getting longer on the ground as the sun sets? To understand why this happens, start by placing a book between a flashlight and a wall in a dark room. You will see that the light cannot pass through the book because it is opaque, leaving a dark shape behind it. If you move the flashlight closer or further away, observe how the size of the dark patch changes. The key insight is that a shadow is simply a region where light is "missing" because something blocked its path.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What are the three essential components required to form a shadow?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The three components are a light source, an opaque object, and a surface. A wrong answer—such as omitting the surface—shows the student does not realize that light must land on something for a shadow to be "seen" by our eyes.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why does a shadow appear black or dark even if the object casting it is bright red?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
A shadow is dark because it is an area where light from the source is absent, not because of the object's color. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks the object "projects" its color onto the ground, missing the concept that shadows are simply regions of "no light."
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If you use a very small, bright bulb as a light source, will you see a penumbra? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
No, you will only see an umbra (a sharp-edged dark patch). Because the source is a "point," it cannot create the partial-shadow region (penumbra) that requires a large light source. A wrong answer reveals confusion between point sources and extended light sources.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the umbra and the penumbra in terms of light blockage.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The umbra is the darkest central part where all light from the source is blocked; the penumbra is the lighter outer part where only some light is blocked. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks both parts block the same amount of light.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
If a teacher holds a book at different distances from a lamp, when will the shadow be largest on the wall?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The shadow is largest when the object is closest to the light source. This is because the object blocks a larger "cone" of light rays as it nears the source. A wrong answer shows a lack of understanding of the geometry of light rays.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple experiment to prove that a transparent object (like a clear plastic sheet) does not form a dark shadow.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Hold the plastic sheet between a torch and a wall; because the material allows light to pass through, no dark region will appear on the wall. A poor answer would suggest using an opaque object instead, failing to test the definition of "opaque."
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that the shadow is "attached" to the object or is a projection of the object's surface features. For example, they might think the shadow of a person should show their eye color or shirt patterns. This happens because they confuse a shadow (absence of light) with a reflection (bouncing of light).
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to stand in a dark room with a torch pointed at their hand. Ask them: "Can you see the skin texture or the color of your shirt in the shadow on the wall?" Point out that the shadow is only the outline or the shape. Remind them that a shadow is simply a "hole" in the light.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A student places a 20 cm tall ruler 10 cm away from a small LED torch. The wall is 50 cm behind the ruler. If the light source is a point source, describe the shadow.
STEPS:
- Identify: The source is a point, so only an umbra forms.
- Method: Light travels in straight lines; the blockage creates a silhouette.
- Application: The ruler is opaque, so it blocks all rays hitting its surface area.
- Conclusion: A sharp-edged black rectangle (the shape of the ruler) appears on the wall.
REASONING: Since the source is a point, there is no light "bending" around the edges to create a partial shadow, ensuring a sharp boundary.
ANSWER: A sharp, dark rectangular shadow.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A large fluorescent tube light (large source) is used to cast a shadow of a ball. Why does the shadow look fuzzy?
STEPS:
- Identify: The source is large, not a single point.
- Method: Different parts of the large source are blocked by different parts of the ball.
- Application: The center of the shadow blocks the entire tube (umbra); the edges block only part of the tube (penumbra).
- Conclusion: The shadow has a dark center surrounded by a gray, fuzzy border.
REASONING: A large source creates overlapping areas of total and partial darkness, resulting in the fuzzy penumbra.
ANSWER: The presence of a penumbra causes the fuzzy edges.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A student wants to double the size of their shadow on a wall using a torch. Should they move the object closer to the wall or closer to the torch?
STEPS:
- Identify: The goal is to increase magnification of the shadow.
- Principle: The closer the object is to the light source, the more light it blocks, spreading the rays out more.
- Application: Moving the object toward the torch increases the shadow size.
- Verification: Moving it closer to the wall makes the shadow smaller and sharper.
REASONING: The light rays spread out as they move away from the source, so an object near the source intercepts a larger "slice" of the light cone.
ANSWER: Move the object closer to the torch.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You are in a room with two different light sources: a small candle and a large overhead tube light. If you hold your hand up, which source will produce a shadow with a clearer, sharper edge? Why?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- Identify: Compare point source (candle) vs large source (tube light).
- Principle: Point sources create only umbra; large sources create umbra and penumbra.
- Reasoning: The candle acts like a point source, creating a sharp umbra. The tube light creates a penumbra, which blurs the edges.
ANSWER: The candle will produce a sharper shadow because it acts as a point source, creating only an umbra without the blurring effect of a penumbra.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: During a solar eclipse, the Moon blocks the Sun. If the Sun acts as a large light source and the Moon as an opaque object, explain why people in different places on Earth see different things (some see total darkness, some partial).
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to physical models
STEPS:
- Identify: The Sun is the large source, the Moon is the opaque object, Earth is the screen.
- Principle: The shadow of the Moon has an umbra and a penumbra.
- Application: People in the umbra see a total eclipse; people in the penumbra see a partial eclipse.
ANSWER: The Moon casts a shadow with an umbra and penumbra. People standing in the dark center (umbra) experience total darkness, while those in the lighter outer region (penumbra) see only a part of the Sun covered.
DIFFICULTY: hard
