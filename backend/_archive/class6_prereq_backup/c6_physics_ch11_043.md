## DEFINITION
Multiple reflection is the process where a light ray bounces off two or more mirrors consecutively, creating several images of a single object. A kaleidoscope is an optical instrument that uses this principle by placing mirrors at an angle to create symmetrical, repeating patterns.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to predict that decreasing the angle between two mirrors increases the number of images formed. They must also be able to explain that a kaleidoscope creates patterns by reflecting colorful objects between angled mirrors.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers how mirror angles influence image count and the basic function of a kaleidoscope. It excludes complex mathematical proofs for image counts, laser physics, and the mechanics of infinity mirrors.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever stood between two mirrors in a trial room or a barbershop and noticed a trail of your own image repeating over and over? This happens because light doesn't just hit one mirror and stop; it bounces from the first mirror to the second, and back again, creating multiple reflections. To begin, imagine two mirrors touching at one edge like the pages of an open book. As you close the "book" (decreasing the angle), you will notice the number of images increases, eventually forming a circle of patterns. This is exactly how a kaleidoscope works: it uses mirrors angled to reflect small, colorful beads into a beautiful, repeating, star-like design.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
If you place two mirrors at 90 degrees to each other, how many images of an object placed between them will you see?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Three images. A wrong answer suggests the student is guessing or confusing the concept with a single reflection, showing they haven't learned the specific relationship between angles and image counts.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why does a kaleidoscope create a circular, symmetrical pattern rather than just a single blurry reflection?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The angled mirrors reflect the objects repeatedly in a circle, creating symmetry. A wrong answer (e.g., "because the beads are colorful") ignores the mirrors' role, showing a lack of understanding that the *mirrors* are what organize the light into a pattern.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If you have two mirrors and you want to see MORE images than you currently see, what should you do to the angle between them?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
You should decrease the angle. By moving the mirrors closer together, the light bounces more times in the confined space, producing a higher count of images. A wrong answer indicates the student thinks the angle and image count are unrelated.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
If a person places a pen between two mirrors angled at 60 degrees, they see 5 images. If they open the mirrors to 180 degrees (making them a single flat surface), what happens to the number of images?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The number of images decreases to one. When mirrors are flat (180 degrees), there is no "bouncing" between mirrors to create extra images, showing the student understands the "multiple" part of multiple reflection.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it possible to get infinite images using two mirrors? If so, how?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, by placing them parallel (0 degrees) to each other facing inward. A wrong answer suggests the student doesn't grasp the extreme limit of the mirror-angle relationship.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a simple experiment to test if the number of images changes as you change the angle of two hinged mirrors.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The student should describe placing an object (like a pencil) between two mirrors joined with tape, then measuring the angle with a protractor and counting the images. A poor answer lacks a controlled method, showing the student cannot translate the concept into an investigation.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think that the *size* of the mirror determines the number of images, rather than the *angle*. For example, they might argue that larger mirrors create more images, even if the angle is wide, because they "catch more light."
## ON_FAILURE
1. Ask the student to take two small mirrors and place a coin between them at a 90-degree angle. 
2. Ask them to count the images. 
3. Now, tell them to keep the mirrors the same size but squeeze them together to a 45-degree angle. 
4. Ask them to recount; they will see more images despite the mirror size remaining unchanged, proving angle is the key factor.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A student has two mirrors at 90 degrees. They place a small toy car in front of them. The student wants to change the setup to see more than 3 images. What should they do?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify current state: Angle is 90 degrees, resulting in 3 images.
- Step 2: Apply rule: The number of images increases as the angle decreases.
- Step 3: Propose change: Move the mirrors to an angle smaller than 90 degrees (e.g., 60 degrees).
- Step 4: Predict result: The number of images will increase.
REASONING: By reducing the angle, the light rays are trapped between the mirrors for more reflections, which results in a higher number of images. 
ANSWER: Decrease the angle between the mirrors.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A kaleidoscope is made with three mirrors in a triangle. If we remove one mirror, will the pattern still form? 
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the function of the mirrors: They reflect the beads to create symmetry.
- Step 2: Analyze the structure: A triangle provides three-way reflection.
- Step 3: Simulate the change: Removing a mirror leaves only two mirrors.
- Step 4: Conclude: A pattern will still form, but it will be a simple reflection (two mirrors) rather than a complex, closed, symmetrical kaleidoscope pattern.
REASONING: The complexity of the kaleidoscope pattern depends on the number of mirrors and their specific angles; removing one breaks the triangular symmetry.
ANSWER: Yes, a pattern will form, but it will lose its complex triangular symmetry.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A student is at a shop. Two mirrors are placed parallel to each other. The student stands between them and sees many images. If the student turns their body to face the side mirror instead of the front mirror, will they see more images?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify initial setup: Parallel mirrors (0 degrees) create infinite images.
- Step 2: Identify the change: Turning to face a side mirror.
- Step 3: Analyze the geometry: The student is now looking at one mirror directly, not the corridor between two parallel mirrors.
- Step 4: Predict: The "corridor" of infinite reflections is no longer in the line of sight.
- Step 5: Final conclusion: The number of visible images decreases drastically.
REASONING: Multiple reflections require the mirrors to be positioned so that light bounces between them; looking away breaks this optical path.
ANSWER: No, the number of visible images will decrease because the line of sight is no longer directed through the parallel mirror path.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Imagine you are designing a toy for a younger child. You want to use the kaleidoscope principle. If you use mirrors at a 120-degree angle, will you get a complex pattern or a very simple one compared to a 60-degree angle? Explain.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Recall the rule: Smaller angles produce more reflections.
- Step 2: Compare 120 and 60: 120 is larger than 60.
- Step 3: Apply the rule: Since 120 is larger, it will produce fewer reflections than 60.
- Step 4: Justify: Fewer reflections result in a simpler pattern.
ANSWER: You will get a simpler pattern because 120 degrees is a wider angle, which leads to fewer reflections compared to the 60-degree angle.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Why does the image in a kaleidoscope disappear if you take the lid off and let all the beads fall out? Does the mirror still work?
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction (identifying the role of the object vs. the mirror).
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify components: The kaleidoscope has mirrors and colorful objects (beads).
- Step 2: Analyze the function: Mirrors reflect whatever is in front of them.
- Step 3: Evaluate the change: Removing beads removes the source of the image.
- Step 4: Conclusion: The mirrors are working, but they have nothing to reflect.
ANSWER: The mirrors still work, but they reflect nothing because the colorful objects (the source) are gone; the kaleidoscope requires both the mirror structure and the objects to function.
DIFFICULTY: hard
