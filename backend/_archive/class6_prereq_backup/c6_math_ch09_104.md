## DEFINITION
Data handling is the process of collecting, organizing, and displaying raw information as facts, numbers, or observations to make it easier to understand, compare, and draw conclusions. It involves turning a disorganized list of information into meaningful summaries like frequency tables, pictographs, or bar graphs.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
analyze
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to organize raw data into a frequency table, choose an appropriate scale for a bar graph, and interpret information from visual representations to answer specific analytical questions.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the collection and organization of data, frequency distribution tables, pictographs, and bar graphs as per Class 6 standards. It does not cover advanced statistical measures like mean, median, mode, or probability theory.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine your teacher asks everyone in class to name their favorite fruit, and you end up with 40 different names written randomly on a sheet of paper—how do you quickly find out which fruit is the class favorite? Start by "organizing" the data: create a table with two columns, one for the item (fruit) and one for the count (frequency). Use "tally marks" (vertical lines) to keep count as you scan the list to avoid missing or double-counting any items. Once you have your counts, if the numbers are large, choose a "scale" (e.g., 1 symbol = 5 students) to draw a clean pictograph or a bar graph. The key insight is that the graph isn't just a drawing; it is a visual tool designed to let your brain compare the sizes of categories at a single glance.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the purpose of using "tally marks" when counting a long list of items?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Tally marks help in keeping an accurate count of items as you process a list one by one without losing track. If a student says they are just for decoration or to make the list look "math-y," they have missed the fundamental purpose of organization for error-free counting.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If a pictograph uses a symbol of a star to represent 10 books, what does "half a star" represent?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Half a star represents 5 books. If a student says "it depends" or "it represents 1 book," they do not understand that the scale applies proportionally to the symbol, which is a common error when interpreting partial images in data displays.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
You have 30 apples, 45 oranges, and 15 mangoes. If you want to draw a bar graph, what would be a logical scale to use on the vertical axis?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
A logical scale would be "1 unit = 5 items" or "1 unit = 10 items." If a student suggests "1 unit = 1 item," they fail to realize the graph would become too tall to fit on a standard page; if they suggest "1 unit = 100 items," the bars would be too tiny to compare effectively.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Why do we leave equal gaps between bars in a bar graph?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Equal gaps ensure that the bars are visually independent and distinct, making it easy to compare the height of one category against another. A student who thinks the gaps are arbitrary or just for "extra space" does not recognize that a bar graph represents discrete categories that should not be confused with continuous measurements.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Look at a bar graph showing rainfall over five days. If the bar for Monday is twice as high as the bar for Tuesday, can we conclude it rained exactly twice as much? Explain.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, because in a standard bar graph, the height of the bar is directly proportional to the frequency. If a student says "no," they might be overthinking or failing to grasp that the linear scale is specifically designed to make such comparisons accurate.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a scenario where a bar graph would be a better choice than a pictograph.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A bar graph is better when the data values are very large (e.g., population of cities) or very precise (e.g., 273 versus 274), where drawing icons would be tedious or impossible to display accurately. A poor answer would focus on "looks" rather than the efficiency of representing large numbers.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often confuse the "scale" with the "frequency." They might think that if a pictograph key says "1 image = 5 units," then the number of images they draw is the actual total. For example, if they need to represent 20 items, they draw 20 images instead of 4 images, forgetting to divide by the scale.
## ON_FAILURE
If a student draws too many symbols, ask them: "If each symbol is a 'bundle' of 5, how many bundles do you need to reach 20?" Have them physically group items into sets of 5 to visualize the division. Remind them that the symbol is a placeholder for a group, not a single unit.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A shopkeeper sold the following number of pens in a week: Monday (20), Tuesday (35), Wednesday (25), Thursday (30), Friday (40). Create a bar graph with a scale where 1 unit = 5 pens.
STEPS:
- Step 1: List the frequencies: Mon=20, Tue=35, Wed=25, Thu=30, Fri=40.
- Step 2: Divide each frequency by the scale (5) to find the bar heights: Mon=4, Tue=7, Wed=5, Thu=6, Fri=8.
- Step 3: Draw a vertical axis representing the number of units (1 to 8) and a horizontal axis for the days.
- Step 4: Draw bars for each day at the calculated heights with equal widths and gaps.
REASONING: Scaling simplifies complex numbers into manageable "units" that fit on paper while maintaining the ratio between days.
ANSWER: A bar graph with bars of heights 4, 7, 5, 6, and 8 units respectively.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: In a class of 25 students, the shoe sizes are: 4, 5, 5, 6, 4, 4, 5, 6, 7, 5, 4, 5, 6, 5, 4, 4, 5, 5, 6, 7, 5, 4, 4, 5, 6. Organize this into a frequency table.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify all unique shoe sizes: 4, 5, 6, 7.
- Step 2: Create a table with "Shoe Size" and "Tally Marks" columns.
- Step 3: Go through the data list, putting a tally mark for each size.
- Step 4: Count the tally marks to find the frequency for each size.
REASONING: Organizing raw, scattered data into a table allows us to see patterns (like the most common shoe size) immediately.
ANSWER: 4: 8 tallies (Freq 8), 5: 10 tallies (Freq 10), 6: 5 tallies (Freq 5), 7: 2 tallies (Freq 2).
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A factory produces 200 lightbulbs on Monday, 350 on Tuesday, and 500 on Wednesday. Represent this using a pictograph where one symbol = 50 bulbs.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify frequencies: Mon=200, Tue=350, Wed=500.
- Step 2: Determine number of symbols: Mon=200/50=4, Tue=350/50=7, Wed=500/50=10.
- Step 3: Select a symbol (e.g., a lightbulb icon).
- Step 4: Draw the symbols in rows corresponding to the days.
REASONING: Pictographs use symbols to make data "at-a-glance" readable for people who might not want to read raw numbers.
ANSWER: Mon: 4 icons, Tue: 7 icons, Wed: 10 icons.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You are given a bar graph showing the sales of different fruits in rupees. The bar for Mangoes is 8 units high, and the bar for Apples is 4 units high. If 1 unit represents 100 rupees, how many more rupees did the shopkeeper earn from Mangoes than Apples?
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Calculate total for Mangoes: 8 units * 100 = 800 rupees.
- Step 2: Calculate total for Apples: 4 units * 100 = 400 rupees.
- Step 3: Find the difference: 800 - 400 = 400 rupees.
ANSWER: The shopkeeper earned 400 rupees more from Mangoes. The difference in bar height (4 units) multiplied by the scale (100) confirms this.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Two students draw a bar graph for the same data. Student A uses 1 unit = 2 books. Student B uses 1 unit = 10 books. Which graph will look taller, and why?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand how scale affects height (Height = Frequency / Scale).
- Step 2: Compare the two scales: A divides by 2, B divides by 10.
- Step 3: Conclude that smaller divisors (Student A) result in larger numbers (taller bars).
ANSWER: Student A's graph will look taller because the scale is smaller. When you divide a number by 2, you get a larger value than dividing by 10, meaning the bars will have more units of height on the graph.
DIFFICULTY: hard
