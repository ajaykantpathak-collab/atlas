## DEFINITION
Rounding decimals is the process of approximating a number to a specific place value to make it easier to work with, by looking at the digit immediately to the right of the target place. If that digit is 5 or greater, we increase the target digit by one; if it is 4 or less, we keep the target digit the same.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify the target place value, check the "deciding" digit to the right, and correctly increase or keep the target digit to produce the rounded decimal.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers rounding decimals to the tenths (one decimal place) and hundredths (two decimal places). It excludes rounding in scientific notation and does not include calculating error bounds.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are at a grocery store and a bag of apples costs ₹45.78, but you only have coins in increments of 10 paise or whole rupees. How do you decide what to pay? To round a number like 3.47 to one decimal place, first underline the target place (the tenths digit, 4). Next, look at the digit to its immediate right (the hundredths digit, 7). Since 7 is 5 or more, you "round up" by adding 1 to your target, making 3.4 become 3.5. If the digit were 4 or less, you would simply leave the target digit as it is.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the rule for rounding up a decimal digit?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
If the digit to the right of the target place is 5, 6, 7, 8, or 9, you increase the target digit by one. A wrong answer here suggests the student has not memorized the threshold for rounding, confusing it with simple truncation.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
When rounding 5.62 to the nearest tenth, why do we ignore the 2?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
We ignore the 2 when deciding whether to round up because only the digit immediately to the right of the target place (the hundredths place) determines the rounding. A student who tries to include the 2 in the calculation shows they don't understand the "look-one-digit-right" rule.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Round 7.84 to the nearest tenth. Show your steps.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The answer is 7.8. Step 1: Identify the tenths place (8). Step 2: Look at the hundredths place (4). Step 3: Since 4 is less than 5, keep the 8 as is. A wrong answer suggests the student is rounding based on the magnitude of the whole number rather than the specific decimal place.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare rounding 2.35 to 2.4 and rounding 2.32 to 2.3. What do they have in common?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Both follow the rule of looking at the hundredths place to decide the tenths place. A student who cannot identify this commonality is likely guessing the answer rather than applying the standard algorithm.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it ever correct to round 4.49 to 4.5 when rounding to one decimal place? Why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, because the hundredths digit (9) is 5 or greater, which forces the tenths digit (4) to round up to 5. Students who argue this is incorrect likely fail to see how the "rounding up" rule works across boundaries.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a decimal that, when rounded to the nearest tenth, becomes 5.5, but is not 5.5 itself.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Examples like 5.46, 5.47, 5.54, or 5.52 are correct. An incorrect answer (like 5.6) shows a failure to understand the range of numbers that round to a specific value.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often try to round by looking at the very last digit in a long decimal (e.g., rounding 3.472 by looking at the 2 instead of the 7). They mistakenly think all digits to the right contribute to the "round up" decision.
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to circle only the digit immediately to the right of the target place. Tell them: "Ignore everything else to the right of that circle; it is just 'noise' that doesn't change the rounding rule." Practice with three-digit decimals to reinforce this focus.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A tailor measures a cloth length as 12.67 metres. Round this to the nearest tenth of a metre.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the tenths place (6).
- step 2: Identify the digit to the right (7).
- step 3: Compare 7 with the rounding rule (7 > 5).
- step 4: Round up the 6 to 7.
- step 5: Result is 12.7 metres.
REASONING: Since 7 is greater than 5, the tenths digit must increase by 1 to indicate a closer approximation.
ANSWER: 12.7 metres
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: The weight of a packet of pulses is 2.34 kg. Round this to the nearest tenth of a kilogram.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the tenths place (3).
- step 2: Identify the digit to the right (4).
- step 3: Compare 4 with the rounding rule (4 < 5).
- step 4: Keep the 3 as it is.
- step 5: Result is 2.3 kg.
REASONING: Since 4 is less than 5, the tenths digit remains unchanged because the value is closer to the current tenth than the next one.
ANSWER: 2.3 kg
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A runner completes a track in 45.685 seconds. Round this to two decimal places (hundredths).
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the hundredths place (8).
- step 2: Identify the digit to the right (thousandths place, 5).
- step 3: Apply rule: 5 is 5 or more, so round up the 8 to 9.
- step 4: Drop the thousandths digit.
- step 5: Result is 45.69 seconds.
REASONING: Rounding to hundredths requires looking at the thousandths column to see if we push the hundredths value up.
ANSWER: 45.69 seconds
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You have two lengths: 8.52 metres and 8.58 metres. If you round both to the nearest tenth, what do you notice? Explain why.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- step 1: Round 8.52 (look at 2, round down) -> 8.5.
- step 2: Round 8.58 (look at 8, round up) -> 8.6.
- step 3: Compare results (8.5 vs 8.6).
- step 4: Explain that rounding highlights the distance between the values relative to the tenth mark.
ANSWER: They round to different values because 8.52 is closer to 8.5, while 8.58 is closer to 8.6.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A bottle holds 1.448 litres of water. If you round to the nearest tenth, you get 1.4. If you round to the nearest hundredth, you get 1.45. Why are these different?
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction
STEPS:
- step 1: Rounding to tenths looks at the hundredths place (4), which is < 5, so 1.4.
- step 2: Rounding to hundredths looks at the thousandths place (8), which is > 5, so 1.45.
- step 3: Explain that rounding to a more precise place value uses different "check" digits.
ANSWER: The results differ because the "deciding digit" changes depending on which place value you are rounding to.
