## DEFINITION
Interest is the price paid for using someone else's money; it acts as a reward when you save money in a bank and a cost when you borrow money from one. It is typically expressed as a percentage of the principal amount over a set period, usually one year.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to calculate simple interest for a given principal and rate, and explain whether a specific financial transaction represents a 'cost' or a 'reward'.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers simple and compound interest concepts and factors affecting rates. Does not cover interest rate derivatives, monetary policy, or inflation indexing.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever wondered why, if you put 1,000 rupees in a piggy bank for a year, you still have 1,000 rupees, but if you put it in a bank account, it grows? Begin by thinking of money as a tool that can be rented. When you deposit money, you are renting your money to the bank, and they pay you "rent" called interest. When you borrow, you are renting the bank's money, so you pay them "rent" for the privilege. The key insight is that interest is simply the price of time—the bank pays for the time they use your money, and you pay for the time you use theirs.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the name of the extra money you earn for keeping your savings in a bank account?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The extra money is called interest. A wrong answer suggests the student confuses interest with a "bonus" or "gift," ignoring that it is a calculated payment for the use of money.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you borrow 5,000 rupees and pay back 5,500 rupees, why did you have to pay the extra 500 rupees?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
You paid the extra 500 rupees as the cost of borrowing the bank's money. A wrong answer shows the student doesn't realize that borrowed money has a "rental fee" attached to it.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If you have 2,000 rupees and the bank offers 5% interest per year, how much interest will you have after one year?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
You will have 100 rupees. (Calculation: 2,000 × 0.05 = 100). A wrong answer indicates trouble with basic percentage multiplication.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Why might a bank charge a higher interest rate for a loan to a person they don't know well compared to a long-time customer?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The bank perceives a higher risk of not being paid back, so they charge more to cover that risk. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks interest rates are fixed and random rather than based on risk factors.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it better to have a higher interest rate when you are saving money, or when you are borrowing money? Explain.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
It is better to have a higher rate when saving (to earn more) and a lower rate when borrowing (to pay less). A wrong answer reveals a failure to distinguish between interest as a benefit versus interest as a burden.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Imagine you want to buy a bicycle that costs 5,000 rupees. You have 2,000 rupees. If you borrow the rest, what factors should you consider before agreeing to the interest rate?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The student should mention the ability to repay the monthly amount and the total cost of the interest over time. A poor answer ignores the long-term impact of the debt on their personal budget.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think interest is a "fixed" amount regardless of the principal, like a flat fee. They fail to realize it is a percentage, meaning the more money involved, the larger the interest payment or reward becomes.
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to calculate 10% of 100, then 10% of 1,000. Point out that the "10%" rule remains the same, but the actual money changes because the starting amount (principal) changed.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: You deposit 10,000 rupees in a savings account at 6% annual interest. How much interest do you earn in one year?
STEPS:
- Identify Principal: 10,000 rupees.
- Identify Rate: 6% (or 0.06).
- Identify Time: 1 year.
- Compute: 10,000 × 0.06 = 600.
- Result: You earn 600 rupees.
REASONING: By converting the percentage to a decimal, we find the specific portion of the principal that represents the annual reward.
ANSWER: 600 rupees.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A shopkeeper borrows 20,000 rupees to buy a new fridge for his store at 10% annual interest. How much interest must he pay after one year?
STEPS:
- Identify Principal: 20,000 rupees.
- Identify Rate: 10% (or 0.10).
- Identify Time: 1 year.
- Compute: 20,000 × 0.10 = 2,000.
- Result: He pays 2,000 rupees in interest.
REASONING: The interest acts as the 'cost' of using the bank's money for the business equipment.
ANSWER: 2,000 rupees.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: You have 5,000 rupees. Bank A offers 5% interest, and Bank B offers 8% interest. If you want to grow your savings, which bank should you choose and how much more will you earn in one year?
STEPS:
- Calculate interest in Bank A: 5,000 × 0.05 = 250 rupees.
- Calculate interest in Bank B: 5,000 × 0.08 = 400 rupees.
- Compare earnings: 400 - 250 = 150.
- Conclude: Choose Bank B.
- Result: You earn 150 rupees more.
REASONING: Comparing rates shows how small percentage differences significantly impact the final reward.
ANSWER: Bank B; 150 rupees more.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Why does a bank pay interest to someone who deposits money?
COMPETENCY: Economic Reasoning
STEPS:
- Identify the bank's role in lending.
- Explain the bank's need for capital.
- Connect interest as a reward for the depositor.
ANSWER: Banks use the money you deposit to lend to other people who need loans. Since the bank makes money from those borrowers, they share a portion of that profit with you as a "reward" for letting them use your money.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: If you take a loan, does the interest you pay benefit the bank? How?
COMPETENCY: System Analysis
STEPS:
- Define the bank's business model (borrowing from depositors, lending to borrowers).
- Identify the interest payment as revenue for the bank.
- Contrast the bank's cost (interest paid to depositors) with revenue (interest received from borrowers).
ANSWER: Yes, it benefits the bank. The bank charges a higher rate to borrowers than it pays to depositors. The difference between what they receive from borrowers and what they pay to depositors is how the bank covers its costs and makes a profit.
DIFFICULTY: hard
