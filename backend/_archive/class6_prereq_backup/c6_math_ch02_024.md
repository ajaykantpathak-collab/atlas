## DEFINITION
The properties of integer operations are fundamental rules that describe how we can rearrange or group numbers during addition and multiplication without changing the final result, and how multiplication interacts with addition. These properties include the commutative, associative, and distributive laws.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify, name, and apply the commutative, associative, and distributive properties to solve integer expressions correctly and explain why a given rearrangement of numbers maintains or violates these properties.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the commutative, associative, and distributive properties for integer addition and multiplication. It excludes formal proofs, field axioms, and ring theory; it focuses on practical application for Class 6 learners.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are at the 'Building of Fun' lift; if you press the '+' button twice, then the '-' button once, does the order matter for your final floor? Start by testing small integers like 3 and -5 to see if swapping their order (3 + (-5) vs (-5) + 3) gives the same result. Notice that for addition and multiplication, the result stays the same, which we call the Commutative Property. Once you see this, try grouping three numbers in different ways, like (2 + 3) + 4 versus 2 + (3 + 4), to see if the grouping (Associative Property) changes the outcome. Finally, look at 3 x (4 + (-2)) and see how the number 3 "distributes" to both numbers inside the parentheses.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What does the commutative property state about the addition of two integers, 'a' and 'b'?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The commutative property states that a + b = b + a. A wrong answer usually indicates the student confuses the property with the associative property or fails to realize that the order of addition is interchangeable.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you have the expression (2 + 3) + 4, how can you rewrite it using the associative property?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
It can be rewritten as 2 + (3 + 4). A wrong answer suggests the student does not understand that associativity allows for changing the grouping of terms without changing the total sum.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Use the distributive property to evaluate 5 x (10 + (-2)).
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
5 x (10 + (-2)) = (5 x 10) + (5 x -2) = 50 + (-10) = 40. A wrong answer often shows the student is not multiplying the outside number by both terms inside the brackets.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the expressions ((-3) x 2) x 4 and (-3) x (2 x 4). Will they result in the same value? Why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Yes, they will result in the same value because of the associative property of multiplication, which states that the grouping of factors does not change the product. An incorrect answer here shows a failure to recognize that multiplication follows the same associative rules as addition.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate the following: Is the expression 10 - (5 + 2) equal to (10 - 5) + 2? Justify your answer.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, 10 - 7 = 3, while 5 + 2 = 7. The subtraction operation is not associative, and a wrong answer reveals the misconception that all operations possess the same properties as addition and multiplication.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a numerical example using three different integers that demonstrates the distributive property.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A valid example would be 2 x (3 + (-1)) = (2 x 3) + (2 x -1) = 4, where both sides equal 4. A poor answer would show a failure to correctly balance both sides of the equation or a misunderstanding of how the multiplier distributes.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often try to apply the commutative and associative properties to subtraction and division (e.g., thinking 10 - 5 = 5 - 10). This happens because they over-generalize the rules they learned for addition and multiplication to all operations.
## ON_FAILURE
If a student applies these properties to subtraction, ask them to calculate the two sides separately. Say: "Let's check 5 - 3 and 3 - 5. Do you get the same result? If the result changes, the property cannot hold for subtraction." Then, have them use the 'Building of Fun' model to visualize that moving down 3 then down 5 is not the same as moving down 5 then down 3 in a way that preserves the starting reference.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A trader at a market has 4 bags, each containing 10 apples and 3 rotten apples (represented as -3). Use the distributive property to find the total number of good apples.
STEPS:
- step 1: Write the expression as 4 x (10 + (-3)).
- step 2: Identify the distributive property: a x (b + c) = (a x b) + (a x c).
- step 3: Apply: (4 x 10) + (4 x -3).
- step 4: Calculate: 40 + (-12).
- step 5: Final total: 28 apples.
REASONING: The distributive property allows us to calculate the good apples in one bag and multiply by the number of bags, or calculate all apples and all rotten ones separately; the result is identical.
ANSWER: 28 apples
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Calculate the total points for a team that scored 3 rounds of 5 points each and 3 rounds of -2 points each. Use the distributive property.
STEPS:
- step 1: Express as 3 x (5 + (-2)).
- step 2: Distribute the 3: (3 x 5) + (3 x -2).
- step 3: Compute the products: 15 + (-6).
- step 4: Combine the integers: 15 - 6 = 9.
- step 5: Verify by grouping first: 3 x (3) = 9.
REASONING: By distributing the common factor (3 rounds), we show that multiplying the total sum of points per round is the same as finding the points for each type of round and adding them.
ANSWER: 9 points
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Simplify the expression (15 + (-7)) + (-3) and compare it to 15 + ((-7) + (-3)) using the associative property.
STEPS:
- step 1: Calculate the left side: (15 - 7) + (-3) = 8 - 3 = 5.
- step 2: Calculate the right side: 15 + (-7 - 3) = 15 + (-10) = 5.
- step 3: Observe that 5 = 5.
- step 4: Conclude that the associative property holds for these integers.
REASONING: The associative property tells us that when adding three integers, the grouping does not matter because addition is a cumulative process of movement on the number line.
ANSWER: 5
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A student claims that because 2 + 3 = 3 + 2, then 2 - 3 must be equal to 3 - 2. Evaluate this claim and explain why it is correct or incorrect using the number line.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- step 1: Calculate 2 - 3 = -1.
- step 2: Calculate 3 - 2 = 1.
- step 3: Compare -1 and 1.
- step 4: Explain that since -1 is not equal to 1, the commutative property does not apply to subtraction.
ANSWER: The claim is incorrect. The commutative property only applies to addition and multiplication. On a number line, starting at 2 and moving 3 units left lands you at -1, while starting at 3 and moving 2 units left lands you at 1.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: You are given the expression (8 x 12) + (8 x 8). Rewrite this expression using the distributive property in reverse (factoring) and calculate the result.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to mathematical expressions
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the common factor '8'.
- step 2: Rewrite as 8 x (12 + 8).
- step 3: Sum the terms in the bracket: 12 + 8 = 20.
- step 4: Multiply by the common factor: 8 x 20 = 160.
- step 5: Verify by calculating the original expression: 96 + 64 = 160.
ANSWER: The expression is 8 x (12 + 8), which equals 160. Factoring allows us to simplify complex-looking additions by grouping common multipliers.
