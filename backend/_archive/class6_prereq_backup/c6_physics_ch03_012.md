## DEFINITION
A simple electrical circuit is a complete, continuous path of conducting material that allows electric current to flow from a power source through a device and back again. It consists of four essential components: a power source (cell/battery), a load (bulb), connecting wires, and an optional switch.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to assemble a circuit that successfully lights a bulb and explain why the bulb stops glowing if a wire is disconnected or the path is broken.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the basic requirements and construction of a simple single-loop circuit. It excludes parallel circuits, series circuits, electrical measurements (voltage/current), and safety devices like circuit breakers.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever wondered how the light bulb in your room turns on the moment you flip a switch? To understand this, imagine electricity as water flowing through a pipe; for the water to move, the pipe must be a complete loop without any holes. Start by placing a battery in front of you—this is your "pump" or power source. Connect one wire from the positive terminal to the bottom of a small bulb, and another wire from the negative terminal to the side of the bulb. If you touch the loose ends of the wires to the bulb's contact points, the bulb glows because you have created a "complete path." The key insight is that electricity needs a closed, unbroken loop to travel; if even one wire is loose, the "pipe" is broken, and the current cannot flow.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What are the four basic components required to build a simple electrical circuit?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The four components are a power source (battery or cell), a load (bulb or motor), connecting wires, and a switch. Answering only with "wires and a battery" reveals the student ignores the need for a load to use the energy and a complete path to flow.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If your bulb is connected to a battery with a single wire, why does it not light up?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The bulb does not light up because the path is incomplete; electricity requires a closed loop to return to the source. A student who thinks one wire is enough is failing to understand the requirement for a two-way path (to and from the battery).
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
You have a battery, a bulb, and two wires. If you connect one wire to the positive side of the battery and the other end of that same wire to the negative side of the battery, what happens?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The current bypasses the bulb, creating a short circuit; the bulb will not light, and the battery may get hot. This tests the student's understanding that the load (bulb) must be part of the path, not just sitting nearby.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
If a circuit is "open," what does that mean for the electricity flowing through it?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
An "open" circuit means there is a gap in the path, so the electricity stops flowing immediately. Students who confuse "open" with "on" are struggling with the literal meaning of a circuit as a "circle" or "closed loop."
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
If a friend suggests that the bulb only needs to touch one terminal of the battery to glow, how would you prove them wrong using your circuit materials?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
You would demonstrate that the bulb only glows when contact is made with both the positive and negative terminals, completing the path. This requires the student to judge the claim based on the necessity of a closed circuit.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a way to add a "switch" using a simple paperclip and two thumbtacks to control the bulb.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The paperclip should be attached to one thumbtack so it can pivot and touch the second thumbtack, closing the gap in the wire. A poor answer fails to realize that the switch must be placed in the *path* of the wire to control the flow.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe electricity "pours" into the bulb from one wire and just disappears. They fail to realize the circuit is a loop where energy must return to the battery. For example, they might draw a diagram with a wire going from the battery to the bulb, but no return wire, thinking the bulb is a "sink" for electricity.
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to trace the path of electricity with their finger on a drawing of a circuit, starting from the positive terminal, through the bulb, and back to the negative terminal. If they get stuck at the bulb, ask: "If the electricity goes in, where does it go next to finish its journey?" Explain that electricity is like a circular road—if the road doesn't lead back home, the cars (electrons) can't finish the trip.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: You have a battery (5 cm wire), a bulb, and another wire (5 cm). You need to connect them to light the bulb. List the steps.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the two contact points on the bulb (the side and the bottom).
- Step 2: Attach one wire to the positive terminal of the battery and the bottom of the bulb.
- Step 3: Attach the second wire to the negative terminal of the battery.
- Step 4: Touch the free end of the second wire to the side of the bulb to close the loop.
REASONING: Following the path from one battery terminal, through the load, to the other terminal ensures a continuous circuit. Without this sequence, the loop remains incomplete.
ANSWER: The bulb will glow when the second wire completes the contact.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Your circuit is built, but the bulb won't light. What are the 4 things you should check?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Check if the battery is inserted correctly (terminals).
- Step 2: Check if both wires are firmly touching the metal parts of the bulb.
- Step 3: Check if the wires are frayed or broken (is the path continuous?).
- Step 4: Check if the bulb filament is intact or the battery is drained.
REASONING: Troubleshooting requires verifying each part of the "complete path" requirement one by one. If any part of the loop is broken, the circuit fails.
ANSWER: The bulb will light once the continuity of the loop is restored.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A student connects a bulb to a battery using a long string instead of a copper wire. Why does the bulb not light, and what replaces the string?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify that the string is an insulator and does not conduct electricity.
- Step 2: Identify the property of the material needed (conductive).
- Step 3: Replace the string with a copper wire.
- Step 4: Ensure the wire connections are tight at the terminals and bulb.
REASONING: Electricity requires a material that allows electrons to move freely; non-conductive materials like string act as a wall, not a path.
ANSWER: The bulb lights because the copper wire provides a conductive path for the current.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Imagine a circuit where a bulb is lit. If you cut the wire in the middle, what happens to the electricity? Explain your reasoning.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to physical models.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand that current flow requires a continuous path.
- Step 2: Identify that cutting the wire creates a physical gap.
- Step 3: Conclude that the gap stops the flow.
ANSWER: The bulb goes out instantly because the circuit becomes "open," preventing the charge from completing its path back to the battery.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A student claims that the switch on the wall works because it "adds" electricity to the room. Critique this claim using the concept of a simple circuit.
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Analyze the function of a switch (making/breaking connection).
- Step 2: Compare the claim to the function.
- Step 3: Identify the error (the switch doesn't create energy).
- Step 4: Formulate the correction.
ANSWER: The claim is incorrect; the switch does not add electricity. It simply closes the loop to allow electricity from the source to flow through the bulb.
