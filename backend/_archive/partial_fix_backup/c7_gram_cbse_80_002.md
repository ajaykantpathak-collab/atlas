## DEFINITION
Sentences multiply their verbs in two legal ways: a compound sentence joins two independent clauses with a coordinator (and/but/or/so) — equals side by side. A complex sentence attaches a dependent clause to a main clause with a subordinator (because/when/although) — one leans on the other.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student distinguishes compound from complex by identifying the joiner type, and converts simple pairs into both structures.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers the two multi-clause structures at identification level. Does NOT cover the full transformation system (class 10-11).

## WALKTHROUGH
Equal boxes vs leaning boxes: 'I was tired, AND I slept' — compound, two equals. 'BECAUSE I was tired, I slept' — complex, the reason leans on the result. The joiner is the tell: and/but/or/so/yet/for = coordinators; because/when/although/if/since = subordinators.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students classify by clause count — any two-clause sentence becomes 'complex' to them, missing that the JOINER type is the actual diagnostic. 'And' between equals is compound, never complex.

## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Count the clauses. Step 2: Find the joiner. Step 3: Coordinator → compound; subordinator → complex. Step 4: If two clauses with NO joiner — that is an error (comma splice), not a type.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Classify: 'She was ill, but she attended the exam.'

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Compound — 'but' is a coordinator joining two equal clauses. A wrong answer 'complex' reveals the count-only classifier — two clauses does not mean complex.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Classify: 'Although she was ill, she attended the exam.'

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Complex — 'although' subordinates the first clause. Same meaning as the previous item, different grammar — the joiner decides the class.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
'It rained, we stayed home.' — which structure is this?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
None — it is a comma splice (error). Needs 'so' (compound), 'because' flipped (complex), or a full stop. A wrong answer calling it compound shows the comma-power misconception.
