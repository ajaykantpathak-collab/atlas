## DEFINITION
The Harappan economy was a thriving system based on agriculture, animal husbandry, and skilled craftsmanship that allowed people to settle in large, organized cities. It represents a transition from small farming villages to a complex urban society with diverse food sources and specialized goods.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to list at least three agricultural products of the Harappans and explain how their diverse economy (farming, animals, and crafts) supported a settled city life.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the Harappan food sources, domestic animals, and the role of specialized crafts. It excludes technical manufacturing processes, complex social hierarchies, and specific long-distance trade routes.
## WALKTHROUGH
Think about the lunchbox you bring to school today—does it have grains, vegetables, or perhaps something made of cotton? Just like your life today, the Harappan people had to organize their day around getting food and making useful things. Imagine living near the Indus river thousands of years ago; you wouldn't just be hunting; you would be busy harvesting wheat and barley, tending to cattle, and working with clay. They were one of the first groups to turn cotton into fabric, which changed how people dressed forever. By looking at these basics, we see that Harappan life wasn't just about survival—it was about building a prosperous, daily lifestyle.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Name two crops that the Harappan people grew for food.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The Harappan people grew wheat and barley. A wrong answer here shows the student has not yet distinguished between early hunter-gatherers and the agricultural Harappan civilization.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why was the development of cotton cultivation significant for the Harappan people?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
It was significant because they were among the first people in the world to cultivate and weave cotton, showing they had advanced skills in clothing production. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks of the Harappans only as farmers and misses their role as innovators in textiles.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If you were an archaeologist finding a piece of pottery with red slip and black designs, what would this tell you about the Harappan economy?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
It tells us that they had specialized craftspeople who didn't just make basic bowls, but decorated them, indicating a prosperous economy where people had time for art. A wrong answer reveals the student views these items as mere "trash" rather than evidence of economic specialization.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
How did keeping domestic animals like cattle and sheep help the Harappans survive alongside farming?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Animals provided a steady source of food (milk/meat) and labor, acting as a "backup" or supplement when harvests were unpredictable. A wrong answer shows a misunderstanding of how a diverse economy provides more security than relying on crops alone.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it accurate to say the Harappan economy was based *only* on farming? Justify your answer.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, it is not accurate because they also relied on fishing, hunting, and highly developed crafts like pottery and bead-making to support their daily lives. A wrong answer indicates the student is ignoring the "craft" and "supplementary" aspects of their economy.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
If you had to design a "Harappan Daily Life" poster, which three items would you include to show they were prosperous?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A student should choose items like a bag of wheat, a spindle for cotton, and a decorated pot. Poor answers that include modern items or items not mentioned (like iron tools) show a failure to apply historical context.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think Harappans were just "farmers" like the earlier Neolithic people. They fail to realize that the "urban" or "city" aspect of the Harappans was possible because they had moved beyond simple farming into organized, specialized work like pottery and textile production.
## ON_FAILURE
If the student persists in thinking they were just simple farmers, ask them to compare a small village (which only farms) with a city (which has markets, potters, and weavers). Ask: "If everyone was just farming, who would make the beautiful pots and cotton clothes?" Guide them to see that specialization is the key to an urban economy.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A farmer in the Harappan period wants to ensure their family has food even if the wheat harvest is poor. What sources of food do they have besides wheat?
STEPS:
- Identify the goal: diversify food sources.
- Recall Harappan food habits: barley, peas, sesame, cotton (non-food), cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, fish.
- Select items: Barley and peas are crops; cattle/sheep/goats are animals; fish is a protein source.
- Formulate the answer: The farmer can rely on barley and peas, milk/meat from their animals, and fishing.
REASONING: By showing multiple food sources, the student understands the "diverse economy" mentioned in the text.
ANSWER: They have barley and peas for grains, and they can get milk or meat from cattle, sheep, and goats, or fish from the rivers.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Why would a Harappan city need both a farmer and a potter to function?
STEPS:
- Identify the roles: Farmer produces raw food; Potter produces storage/cooking containers.
- Analyze the relationship: The farmer needs pots to store grain; the potter needs food to eat while they work.
- Connect to the economy: This is a system of dependency and trade.
- Conclude: Both are needed for an organized urban life.
REASONING: This demonstrates an understanding of economic interdependence.
ANSWER: The farmer provides food that the potter needs to live, and the potter provides the containers the farmer needs to store and cook that food, allowing the community to thrive together.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: How did the discovery of cotton change the Harappan economy compared to a basic farming village?
STEPS:
- Compare: Basic farming village (food only) vs. Harappan city (food + tradeable goods).
- Identify the product: Cotton.
- Analyze the use: Weaving cotton into fabric requires skill and time.
- Predict the impact: This creates a surplus or a specialized craft that can be exchanged.
REASONING: This connects the craft development to economic prosperity.
ANSWER: Cotton allowed the Harappans to produce fabric, which is a specialized tradeable good. Unlike a village that only grows food to survive, a city producing cotton has extra goods to exchange, making the economy more prosperous.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Imagine a Harappan city suddenly stops trading with all other villages. Which group of people—the farmers or the specialized craftspeople—would feel the change more, and why?
COMPETENCY: Economic Analysis
STEPS:
- Define the roles of each group.
- Analyze the dependence of craftspeople on farmers for food.
- Analyze the dependence of farmers on craftspeople for tools/storage.
- Conclude the impact of isolation.
ANSWER: The craftspeople would feel it more because they depend entirely on farmers for food, whereas farmers could technically survive on their own food, even if their life becomes less "prosperous" without new tools or pottery.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: If you found a site with only stone tools but no pottery or cotton, would you categorize it as a developed Harappan city? Why or why not?
COMPETENCY: Historical Evidence Evaluation
STEPS:
- List the hallmarks of a developed Harappan economy.
- Compare these with the evidence in the scenario.
- Determine if the criteria are met.
- Provide a justification.
ANSWER: No, because a developed Harappan city is defined by its specialized crafts like pottery and cotton weaving. Finding only stone tools suggests a much earlier or simpler community that had not yet reached the Harappan level of economic complexity.
DIFFICULTY: hard
