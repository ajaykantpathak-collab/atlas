## DEFINITION
A resource is any asset, attribute, or means—human or non-human—that an individual or group can utilise to achieve a specific goal; it must possess utility for the given situation, be accessible to the user, and be manageable through planning and allocation. Resources are not inherently valuable; they become resources only when recognised, accessed, and deployed purposefully toward an objective.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you're planning a surprise birthday party for your best friend with a budget of ₹2,000 and only three days to prepare. You have your creativity, your cousin's car, a community park nearby, and your mother's recipe knowledge—which of these are resources? Start by listing everything you might use, then ask: does it help me reach my goal (utility)? Can I actually get it (accessibility)? Could I swap it for something else (interchangeability)? Can I plan its use (manageability)? The key insight: a bicycle gathering dust in your garage is not your resource today, but becomes one the moment you decide to use it for deliveries to earn party money—resources are defined by intentional use toward a goal, not by mere existence.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Define 'resource' as per the NCERT Class 11 Home Science textbook, and state the two-word phrase that captures when something becomes a resource.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
A resource is anything which we make use of while carrying out any activity to accomplish our goals. The key phrase is "make use of"—mere possession or availability does not make something a resource; it must be actively employed toward a goal. A wrong answer (e.g., "resources are things we own") reveals the misconception that ownership or existence alone confers resource status, ignoring the intentional, goal-directed utilisation central to the definition.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Your grandmother's old sewing machine has been stored in the attic for ten years. Last week, you decided to learn tailoring and started using it to stitch clothes. Explain, using the textbook definition, at what point the sewing machine became a resource for you and why it was not a resource during the ten years in the attic.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The sewing machine became a resource the moment you decided to learn tailoring and actively used it for stitching—because only then were you "making use of" it to accomplish a goal. During the ten years in the attic, it was merely an object in your possession; it had potential utility but no actual utility since no goal-directed activity employed it. A wrong answer (e.g., "it was always a resource because it could be used") reveals confusion between potential utility and actual resource status, missing the textbook's emphasis on active utilisation.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Classify each of the following into human/non-human, individual/shared, and natural/community categories. Justify each classification in one sentence: (a) Your ability to speak fluent English, (b) The metro rail system in your city, (c) Rainwater collected in your building's harvesting tank, (d) Your friend's willingness to help you study.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
(a) Human, Individual, Community (skill developed personally but language is socially acquired); (b) Non-human, Shared, Community (infrastructure provided by government for public use); (c) Non-human, Shared, Natural (water is natural, but the harvesting system makes it a shared community asset); (d) Human, Shared, Community (friend's motivation is human, accessible to you as shared help, and exists within your social community). A wrong classification (e.g., calling the metro "individual") reveals failure to distinguish ownership/access scope—shared resources are available to multiple users by design.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Analyse the statement: "Time is the most democratic resource because everyone has exactly 24 hours a day." Using the four characteristics of resources (utility, accessibility, interchangeability, manageability), evaluate whether this statement is fully accurate or incomplete. Provide one specific counter-example for any characteristic where the claim fails.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The statement is incomplete. While time has equal accessibility in quantity (24 hours), its utility varies by individual (peak productivity hours differ), its interchangeability is limited (you cannot buy more time, only trade leisure for work), and its manageability depends on health, obligations, and circumstances—a single parent working two jobs has far less manageable discretionary time than a student with flexible schedule. Counter-example for manageability: a daily-wage labourer cannot "manage" time to study for an exam without losing income, unlike a salaried employee with paid leave. Wrong evaluation (calling it fully accurate) ignores how accessibility ≠ equity in resource characteristics.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate this claim: "Money is the most powerful resource because it can substitute for almost all other resources." Use the concept of interchangeability and at least two other characteristics to defend or refute the claim with a concrete family scenario.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The claim is overstated. Money has high interchangeability (can buy skills, save time, purchase energy-saving appliances), but it lacks intrinsic utility (cannot directly cook a meal or provide emotional motivation), its accessibility is highly unequal, and its manageability requires financial literacy—a family with ₹50,000 but no cooking skills or health cannot instantly convert money into a nutritious home-cooked meal without time, knowledge, and energy. A poor evaluation (agreeing fully) treats interchangeability as universal substitutability, ignoring that some human resources (e.g., parental affection, personal aptitude) are non-purchasable.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a new, original example of a resource that fits ALL three classification systems simultaneously (i.e., can be viewed as human/non-human, individual/shared, natural/community depending on context). Describe the context shifts that change its classification.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Example: A home garden with vegetables. As non-human/individual/natural: the tomatoes growing in your private backyard for family consumption. As human/shared/community: your gardening knowledge taught to neighbours in a community workshop. As human/individual/community: your physical energy spent maintaining the garden, which benefits the neighbourhood's green cover. Context shifts: ownership/use scope (individual vs. shared), source (nature vs. human skill), and beneficiary (self vs. community). A weak answer gives a static example (e.g., "my phone") that cannot plausibly shift across all three systems, showing inability to grasp contextual classification.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students believe classification categories are mutually exclusive and fixed—e.g., "money is only non-human" or "time is only individual." They fail to see that the SAME resource can shift categories based on user, goal, and context. For instance, a teacher's knowledge is human/individual when used for personal lesson planning, but becomes human/shared/community when the same teacher conducts a free weekend workshop for underprivileged students. The error stems from treating classification as an inherent property of the object rather than a relational property between resource, user, and goal.

## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Ask the student to pick one object (e.g., a laptop) and write three distinct scenarios where it functions as (a) individual resource, (b) shared resource, (c) community resource. Step 2: For each scenario, identify who the user is, what the goal is, and who else can access it. Step 3: Explicitly map each scenario to the three classification dimensions. Step 4: Have the student create their own "shape-shifting resource" example with three context shifts. This forces recognition that classification depends on the resource-user-goal triangle, not the object alone.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Rahul, a Class 11 student, wants to organise a blood donation camp in his residential colony. He has: his leadership skills, ₹5,000 saved from pocket money, the colony community hall (free for residents), his father's car, two friends willing to volunteer, and a contact at the government blood bank. Classify each resource into the three classification systems (human/non-human, individual/shared, natural/community) and justify ONE classification where the categorisation might be debated.

STEPS:
- Step 1: List all six resources explicitly: (1) leadership skills, (2) ₹5,000, (3) community hall, (4) father's car, (5) two friends' volunteer time, (6) blood bank contact.
- Step 2: Classify each on human/non-human dimension: (1) human, (2) non-human, (3) non-human, (4) non-human, (5) human, (6) human (contact represents institutional knowledge/relationship).
- Step 3: Classify each on individual/shared dimension: (1) individual (Rahul's own skill), (2) individual (his savings), (3) shared (available to all residents), (4) shared (father's car used with permission), (5) shared (friends' time offered to cause), (6) shared (institutional contact accessible to camp).
- Step 4: Classify each on natural/community dimension: (1) community (skill developed socially), (2) community (money is social instrument), (3) community (government-provided), (4) community (car is manufactured good), (5) community (volunteerism is social), (6) community (public health system).
- Step 5: Identify debatable classification: Father's car as "shared" vs "individual"—debatable because Rahul has permission but not ownership; if father revokes permission, it ceases to be his resource. This shows accessibility is conditional.
- Step 6: Synthesise: The blood bank contact is the most complex—human (relationship), shared (institutional), community (public system)—illustrating how one resource spans categories.

REASONING: This worked example forces multi-dimensional classification of each resource, mirroring board exam questions that ask "classify the following resources" with justification. The debatable classification step develops analytical skill for the "justify" command word in exams.

ANSWER: Leadership skills: Human, Individual, Community. ₹5,000: Non-human, Individual, Community. Community hall: Non-human, Shared, Community. Father's car: Non-human, Shared (debatable), Community. Friends' volunteer time: Human, Shared, Community. Blood bank contact: Human, Shared, Community. Debatable: Father's car as shared—access depends on father's permission, not right.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Meena's family faces a water shortage. They have: (a) a rainwater harvesting tank (capacity 2000L, currently 30% full), (b) Meena's knowledge of water-saving techniques, (c) ₹3,000 to buy tanker water, (d) a municipal water connection (2 hours supply daily), (e) Meena's brother's physical strength to carry buckets. Using the four characteristics of resources (utility, accessibility, interchangeability, manageability), rank these five resources from MOST to LEAST effective for meeting the family's immediate drinking/cooking water need (approx. 50L/day). Justify the top and bottom rankings with specific characteristic analysis.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Define the goal clearly: meet 50L/day drinking/cooking need during shortage. Immediate = next 7 days.
- Step 2: Assess each resource on utility for THIS goal: (a) tank water = high utility (directly potable if filtered), (b) knowledge = indirect utility (reduces need but doesn't supply water), (c) money = high utility (buys tanker water), (d) municipal supply = high utility (direct source), (e) brother's strength = medium utility (enables transport only).
- Step 3: Assess accessibility: (a) 600L available immediately (30% of 2000L), (b) always accessible (internal), (c) accessible but finite (₹3,000 buys ~15,000L at ₹200/1000L), (d) accessible only 2 hours/day (time-constrained), (e) accessible but limited by brother's time/energy.
- Step 4: Assess interchangeability: (a) low (tank water cannot become money easily), (b) medium (knowledge can be shared/applied elsewhere), (c) high (money substitutes for almost all), (d) low (municipal supply fixed schedule), (e) low (strength specific to physical tasks).
- Step 5: Assess manageability: (a) high (can ration 600L over 12 days), (b) high (can apply techniques anytime), (c) medium (budgeting required), (d) low (no control over supply timing), (e) medium (can schedule brother's effort).
- Step 6: Rank for IMMEDIATE drinking/cooking: 1. Municipal supply (high utility, daily renewal, manageable via storage) — TOP. 2. Tank water (high utility, immediately available, manageable). 3. Money + tanker (high utility, high interchangeability, but delivery delay). 4. Brother's strength (enables collection from municipal/tanker). 5. Knowledge (long-term utility, zero immediate supply) — BOTTOM.
- Step 7: Justify top: Municipal supply wins on utility + renewability + manageability (storage during 2-hour window). Justify bottom: Knowledge has zero interchangeability for immediate physical water—it cannot substitute for supply.

REASONING: This example requires evaluating resources on multiple characteristics simultaneously for a specific goal, reflecting board exam "analyse/evaluate" questions. The ranking forces trade-off reasoning: accessibility + utility + manageability > interchangeability for immediate needs.

ANSWER: Rank: 1. Municipal supply, 2. Rainwater tank, 3. Money/tanker, 4. Brother's strength, 5. Knowledge. Top: Municipal—daily renewable, storable, direct utility. Bottom: Knowledge—no immediate supply, zero interchangeability for physical water.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A school's Eco Club (15 students, ₹10,000 budget, 2 teacher mentors) plans a "Zero-Waste Week" campaign. They have access to: school auditorium, art room supplies, social media accounts, local NGO partnership, and municipal waste collection schedule. The campaign must achieve: (a) 80% student participation, (b) 50% reduction in cafeteria waste, (c) a permanent composting system. Map each resource to the campaign sub-goals using a resource-goal matrix. Then identify which TWO characteristics (utility, accessibility, interchangeability, manageability) are MOST critical for EACH sub-goal and explain why.

STEPS:
- Step 1: List resources with classifications: (1) 15 students (human, shared, community), (2) ₹10,000 (non-human, shared, community), (3) 2 teacher mentors (human, shared, community), (4) auditorium (non-human, shared, community), (5) art supplies (non-human, shared, community), (6) social media accounts (non-human, shared, community), (7) NGO partnership (human, shared, community), (8) municipal schedule (non-human, shared, community).
- Step 2: List sub-goals: G1 = 80% participation, G2 = 50% waste reduction, G3 = permanent composting.
- Step 3: Build matrix (resource × sub-goal) with HIGH/MEDIUM/LOW utility ratings: Students HIGH for all (labour, peer influence). Money HIGH for G2,G3 (bins, compost setup), MEDIUM for G1 (prizes). Teachers HIGH for G1,G3 (authority, guidance), MEDIUM for G2. Auditorium HIGH for G1 (assembly), LOW for G2,G3. Art supplies HIGH for G1 (posters), LOW for G2,G3. Social media HIGH for G1 (reach), LOW for G2,G3. NGO HIGH for G2,G3 (expertise), MEDIUM for G1. Municipal schedule HIGH for G3 (collection sync), LOW for G1,G2.
- Step 4: For G1 (participation): Critical characteristics = ACCESSIBILITY (social media + auditorium reach all students) and INTERCHANGEABILITY (if auditorium booked, social media substitutes; if NGO unavailable, teachers substitute).
- Step 5: For G2 (waste reduction): Critical = UTILITY (money buys bins, NGO gives expertise—direct waste impact) and MANAGEABILITY (daily monitoring of cafeteria waste by students/teachers).
- Step 6: For G3 (composting): Critical = UTILITY (municipal schedule ensures compost collection, money builds bins) and ACCESSIBILITY (ongoing access to space, municipal coordination).
- Step 7: Verify no resource is wasted: Art supplies only used for G1—could they serve G2? Yes, repurpose for bin labels (interchangeability). This optimisation is the "manageable" characteristic in action.

REASONING: This multi-goal, multi-resource mapping mirrors the board exam's "case study" format where students must allocate resources across objectives. Identifying critical characteristics per sub-goal tests deep understanding of how utility/accessibility/interchangeability/manageability operate differently for different targets.

ANSWER: Matrix shows students/teachers/NGO as high-utility across goals. G1 critical: Accessibility + Interchangeability. G2 critical: Utility + Manageability. G3 critical: Utility + Accessibility. Optimisation: Art supplies repurposed for bin labels (interchangeability in action).

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: The textbook states: "Resources can be managed. Since resources are limited, they should be managed properly and effectively for their optimum utilisation." Consider a family with: monthly income ₹40,000, two school-going children, one working parent, one homemaker parent, 2-hour daily commute, and a goal to save ₹10,000/month for a medical emergency fund. Identify FOUR distinct resources (mix of human and non-human) this family possesses. For EACH resource, specify: (a) its classification on all three dimensions, (b) its current utilisation level (low/medium/high), (c) one specific management action to increase its utilisation toward the savings goal, and (d) which resource characteristic (utility, accessibility, interchangeability, manageability) that action leverages. Present as a structured table.

COMPETENCY: Multi-dimensional analysis; integrating classification, characteristics, and management; justifying decisions with conceptual reasoning.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify four diverse resources (e.g., homemaker's cooking skill, working parent's commute time, ₹40,000 income, community park near home).
- Step 2: Classify each on three dimensions.
- Step 3: Assess current utilisation toward savings goal.
- Step 4: Design one concrete management action per resource.
- Step 5: Map action to the specific characteristic it exploits.
- Step 6: Verify all four resources cover both human/non-human and all three classification systems.

ANSWER: 
| Resource | Classification (Human/Non-human, Individual/Shared, Natural/Community) | Current Utilisation | Management Action | Characteristic Leveraged |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Homemaker's cooking skill | Human, Individual, Community | Medium (cooks daily but buys some processed food) | Replace ₹2,000/month packaged snacks with home-made equivalents | Utility (skill directly produces value) + Interchangeability (skill substitutes for money) |
| Working parent's 2-hr commute | Human (time), Individual, Community | Low (passive travel) | Use commute for paid online tutoring/transcription (₹3,000/month) | Manageability (time can be planned) + Accessibility (time available daily) |
