## DEFINITION
Non-metals are materials that generally lack metallic properties such as lustre, malleability, and the ability to conduct heat or electricity. They exist in various forms in nature and serve essential roles in life, such as oxygen for breathing and carbon in our food.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to classify a given material as a non-metal based on its physical properties (appearance, conductivity, and state) and distinguish it from metals through simple observation.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the definition and basic physical properties (lustre, conductivity, state) of non-metals. It does not cover atomic structure, chemical bonding, or complex chemical reactions.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever wondered why the handle of a pressure cooker is made of plastic while the body is metal? Start by looking at the objects in your room: a wooden pencil, a plastic eraser, and a metal spoon. Compare how they look—does the wood shine like the spoon? Try touching them to feel if they are hard or soft, and think about why we use plastic to cover copper wires. By identifying that non-metals like plastic and rubber are dull and poor conductors, you can see why they are used as insulators to keep us safe from electricity.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Name two physical properties that distinguish most non-metals from metals.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Non-metals are generally dull (lack lustre) and are poor conductors of heat and electricity. A student who says "non-metals are hard and shiny" is likely confusing them with metals, revealing they have not yet grasped the basic appearance difference.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why is plastic used to coat the metal wires in your house?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Plastic is a non-metal that acts as an insulator, meaning it does not allow electricity to pass through it, protecting us from shocks. A student who says "because it is pretty" misses the functional scientific property of conductivity.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If you have a piece of sulfur and a piece of iron, how can you test which one is a metal?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
First, observe if it is shiny (lustre); then, try to see if it conducts heat or electricity. Metals are shiny and conduct; sulfur is dull and does not conduct. This shows the student can apply the definition to a practical test.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Are all non-lustrous materials necessarily non-metals?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
No, some metals can appear dull due to oxidation or weathering, and some materials like wood are non-metals but are not shiny. A student who says "yes" assumes that appearance is the only factor, ignoring surface changes or material types.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Could you use a non-metal like rubber to make a cooking pot? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, because rubber is a poor conductor of heat and would melt or burn before cooking the food, unlike metal which transfers heat efficiently. If a student says "yes," they fail to understand the property of thermal conductivity required for kitchenware.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Think of three non-metals you interact with daily and describe how their property of being a non-metal makes them useful.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Examples: Oxygen (gas state, needed for breathing), Plastic (insulator, for electrical safety), and Cotton/Fabric (soft, for clothing). A poor answer fails to connect the property to the specific use.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that if a material is solid and hard, it must be a metal. They ignore non-metals like wood or diamond (though diamond is advanced, simple carbon-based materials fit the grade 6 level) that are hard but not metallic. They confuse "hardness" with "metallic nature," failing to check for lustre or conductivity.
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to compare a piece of coal (non-metal) and a coin (metal). Tell them to rub both on sandpaper and observe the surface; the coin will show a metallic shine, while the coal will remain dull. Explain that hardness is a separate property, and they must check for shine and conductivity to confirm if something is a metal.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: You have three mystery items: a copper wire, a graphite lead from a pencil, and a piece of dry wood. How do you classify them based on their properties?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Observe surface for shine (lustre). Copper shines; wood and graphite do not.
- Step 2: Test conductivity using a battery and bulb.
- Step 3: Copper wire completes the circuit (conductor).
- Step 4: Wood and graphite (in the context of basic school sets) fail to conduct or conduct poorly compared to metals.
- Step 5: Classify copper as metal, and wood and graphite as non-metals based on these observations.
REASONING: By testing both lustre and conductivity, we can definitively separate metals from non-metals, as these properties are consistent for the groups.
ANSWER: Copper is a metal; wood and graphite are non-metals.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Identify the properties of oxygen gas that make it different from iron.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Compare states at room temperature. Oxygen is a gas; iron is a solid.
- Step 2: Compare lustre. Iron has metallic lustre; oxygen is invisible/gaseous.
- Step 3: Compare conductivity. Iron is a good conductor; oxygen gas is a poor conductor.
- Step 4: Determine that since oxygen lacks metallic properties, it is a non-metal.
REASONING: Non-metals exist in all three states, while metals are typically solid, proving states alone don't define a metal.
ANSWER: Oxygen is a non-metal (gas, dull, poor conductor) while iron is a metal (solid, lustrous, good conductor).
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A student has a container of salt (solid), a piece of plastic, and a bar of gold. How would they organize these by solubility and conductivity?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Test solubility: Salt dissolves in water (soluble); plastic and gold do not.
- Step 2: Test conductivity: Gold conducts electricity; plastic and salt (as solid crystals) do not.
- Step 3: Identify materials: Gold is a metal; plastic is a non-metal; salt is a compound (non-metallic).
- Step 4: Organize: Gold (Metallic, Insoluble), Plastic (Non-metal, Insoluble), Salt (Non-metal/Compound, Soluble).
REASONING: Materials have diverse properties; grouping them requires checking multiple traits like solubility and conductivity together.
ANSWER: Gold (Metal, Insoluble), Plastic (Non-metal, Insoluble), Salt (Non-metal, Soluble).
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Why is it dangerous to use a metal rod to stir a hot pot of soup, but safe to use a wooden spoon? Explain using the property of conductivity.
COMPETENCY: Explaining scientific phenomena using physical properties.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify that metal is a good conductor of heat.
- Step 2: Identify that wood is a poor conductor of heat (insulator).
- Step 3: Explain that heat from the soup travels through the metal rod to the hand.
- Step 4: Conclude that the wood prevents heat transfer, keeping the hand safe.
ANSWER: Metal conducts heat rapidly from the soup to your hand, causing burns. Wood, being a non-metal insulator, prevents this heat transfer, making it safer.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: You are given a list: Silver, Nitrogen, Sulfur, Aluminum, Iodine. Create a table to group them as "Metal" or "Non-metal" and provide one reason for each group.
COMPETENCY: Classification and justification.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Recall definitions of metals (lustrous, conductors).
- Step 2: Group Silver and Aluminum as metals because they are shiny and conduct electricity.
- Step 3: Group Nitrogen, Sulfur, and Iodine as non-metals because they are generally dull and do not conduct electricity well.
- Step 4: Organize into a table format.
ANSWER: 
| Metal | Non-metal |
| :--- | :--- |
| Silver, Aluminum | Nitrogen, Sulfur, Iodine |
Reason: Metals have lustre and conduct electricity; non-metals are dull and are poor conductors.
DIFFICULTY: hard
