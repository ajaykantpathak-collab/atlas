## DEFINITION
Cyber safety refers to the practices and measures taken to protect computers, networks, and data from unauthorized access, attacks, or damage, while cyber threats are malicious activities or vulnerabilities that exploit weaknesses in digital systems to cause harm, steal information, or disrupt services.

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you receive an email that looks exactly like it's from your bank, asking you to "verify your account" by clicking a link and entering your password. You click it, and the next day your account is empty. This is a classic cyber threat — but could you have spotted it? Start by asking: "What makes a digital interaction unsafe?" Then, list all the ways a stranger could harm you through your computer: stealing data, locking your files, spying on your keystrokes, flooding a website until it crashes. Group these into categories: malicious software (malware), deceptive messages (spam/phishing), insecure communication (HTTP), unauthorized access (hackers), and network attacks (DoS, snooping). The key insight is that every threat exploits either a technical vulnerability (like unencrypted data) or a human vulnerability (like trust) — and cyber safety means defending both.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the primary difference between a computer virus and a computer worm in terms of how they spread?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
A virus requires a host program and human action (like opening an infected file) to replicate and spread, whereas a worm is a standalone program that can self-replicate and spread automatically across networks without user interaction.  
A wrong answer (e.g., "both need a host file" or "both spread only via email") reveals the student confuses the replication mechanism — a critical distinction because worms can cause faster, uncontrolled outbreaks (e.g., WannaCry) while viruses often rely on social engineering.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why HTTPS is considered secure for online banking while HTTP is not, using the terms "encryption" and "plain text" in your answer.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
HTTPS encrypts data before transmission, so even if intercepted, the information appears as unreadable ciphertext; HTTP sends data in plain text, allowing anyone monitoring the network to read passwords, card numbers, or personal details directly.  
A wrong answer (e.g., "HTTPS is just faster" or "HTTP uses a firewall") shows the student misunderstands the core security mechanism — encryption — and may incorrectly believe visual cues (like a padlock icon) alone guarantee safety without understanding the underlying protocol.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A student uses a free public Wi‑Fi at a café to log into their school email. They see "http://" in the address bar. Identify the specific cyber threat this exposes them to and name one tool that could mitigate it.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The threat is **snooping (or sniffing)** — an attacker on the same network can capture unencrypted HTTP traffic and read the login credentials in plain text. A tool to mitigate this is a **VPN (Virtual Private Network)**, which encrypts all traffic between the device and the VPN server, making snooping ineffective.  
A wrong answer (e.g., "phishing" or "antivirus") indicates the student cannot match the threat to the vulnerability (unencrypted channel) or selects a tool that doesn't address network-level interception.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare and contrast a DoS attack and a DDoS attack in terms of source, scale, and difficulty of mitigation. Which is more dangerous for a large e‑commerce site during a sale? Justify.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
A DoS attack originates from a single source, making it easier to block by filtering that IP; a DDoS attack uses a botnet of compromised devices (zombies) distributed globally, generating massive traffic from thousands of IPs, making filtering extremely difficult without blocking legitimate users. For a large e‑commerce site during a sale, **DDoS is far more dangerous** because the distributed nature mimics legitimate high traffic, overwhelming the server while evading simple IP-based defenses.  
A wrong answer (e.g., "DoS is worse because it's faster" or "both are same") shows the student misses the critical role of distribution in evading mitigation — a key concept in network security design.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
A company installs a firewall but still suffers a data breach because an employee clicked a malicious email attachment that installed a keylogger. Evaluate whether the firewall failed, and explain what additional layer of defense was missing.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The firewall did **not** necessarily fail — it is designed to filter *network traffic* based on rules, not to inspect user actions like opening email attachments. The missing layer is **endpoint protection** (antivirus/anti‑malware with real‑time scanning) and **user awareness training** to prevent social engineering. Firewalls are perimeter defenses; they cannot stop threats that originate from inside the trusted network via user interaction.  
A wrong answer (e.g., "firewall should have blocked the email" or "firewall is useless") reveals a misconception that firewalls inspect content of all user actions — they don't. This confusion leads to over‑reliance on a single control.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a 3‑step "Cyber Safety Checklist" for a Class 12 student who uses a shared computer in a school lab for online exam registration. Each step must address a different threat category covered in this node.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A strong checklist (example):
1. **Verify HTTPS & Padlock** before entering any credentials — prevents snooping/eavesdropping on unencrypted HTTP (network threat).
2. **Use the on‑screen virtual keyboard** (randomized layout) for password entry — defeats hardware/software keyloggers (malware threat).
3. **Log out completely, clear browser cache/cookies, and avoid saving passwords** — prevents session hijacking and data leakage via cookies (privacy/threat from shared device).  
A poor answer (e.g., "install antivirus, use strong password, don't click links") mixes generic advice without mapping each step to a distinct threat category from this node (malware, network, privacy), showing the student cannot categorize threats or apply layered defense.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that **having an antivirus installed makes them completely safe from all cyber threats**. They write in exams: "Antivirus protects against hackers, phishing, and data theft." This is wrong because antivirus primarily detects *known malware signatures* and some behavioral anomalies — it cannot prevent phishing (which relies on deception), stop a DDoS attack (network flood), protect data sent over HTTP (no encryption), or block a keylogger installed via a malicious USB if real-time scanning is off. The misconception arises from marketing language ("total protection") and not understanding that cyber safety requires *layered defenses*: firewall + HTTPS + user caution + updates + backups, not just one tool.

## ON_FAILURE
If a student holds this misconception, do the following:  
1. Show a 2-minute demo: send a harmless phishing email (simulated) to a test account with antivirus running — the email arrives because it's not malware.  
2. Ask: "Did the antivirus stop it? Why not?" Guide them to realize antivirus scans *files/programs*, not *intent* or *network traffic*.  
3. Have them complete a table: Threat Type | Stopped by Antivirus? | What Actually Stops It? (e.g., Phishing → No → User awareness + spam filter; DDoS → No → Firewall + rate limiting; HTTP snooping → No → HTTPS).  
4. Re‑ask Diagnostic Question 5 — they should now identify the missing layers.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Rahul receives an SMS: "Your bank account will be closed in 2 hours. Click here to verify: http://bank-verify-login.xyz". He clicks the link on his phone (using mobile data), enters his username and password, but the page shows an error. Two hours later, ₹45,000 is withdrawn. Analyze the attack step by step.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the threat type — the SMS is a **smishing** (SMS phishing) attack, a form of social engineering that tricks the user into revealing credentials.
- Step 2: Identify the protocol vulnerability — the URL uses **HTTP** (not HTTPS), so credentials were sent in **plain text** over the mobile network, allowing interception via snooping.
- Step 3: Identify the malware risk — the fake site may have also silently downloaded a **keylogger** or **spyware** onto the phone, capturing future keystrokes.
- Step 4: Determine the failure points — Rahul did not verify the domain (bank-verify-login.xyz vs official bank domain), ignored the HTTP warning, and entered credentials on an untrusted link.
- Step 5: Recommend preventive measures — (a) Never click unsolicited links; (b) Always check for HTTPS and correct domain; (c) Use bank's official app; (d) Enable 2FA; (e) Install mobile antivirus with anti-phishing.

REASONING: This example combines three threat categories from the node: social engineering (phishing), insecure communication (HTTP), and potential malware (spyware/keylogger). It shows how a single incident often involves multiple layered threats, and defense requires addressing each layer.

ANSWER: The attack was a smishing (SMS phishing) campaign using an HTTP fake site to steal credentials in plain text, possibly compounded by spyware. Prevention requires user vigilance, HTTPS enforcement, and endpoint protection.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A school's computer lab has 30 PCs connected via LAN. The admin configures a **network firewall** to block all incoming traffic except to the web server (port 80, 443). Students complain they cannot access the internet. The admin checks and finds the firewall rule: "Allow OUTBOUND on port 80, 443 only." Why does this break browsing, and how to fix it without compromising security?

STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand firewall direction — the rule allows *outbound* traffic *from* the LAN *to* the internet on ports 80/443, but **blocks return traffic** (inbound responses) because no rule permits incoming packets on those ports.
- Step 2: Recall stateful inspection — modern firewalls track connection state; if the rule is stateless, it must explicitly allow *inbound* traffic for *established* connections (responses to student requests).
- Step 3: Identify the fix — add a rule: "Allow INBOUND traffic on ports 80, 443 **only for established/related connections**" (stateful) OR use a stateful firewall that automatically permits return traffic for allowed outbound sessions.
- Step 4: Verify security — this still blocks unsolicited incoming connections (e.g., from hackers scanning the LAN) while allowing legitimate browsing.
- Step 5: Test — students can now browse; admin verifies logs show no unauthorized inbound connections.

REASONING: Firewalls filter based on direction, port, and state. A common error is writing one-way rules that break return traffic. The fix uses stateful inspection — a core concept in network security — to allow legitimate sessions without opening permanent inbound holes.

ANSWER: The stateless outbound-only rule blocked return HTTP/HTTPS responses. Fix: Use a stateful firewall or add a rule allowing inbound traffic for established connections on ports 80/443.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Priya uses a **shared library computer** to apply for a scholarship. She fills a form with her Aadhaar number, bank details, and phone number. The browser offers to "Save password" and "Save address". She clicks "Yes" for convenience. After submitting, she closes the tab but does not log out or clear data. The next user, a stranger, opens the browser and sees her data auto-filled. Identify every security failure and map each to a threat category from this node.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Failure 1 — **Saving sensitive data (Aadhaar, bank) in browser autofill/cookies** on a shared device. Threat category: **Privacy / Data leakage via cookies** (Node 12.7). Cookies and form data persist across sessions.
- Step 2: Failure 2 — **Not logging out** of the scholarship portal. Threat category: **Session hijacking / Unauthorized access** (related to Network Intrusion, Node 12.9.2). The session cookie remains valid.
- Step 3: Failure 3 — **Not clearing browser cache, cookies, history** after use. Threat category: **Snooping / Data remanence** (Node 12.9.3). The next user can recover form data from cache or cookies.
- Step 4: Failure 4 — **Using a shared device for high-value transactions without a privacy mode**. Threat category: **Malware / Keylogger risk** (Node 12.2.7). The computer might have a hardware/software keylogger.
- Step 5: Correct procedure — (a) Use **incognito/private mode**; (b) **Never save passwords/form data** on shared devices; (c) **Log out explicitly**; (d) **Clear all browsing data** (Ctrl+Shift+Del) after session; (e) Use **virtual keyboard** for sensitive fields.
- Step 6: Explain how each step maps to a defense: Private mode → no cookies/history saved; No autofill → no credential storage; Logout → invalidates session token; Clear data → removes residual cache; Virtual keyboard → defeats keyloggers.

REASONING: This scenario integrates cookies, session management, shared-device risks, and keyloggers — all covered in this node. It demonstrates that cyber safety is a *process*, not a single setting, and that human behavior (convenience vs. security) is often the weakest link.

ANSWER: Four failures: (1) Saved sensitive data in browser → cookie/privacy threat; (2) No logout → session hijacking; (3) No data clearing → snooping/data remanence; (4) No virtual keyboard → keylogger risk. Fix: Private mode + no autofill + explicit logout + clear data + virtual keyboard.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Your friend says: "I don't need a firewall because I have antivirus — it stops all viruses and hackers." Using concepts from this node, write a 4-point rebuttal explaining why this statement is dangerously wrong. Each point must name a specific threat that antivirus alone cannot stop and the defense that *does* stop it.

COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to security concepts / Comparing and justifying defenses

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the claim — "antivirus alone is sufficient".
- Step 2: Recall threat categories from this node that antivirus does not cover: network floods, unencrypted traffic, social engineering, unauthorized network access.
- Step 3: For each, name the threat, why antivirus fails, and the correct defense.
- Step 4: Structure as four clear, distinct points.

ANSWER:
1. **DDoS Attack** — Antivirus scans files, not network traffic volume. A firewall with rate limiting / DDoS mitigation service stops it.
2. **HTTP Snooping** — Antivirus doesn't encrypt data in transit. HTTPS (TLS/SSL) encrypts communication so intercepted packets are unreadable.
3. **Phishing / Smishing** — Antivirus may not block a deceptive email/SMS with a malicious link (no malware attached). User awareness + spam filters + link verification stop it.
4. **Unauthorized Network Access (e.g., worm spreading via open port)** — Antivirus reacts after infection; a **network firewall** blocks the malicious traffic *before* it reaches the machine.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A small business uses a single Wi‑Fi network for both **customer guest access** and **POS (point‑of‑sale) terminals** processing card payments. They have a firewall at the internet gateway but no internal segmentation. A hacker connects to the guest Wi‑Fi, scans the LAN, finds an unpatched POS terminal, and installs ransomware.  
(a) Identify the **two fundamental network security violations** in this setup.  
(b) Propose a **segmented network architecture** with firewall rules that would prevent this attack, using terms: VLAN, DMZ, least privilege, stateful inspection.  
(c) Explain why **PCI DSS** (Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard) would consider this non‑compliant.

COMPETENCY: Designing secure systems / Applying layered defense principles

STEPS:
- Step 1: Analyze the breach — guest network and POS on same LAN = no segmentation; firewall only at perimeter = no internal protection.
- Step 2: Identify violations: (1) Lack of network segmentation (guest vs. payment systems); (2) No internal firewall / least privilege between zones.
- Step 3: Design fix: Create VLANs — VLAN 10 (Guest Wi‑Fi), VLAN 20 (POS), VLAN 30 (Admin). Place a **stateful inspection firewall** between VLANs. Rules: Guest → Internet only (no access to VLAN 20/30). POS → Payment gateway only (no internet, no guest). Admin → POS (management ports only).
- Step 4: Link to PCI DSS — Requirement 1: Firewall configuration; Requirement 2: Change defaults; Requirement 4: Encrypt transmission; Requirement 10: Track access. Flat network violates all.

ANSWER:
(a) Violations: (1) No network segmentation — guest and payment systems on same broadcast domain. (2) Perimeter-only firewall — no internal enforcement of least privilege.
(b) Architecture: Three VLANs (Guest, POS, Admin) separated by a stateful firewall. Rules: Guest → Internet only. POS → Payment processor only (port 443). Admin → POS (SSH/RDP) only. All other inter‑VLAN traffic denied by default.
(c) PCI DSS requires: (1) Firewall between cardholder data and other networks (Req 1.2); (2) No direct access from untrusted networks (Req 1.3); (3) Encrypted transmission (Req 4); (4) Access logging (Req 10). The flat network fails all.
