## DEFINITION
An argumentative essay has a five-paragraph spine: introduction with a thesis (the claim) → three body paragraphs each = topic sentence + evidence + link-back → conclusion that answers 'so what'. Every paragraph earns its place by advancing the thesis; decoration is not argument.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student drafts a complete spine — a one-line thesis, three topic sentences that each argue a distinct reason, and a conclusion that answers so-what — and can diagnose a body paragraph that fails to advance the thesis.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers argumentative/persuasive structure. Does NOT cover narrative/descriptive essays (93_002).

## WALKTHROUGH
Build it like a legal case: thesis = the charge; each body paragraph = one witness; conclusion = the closing argument. Test any paragraph with the delete test — remove it; if the argument survives unchanged, the paragraph was decoration. 'Discipline is important' is a topic; 'Discipline converts talent into results' is a thesis — it can be argued against, which is the point.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students write introductions that narrate ('Since ancient times…'), body paragraphs that list facts without arguing, and conclusions that merely repeat the title — the 'essay-shaped' answer that earns half marks.

## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Write the thesis — a claim someone could disagree with. Step 2: Plan three distinct reasons (not three restatements). Step 3: Each paragraph: topic sentence → evidence/example → 'this shows that…' link. Step 4: Conclusion answers 'so what — why should anyone care?'

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Which is a thesis: 'Sports are played in school' or 'Sports periods should be mandatory because they teach failure-recovery that classrooms cannot'?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The second — it claims something contestable with a reason attached; the first is an observation no one can argue with. A wrong answer reveals the student can't tell a claim from a fact — the essay's foundation stone.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
A body paragraph reads: 'Many students play sports. Sports include cricket and football. Cricket is very popular in India.' — what's wrong?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
It never advances the thesis — it's a list, not an argument. Missing: a reason (why sports matter for the claim) and a link-back. A wrong answer calling it 'a good paragraph' reveals the student grades by content-presence, not by argumentative function.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Write the topic sentence for reason 2 of 'Social media should be limited for under-16s' (reason: attention damage).

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Something like 'Beyond safety, social media's design deliberately fractures adolescent attention.' A wrong answer writing 'Secondly, attention is also affected' shows the student knows the slot exists but not that the topic sentence must STATE the reason, not gesture at it.
