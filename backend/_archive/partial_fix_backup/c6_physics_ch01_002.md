## DEFINITION
The scientific method is a structured, step-by-step process used to explore questions about the natural world by making observations, forming logical guesses, and testing those guesses through experiments. It transforms simple curiosity into reliable knowledge by verifying if our explanations match what we see.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify and order the five steps of the scientific method (Observation, Question, Hypothesis, Test/Experiment, Analysis) and apply them to a simple, non-scientific daily problem.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the fundamental steps of the scientific method as an investigative tool. It does not include advanced statistical analysis, laboratory report formatting, or the philosophy of science.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine your kitchen light doesn't turn on when you flip the switch. You notice the darkness (Observation) and wonder, "Why is the light out?" (Question). You guess that maybe the bulb is fused (Hypothesis), so you check the bulb or try a new one (Test/Experiment). If the light turns on, your guess was right; if not, you look for another reason like a power cut (Analysis). This cycle shows that science is just a way of thinking to solve mysteries, not just a subject for the classroom.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the very first step in the scientific method?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The first step is observation. If a student says "forming a hypothesis," they confuse the *start* of the process with the *creative* part; they are missing that science must begin by noticing something in the real world.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you test your hypothesis and find it is wrong, have you failed at doing science?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
No, you have not failed; you have gained knowledge by ruling out one possibility, which helps you form a better hypothesis. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks science is about "being right" instead of "finding the truth," which is a core misunderstanding of scientific inquiry.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
You notice your plant in the balcony is wilting. How would you apply the scientific method to find out why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
First, observe the plant (it's dry). Second, ask why (does it need water?). Third, guess the cause (the soil is too dry). Fourth, test it (add a litre of water). Fifth, analyze (did it perk up?). A wrong answer here shows the student cannot connect the abstract steps to a concrete action.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Why is it important to test a hypothesis instead of just believing our first guess?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Testing ensures that our explanation is based on evidence rather than just a hunch or bias. If the student answers that "testing is just a school rule," they fail to see that testing is the filter that separates mere opinions from facts.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
If two people look at the same cloud and guess differently about what shape it is, is one of them "doing science" better than the other?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Science isn't about the guess itself, but how they test it; if one person looks for more clouds to see if the shape repeats, they are applying the method better. A wrong answer reveals the student thinks science is a collection of static opinions rather than a process of verification.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Think of a problem you face while getting ready for school. Create a mini-experiment to solve it using the scientific method steps.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good answer will clearly list: Observation (e.g., bag is heavy), Question (Why?), Hypothesis (too many books?), Experiment (remove one book), and Analysis (is it lighter now?). A poor answer describes a random action without a clear hypothesis or testing phase.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think the scientific method is a rigid, linear path that *must* always lead to a "correct" discovery. In reality, it is a loop; if a test fails, you return to the hypothesis step and try again. Students mistakenly believe that if the result doesn't match their guess, the experiment was a failure.
## ON_FAILURE
If a student is stuck, tell them: "Think of a detective in a mystery movie." First, they observe the clues; then they form a theory (hypothesis) about who committed the crime. If they find evidence that proves the suspect was elsewhere, they don't give up—they change their theory. Ask the student to re-evaluate their hypothesis based on the new evidence rather than abandoning the investigation.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A student’s 2-litre water bottle is leaking in their bag. How do they find the leak?
STEPS:
- 1: Observe the wet spot on the bag and empty the bottle.
- 2: Ask: "Is the hole at the bottom or the side?"
- 3: Hypothesis: "The hole is at the bottom."
- 4: Experiment: Fill the bottle with 500ml of water and observe where it drips.
- 5: Analyze: If it drips from the base, the hypothesis is confirmed; if not, check the cap.
REASONING: By isolating the variable (the location of the leak), we can systematically eliminate causes.
ANSWER: The leak is located by observing where the water exits during the controlled test.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A student finds that their iron ruler feels very cold in winter. Why?
STEPS:
- 1: Observe the metal surface temperature.
- 2: Question: "Does the metal cool down more than the wood desk?"
- 3: Hypothesis: "Metal conducts heat away from my hand faster than wood."
- 4: Experiment: Touch the metal ruler and a wooden pencil for 10 seconds.
- 5: Analyze: Compare the sensation of coldness to verify if the material affects heat transfer.
REASONING: This uses a comparative test to move from an observation (feeling cold) to a scientific explanation.
ANSWER: The metal is a better conductor, confirming the hypothesis.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A bicycle tyre keeps going flat. How to fix it?
STEPS:
- 1: Observe: Tyre is soft.
- 2: Question: Is there a hole or a faulty valve?
- 3: Hypothesis: "There is a hole in the tube."
- 4: Experiment: Submerge the tube in a basin of water and look for bubbles.
- 5: Analysis: If bubbles appear, the hypothesis is correct; if no bubbles, check the valve.
- 6: Conclusion: Apply a patch if bubbles are found or replace the valve.
REASONING: This combines observational evidence (bubbles) with a systematic testing method to reach a conclusion.
ANSWER: The source of the leak is found through the bubbling evidence.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You see a friend's plant growing faster than yours. How do you design a simple investigation to see if it is the sunlight or the water?
COMPETENCY: Designing a controlled experiment.
STEPS:
- 1: Identify variables: Sunlight and water.
- 2: Keep one variable the same (e.g., same amount of water).
- 3: Change one variable (e.g., move your plant to the same sunlight location as the friend's).
- 4: Observe growth over a week to see if the rate changes.
ANSWER: By changing only one factor (sunlight) while keeping others constant, you can conclude if that factor caused the difference.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Why is it important to tell other scientists about your experiment results, even if the experiment didn't work as you expected?
COMPETENCY: Scientific communication and integrity.
STEPS:
- 1: Understand that science is a team effort.
- 2: Recognize that "failed" experiments save time for others.
- 3: Explain that sharing results helps others avoid the same mistakes.
ANSWER: Sharing results prevents others from repeating the same incorrect steps, allowing the scientific community to refine their approach together.
DIFFICULTY: hard
