## DEFINITION
Prime factorisation is the process of breaking down a composite number into a product of its prime number building blocks. The Highest Common Factor (HCF) is the largest number that perfectly divides two or more given numbers without leaving a remainder.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to decompose any number up to 100 into its prime factors and identify the HCF of two numbers by comparing their prime factors.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers prime factorisation and HCF for integers up to 100. It excludes the Euclidean algorithm for HCF and the relationship formula between HCF and LCM.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you have two ribbons, one 12 cm long and the other 18 cm long, and you want to cut them into smaller, equal-sized pieces without any waste. To find the largest possible piece size, start by finding the "DNA" of these numbers—their prime factors. For 12, divide by the smallest prime (2) to get 6, then 2 again to get 3, so 12 = 2 × 2 × 3. For 18, divide by 2 to get 9, then 3 to get 3, so 18 = 2 × 3 × 3. By circling the prime factors they share (one 2 and one 3), you multiply them together to get 6, the HCF. The key insight is that the HCF is simply the product of the common building blocks.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the prime factorisation of 20?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
20 = 2 × 2 × 5 or 2² × 5. A student who answers "4 × 5" mistakes a composite number (4) for a prime factor, revealing they haven't fully decomposed the number into its prime "building blocks."
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If the prime factors of 8 are (2 × 2 × 2) and the prime factors of 12 are (2 × 2 × 3), why is the HCF 4 and not 24?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The HCF is the largest *divisor*, not a multiple. A student who says 24 is confusing HCF with LCM; they need to remember that a factor must be smaller than or equal to the numbers being compared.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Find the HCF of 16 and 24 using prime factorisation.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
16 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2; 24 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3. The common factors are 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. If a student gets 2 or 4, they have missed some common prime factors during the matching process.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Why can't the HCF of 15 and 20 be 10?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
10 does not divide 15 perfectly (15 ÷ 10 = 1.5). A student answering 10 likely checked only one number (20) instead of both numbers, highlighting a failure to verify the "common" requirement of the HCF.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is 1 always a common factor of any two numbers? Does this mean 1 is always the HCF?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, 1 is always a common factor, but it is rarely the *Highest* Common Factor. A student who thinks 1 is always the HCF fails to look for larger shared prime building blocks.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a pair of numbers whose HCF is 5. Explain how you found them.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Examples include 10 and 15, or 5 and 20. A good answer identifies that both numbers must have 5 as a prime factor. A poor answer (like 5 and 6) shows a guess-and-check approach without understanding the prime factor requirement.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often stop factorising too early, such as writing 12 = 3 × 4 and claiming they are done. They forget that 4 is a composite number that can be split further into 2 × 2. This happens because they view factorisation as just "any multiplication" rather than specifically "prime multiplication."
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to circle all the prime numbers in their list. If they see a number that is not circled (like 4 or 6), ask them: "Can this number be broken into even smaller pieces?" Continue splitting until only circled prime numbers remain. Use the example of 12: "If 4 is 2 × 2, then replacing 4 in your original list gives you 3 × 2 × 2, which are all primes."

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A tailor has two pieces of fabric, one 24 metres and the other 36 metres long. He wants to cut them into equal strips of the same maximum length without any fabric leftover. How long should each strip be?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Prime factorise 24: 24 = 2 × 12 = 2 × 2 × 6 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 3.
- Step 2: Prime factorise 36: 36 = 2 × 18 = 2 × 2 × 9 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3.
- Step 3: Identify common prime factors: Both share two 2s and one 3.
- Step 4: Multiply common factors: 2 × 2 × 3 = 12.
- Step 5: The maximum strip length is 12 metres.
REASONING: By breaking the lengths into prime building blocks, we find the largest shared "package" of factors, ensuring the strip size divides both total lengths perfectly.
ANSWER: 12 metres.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Two groups of students are in a room: 20 in the science club and 30 in the math club. We need to divide them into groups of equal size, with the same number of students in each group, and no students left over. What is the largest possible group size?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Prime factorise 20: 20 = 2 × 2 × 5.
- Step 2: Prime factorise 30: 30 = 2 × 3 × 5.
- Step 3: Identify common prime factors: Both share one 2 and one 5.
- Step 4: Multiply common factors: 2 × 5 = 10.
- Step 5: The largest group size is 10 students.
REASONING: The group size must be a factor of both 20 and 30. Finding the common prime factors and multiplying them gives the highest common factor.
ANSWER: 10 students.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A merchant has 18 kg of rice and 45 kg of sugar. He wants to pack them into bags of equal weight such that each bag is as heavy as possible and no items are mixed. What is the weight of one bag?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Factorise 18: 18 = 2 × 3 × 3.
- Step 2: Factorise 45: 45 = 3 × 3 × 5.
- Step 3: Identify common factors: Both share two 3s.
- Step 4: Multiply common factors: 3 × 3 = 9.
- Step 5: Check: 18 ÷ 9 = 2 bags, 45 ÷ 9 = 5 bags.
- Step 6: Verify: 9 is the largest number dividing both.
REASONING: The weight of the bag must divide both total amounts. Finding the HCF ensures the bags are the heaviest possible while remaining equal.
ANSWER: 9 kg.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Can the HCF of two numbers be one of the numbers themselves? Explain using 12 and 24.
COMPETENCY: Explaining relationships between numbers.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Factorise 12: 2 × 2 × 3.
- Step 2: Factorise 24: 2 × 2 × 2 × 3.
- Step 3: Identify common factors: 2 × 2 × 3 = 12.
- Step 4: Conclude: Since 12 is a factor of 24, the HCF is 12.
ANSWER: Yes, if the smaller number divides the larger number perfectly, the smaller number is the HCF.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: If you find the HCF of 12 and 18 is 6, what does this tell you about the possible sizes of equal pieces you could cut from a 12m and 18m rope?
COMPETENCY: Applying mathematical results to real-world context.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Understand HCF is the largest possible size (6m).
- Step 2: Consider other common factors.
- Step 3: Identify that any factor of the HCF (like 1, 2, 3) is also a possible piece size.
- Step 4: Conclude that 6m is the maximum, but 1m, 2m, or 3m are also valid.
ANSWER: It tells us 6m is the largest possible piece size, but we could also cut pieces of 1m, 2m, or 3m, as these are all factors of 6.
DIFFICULTY: hard
