## DEFINITION
Fundamental rights and duties are a pair of responsibilities and benefits that exist together, where every freedom you enjoy is balanced by a specific task you must perform to keep the community healthy. They are like two sides of one coin; you cannot have the benefit without taking on the responsibility.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify the corresponding duty for at least three different rights and explain how the neglect of that duty harms the community.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the interdependent relationship between rights and duties in a social context. It excludes legal enforceability, political philosophy, and civic republicanism.
## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you are at a school playground. You have the right to play with the swings, but does that mean you can stay on them forever while others wait? To keep the "right" of playtime fun for everyone, you have the "duty" to take turns and not hog the equipment. We start by listing things we enjoy (rights) and then ask, "What must I do to make sure everyone else can also enjoy this?" By connecting our personal benefits to our actions toward others, we see that citizenship is a shared activity, not just a list of rules.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What does it mean to say rights and duties are "two sides of the same coin"?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
It means they are inseparable parts of a whole; you get a benefit (right) only when you accept a responsibility (duty). A wrong answer suggests the student thinks rights are free gifts or duties are punishments, missing the idea of balance.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you have the right to a clean park, what is your corresponding duty?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Your duty is to not litter and to pick up your own trash. A wrong answer (like "telling the government to clean it") shows the student ignores their own individual role in maintaining the right.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If your friend is exercising their right to speak, what duty do you have to ensure the balance of rights?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Steps: 1. Identify the right (freedom of speech). 2. Identify the duty (active listening and respectful response). 3. Apply: I must listen without interrupting so they can finish their thought. 4. Verify: Does this allow both of us to communicate? A wrong answer shows an inability to link personal behavior to the rights of others.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Why does a right to education come with a duty to study?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Education requires resources provided by others; if you do not study, those resources are wasted, preventing others from having the same opportunity. A wrong answer suggests education is purely for personal gain rather than a social responsibility.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it fair to claim a right without performing the duty that supports it? Why?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, because rights are maintained by the actions of all citizens; if everyone claimed rights but ignored duties, the community would fall apart. A wrong answer indicates a failure to understand social interdependence.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a scenario where a duty is ignored and explain how a specific right is lost for others.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
If everyone ignores the duty to follow traffic rules (a duty for safety), the right to move freely on the roads safely disappears for everyone. A poor answer fails to connect the specific duty to the specific impact on the community.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think "Rights" are what I get and "Duties" are what I am forced to do by the government. They miss that duties are actually the *conditions* that make our rights possible in a shared space. For example, they think the right to a clean street is the government's job, ignoring that if everyone litters, no amount of government cleaning will keep it clean.
## ON_FAILURE
1. Ask the student: "If you take a toy from a friend, what happens if they take one from you?" 2. Explain that the 'right' to play with toys depends on the 'duty' to share. 3. Ask them to apply this to a clean classroom: "If you want a clean desk, what is your duty?" 4. Emphasize that duties are not 'bossy rules' but 'maintenance steps' for our shared life.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A student wants the right to a quiet study hall, but their classmate is talking loudly. How do you resolve this using the concept of rights and duties?
STEPS:
- Identify the right: Every student has the right to a quiet environment to study.
- Identify the duty: Every student has the duty to keep their voice low.
- Analyze the conflict: The loud student is claiming their right to talk but ignoring their duty to respect others' right to study.
- Propose a resolution: Remind the classmate that their right to speak exists, but their duty to the group requires them to speak softly in a quiet zone.
REASONING: By framing the request as a duty rather than a complaint, we appeal to the balance required for the community to function.
ANSWER: The classmate must lower their voice to fulfill their duty of maintaining a study-friendly environment for everyone.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: The village water tank is shared by all. Some people leave the tap open, wasting water. How does this link to rights and duties?
STEPS:
- Identify the shared right: Everyone has the right to access water.
- Identify the duty: Everyone has a duty to conserve water so it doesn't run out.
- Analyze the behavior: Leaving the tap open violates the duty of conservation.
- Determine the consequence: If the duty is ignored, the right to water is threatened for everyone.
REASONING: Resources are limited, so personal use must be balanced by collective care.
ANSWER: People must stop leaving the tap open because their duty to conserve water is necessary to protect the right of everyone in the village to have enough water.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: You notice your school garden is messy, but you want a beautiful place to sit. How can you apply the concept of rights and duties to fix this?
STEPS:
- Identify the right: The right to enjoy a beautiful, clean green space.
- Identify the duty: The duty to not litter and to help maintain the plants.
- Formulate a plan: Organize a small group to clean the garden for one hour a week.
- Execute: Show that by performing the duty (cleaning), the group secures the right (beautiful garden) for the whole school.
- Reflect: How did the duty contribute to the enjoyment of the space?
REASONING: Rights are not just passive; they require active participation to be maintained.
ANSWER: By taking the duty to clean and maintain the garden, the students ensure that the right to a beautiful environment is available for everyone to enjoy.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If a person says, "I have the right to freedom of expression, so I can say whatever I want about anyone," how would you use the concept of rights and duties to correct them?
COMPETENCY: Critical Thinking/Social Reasoning
STEPS:
- Clarify the right: Freedom of speech is a right.
- Identify the restriction/duty: The duty is to speak truthfully and not spread hatred.
- Explain the logic: Rights end where they harm others' rights.
- Conclusion: Freedom of expression does not mean freedom to harm or defame others.
ANSWER: You would explain that while they have the right to express themselves, they have a corresponding duty to ensure their words do not spread hatred or harm others, as everyone else has a right to be treated with respect.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Why is it that in a community, if you only focus on your rights and ignore your duties, the community fails? Provide an example.
COMPETENCY: Analytical Reasoning
STEPS:
- Identify the mechanism: Rights are supported by the duties of others.
- Contrast: If only rights are sought, duties (the foundation) are neglected.
- Example selection: Use the example of shared public space (a park or a road).
- Consequence: Chaos, destruction of resources, and ultimate loss of the right itself.
ANSWER: A community functions like a team; if everyone only takes and never contributes (duties), the system runs out of resources or becomes dysfunctional. For example, if everyone in a village uses the public road but no one follows traffic rules or avoids damaging the road, eventually the road becomes unusable, meaning no one can exercise their right to travel anymore.
