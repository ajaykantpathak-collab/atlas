## DEFINITION
"Before You Read: Context and Background" provides the essential historical, social, biographical, and literary framework needed to comprehend Krishna Sobti's autobiographical narrative "व्यक्ति" (Vyakti), enabling students to situate the text within post-Independence Hindi literature's exploration of gender, labor, and identity.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
Student can independently explain how Krishna Sobti's life experiences and the socio-political landscape of 1950s-70s urban India shape the narrative's central concerns, and can predict three major thematic conflicts the protagonist will face based on the contextual background.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers: author biography (Krishna Sobti), historical context (post-Partition urban migration, domestic labor economy), literary significance (women's writing in Hindi, autobiographical mode), and key conceptual vocabulary (अस्मिता, श्रम की गरिमा, पितृसत्ता). Does NOT cover: plot summary, character analysis, or textual interpretation (those belong to subsequent nodes c11_hin_v_cbse_03_002 onwards).

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you're meeting a new classmate who speaks a different dialect, wears clothes unfamiliar to you, and mentions working three jobs before school—you'd want to know their story before judging their choices. Similarly, before reading "व्यक्ति," we must understand the world Krishna Sobti came from: a Partition-displaced Punjabi woman navigating Delhi's middle-class households in the 1950s, writing when few women published autobiographical fiction. Start by researching Sobti's biography—note her Jnanpith Award, her refusal of the Padma Bhushan, her association with the Progressive Writers' Movement. Then map the historical context: post-1947 Delhi's refugee colonies, the invisible economy of domestic workers (mostly migrant women), and the literary breakthrough of women writing in first person about labor. The key insight: this text isn't just a story—it's a deliberate act of claiming voice in a literary tradition that erased working-class women's experiences.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Krishna Sobti's "व्यक्ति" is considered a landmark text in Hindi literature primarily because it:

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Correct answer: It centers a domestic worker's first-person narrative, challenging the middle-class literary establishment's exclusion of laboring women's voices. A wrong answer (e.g., "it uses beautiful language" or "it describes Partition") reveals the student recognizes Sobti's stature but misses the specific political intervention of this text—making the invisible visible by letting a 'kaamwali bai' narrate her own life without mediation.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain why the post-Partition refugee experience in Delhi is essential context for understanding the protagonist's movement between households in "व्यक्ति."

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Correct answer: Partition created a massive migrant population in Delhi (especially Punjabi refugees) who lacked social networks, property, and stable employment—forcing women into domestic work across middle-class homes where they faced intrusive questioning about marital status, caste, and 'character.' A wrong answer focusing only on "poverty" or "displacement" without connecting to the specific power dynamics in employer-employee relationships (evident in the text's repeated interrogation scenes) shows the student hasn't grasped how historical trauma structures everyday interpersonal power.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
A Class 11 student writes: "Krishna Sobti wrote 'व्यक्ति' to show how hard life was for poor women in the 1950s." Using the concept of 'autobiographical fiction' (आत्मकथात्मक उपन्यास), explain what this statement misses about the text's literary strategy.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Correct answer: The statement reduces a crafted literary work to sociological documentation. Autobiographical fiction deliberately blurs author/narrator boundaries—Sobti uses her own experiences (Partition displacement, writing career, refusal of awards) but transforms them through narrative techniques: fragmented chronology, stream-of-consciousness, meta-fictional moments where the protagonist discusses writing itself. A wrong answer treats the text as transparent window rather than constructed artifact, missing how Sobti makes the act of writing part of the protagonist's liberation.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the narrative stance of "व्यक्ति" with Premchand's "कफन" (Kafan) regarding representation of laboring classes. What fundamental shift in Hindi literature does this comparison reveal?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Correct answer: Premchand's third-person, reformist gaze observes Budhia and Ghisu from outside with authorial judgment; Sobti's first-person narrative refuses external perspective entirely—the protagonist speaks, judges, withholds, and controls her own disclosure. This marks the shift from *social realism* (writer as reformer documenting 'the poor') to *self-representation* (marginalized subject as writer). A wrong answer noting only "different time periods" or "different authors" fails to analyze the epistemological shift: who has the right to represent whom, and from which position.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate this claim: "The protagonist's refusal to answer employers' questions about her husband and children is an act of feminist resistance." Support your evaluation with two specific textual strategies Sobti employs.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Correct answer: Partially valid but incomplete. The refusal IS resistance, but Sobti complicates it: (1) The protagonist *does* answer selectively—she performs the 'good servant' role when convenient (cooking festivals, caring for children), revealing resistance as tactical, not absolute. (2) The narrative structure itself resists—fragmented memories, non-linear time, and the meta-fictional diary/letter episodes (रक्रूक's gift of notebook) make the text's form an argument for narrative sovereignty. A wrong answer treating resistance as binary (she resists / she doesn't) misses Sobti's nuanced portrayal of survival within patriarchy.

Write a 100-word 'context card' for a classmate who hasn't read the chapter, explaining: who Krishna Sobti was, what historical moment "व्यक्ति" emerges from, and why this text matters for understanding contemporary debates about domestic workers' rights in India.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
Expected criteria: Mentions Sobti's identity (Punjabi refugee, Jnanpith winner, feminist writer), historical moment (1950s-70s Delhi, refugee colonies, domestic labor informalization), and connects to present (2023 Supreme Court ruling on domestic workers' social security, #MeToo in domestic work, invisibility of care labor). A poor answer lists facts without synthesis—e.g., "Sobti was a writer. Partition happened. Domestic workers have rights now"—revealing inability to construct historical continuity between literary representation and policy discourse.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often confuse 'context' with 'summary'—they retell the story's events (husband left, children grew up, mother died) instead of explaining the *conditions of possibility* for the text: the literary-historical moment when a woman like Sobti could publish a first-person narrative of domestic labor in Hindi, the Progressive Writers' Movement's influence, the specific economics of 'part-time' domestic work in post-Partition Delhi. This error occurs because school assessments traditionally reward plot recall over contextual framing, and students haven't been taught to distinguish *diegetic* content (what happens in the story) from *extradiegetic* context (what makes the story possible and meaningful).

## ON_FAILURE
If a student conflates context with summary: (1) Give them two columns—label one "Inside the Story" (characters, events, dialogue) and one "Outside the Story" (author's life, publication history, social laws, literary movements). (2) Have them place 10 items (e.g., "Partition 1947," "Protagonist's husband leaves," "Sobti wins Jnanpith 2017," "Employer asks about caste") in correct columns. (3) Ask: "Which column helps you understand *why* Sobti chose first-person narration?" (4) Assign: write 3 sentences using ONLY "Outside" column items to explain the text's significance.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A student reads the opening pages of "व्यक्ति" where the protagonist describes employers asking: "तुम्हारा पति कहाँ रहता है? तुम यहाँ कब से हो? तुम्हारे बच्चे कहाँ हैं?" The student writes: "This shows employers were curious about her personal life." Improve this answer using contextual knowledge about power dynamics in 1950s domestic work.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the gap—'curiosity' is a neutral/positive word that obscures the power imbalance; the questioning is surveillance, not conversation.
- Step 2: Recall context—post-Partition migrant women had no labor protections; employers controlled wages, housing, references, and could dismiss without notice.
- Step 3: Apply the concept of 'interrogation as control'—the questions verify 'character' (चरित्र), a euphemism for sexual respectability, determining whether she's 'safe' to employ.
- Step 4: Connect to Sobti's authorial choice—the protagonist's internal monologue ("ऐसी बातें सुनकर किसी के दिल की धड़कन न बढ़े") reveals the psychological toll, which 'curiosity' erases.
- Step 5: Rewrite using analytical vocabulary: "The employers' intrusive questioning functions as moral surveillance, leveraging economic dependency to police the migrant woman's sexuality and marital status—a mechanism of class and gender control in the informal domestic economy."

REASONING: This models the move from descriptive summary (what happens) to analytical interpretation (what it means in context), using specific historical knowledge (informal economy, moral policing of migrant women) and literary analysis (internal monologue as resistance).

ANSWER: Improved answer: "The employers' questioning isn't mere curiosity—it's moral surveillance rooted in the power asymmetry of 1950s informal domestic labor. As a Partition-displaced migrant without contracts or protections, the protagonist's employment depends on passing 'character' verification. The repeated interrogation about husband, children, and residence functions to police her sexuality and enforce middle-class respectability norms. Sobti conveys the psychological violence through the protagonist's internal monologue ('ऐसी बातें सुनकर किसी के दिल की धड़कन न बढ़े'), showing how economic vulnerability compounds gendered humiliation."

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: The text mentions the protagonist learning to read/write later in life and receiving a notebook from रक्रूक (Rakrook). A student asks: "Why does Sobti include this meta-fictional detail about writing materials?" Answer using knowledge of women's literary history in Hindi.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the specific detail—रक्रूक gifts a pen and notebook, urging the protagonist to write her life story ("अपनी जीवन-कथा भी लिख डाल").
- Step 2: Recall context—Hindi literary sphere (1950s-70s) was male-dominated; women's writing was confined to 'domestic' themes; autobiographical voice was suspect.
- Step 3: Analyze the symbolism—the notebook represents *access to literacy* and *legitimization of her voice*; रक्रूक (an educated male employer) acts as patron but the protagonist claims authorship on her own terms.
- Step 4: Connect to Sobti's biography—Sobti herself faced criticism for 'unfeminine' writing; her protagonist's writing journey mirrors the author's struggle for recognition.
- Step 5: Synthesize—The notebook episode transforms the text from 'story of a servant' to 'story of a writer emerging,' asserting that laboring women are not just objects of literature but its producers.

REASONING: This demonstrates how a textual detail (gift of notebook) operates at multiple registers: realistic (plot device), symbolic (literacy as power), meta-fictional (commentary on authorship), and biographical (author's own struggle)—requiring context to unlock.

ANSWER: The notebook scene functions as a meta-fictional declaration: the domestic worker is not merely a subject to be written *about* (by middle-class authors like Premchand) but a writer who produces her own narrative. In 1950s-70s Hindi literature, women's autobiographical writing was marginalized as 'confessional' or 'domestic.' By staging the moment of receiving writing tools—and the protagonist's subsequent struggle with "लिखना नहीं आता" yet persisting—Sobti parallels her own entry into the literary field. The detail refuses the 'native informant' role; the protagonist controls what gets written, what's withheld (burning letters), and how her story circulates.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3

STEPS:
- Step 1: Deconstruct the prompt—requires (a) historical context: Partition displacement, (b) textual evidence: specific protagonist experiences, (c) analytical link: how context shapes identity/employment.
- Step 2: Draft thesis—Partition created the structural conditions (landlessness, disrupted kinship, urban refugee economy) that forced the protagonist into migratory domestic work and made her identity perpetually interrogable.
- Step 3: Select evidence—(i) Repeated questioning about husband/village = no fixed address/kinship verification; (ii) Movement between households = no stable employment, characteristic of refugee women's labor; (iii) "घर का मालिक नहीं हूँ" = loss of property/patriarchal anchor; (iv) Children's schooling struggles = intergenerational refugee disadvantage.
- Step 4: Structure using PEEL—Point: Partition displacement structures her precarity. Evidence: Textual moments of interrogation/mobility. Explanation: Refugee women entered informal domestic economy without labor rights. Link: This makes her identity fragmented and contested.
- Step 5: Refine vocabulary—Use terms: 'अनौपचारिक अर्थव्यवस्था' (informal economy), 'नैतिक निगरानी' (moral surveillance), 'विस्थापित अस्मिता' (displaced identity).
- Step 6: Verify word count and board-exam requirements—clear thesis, textual evidence, analytical terminology, concluding sentence linking to broader significance.

REASONING: Models the exact board-exam skill: integrating historical context with textual analysis in a structured argument. The 6 steps mirror the cognitive process: prompt analysis → thesis → evidence selection → structure → terminology → polish.

ANSWER: The 1947 Partition generated a massive refugee influx into Delhi, dismantling agrarian kinship networks and property rights—conditions that directly produce the protagonist's precarious existence in "व्यक्ति." As a displaced Punjabi woman without paternal home or husband's patronage, she enters the informal domestic economy where employment depends on daily moral verification. The text illustrates this through repeated employer interrogations—"तुम्हारा पति कहाँ है? तुम्हारे बच्चे कहाँ हैं?"—which function as citizenship tests in a city where refugees lacked documentation. Her mobility across households (लोगों के घरों में काम करती रही) mirrors the refugee woman's exclusion from formal labor protections. When she states "मुझे घर का मालिक नहीं हूँ," it encapsulates the structural landlessness of Partition refugees. Her children's interrupted education reflects intergenerational displacement. Sobti thus transforms personal narrative into historical testimony: the protagonist's fragmented identity and laboring body bear the archive of a refugee crisis that the nation's literature had largely silenced.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: "Krishna Sobti's 'व्यक्ति' has been called 'a history of the present written from below.' Using your knowledge of the author's biography and the socio-political context of 1950s-70s Delhi, explain what 'from below' means in this formulation. Provide two specific textual strategies Sobti uses to achieve this perspective."

COMPETENCY: Historical-literary synthesis — connecting authorial position, historical context, and narrative technique to evaluate a critical claim.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Define 'from below'—not just 'about poor people' but *epistemic perspective*: the narrative originates in the laboring woman's consciousness, not the employer's gaze.
- Step 2: Link to Sobti's biography—she was a Partition refugee, wrote from margins of literary establishment, refused state honors (Padma Bhushan), aligned with progressive movements—her own position informs the narrative stance.
- Step 3: Identify textual strategy 1—First-person interiority: the protagonist's unspoken thoughts ("मन में सोचती") contradict her spoken compliance, giving readers access to a consciousness employers never see.
- Step 4: Identify textual strategy 2—Refusal of resolution: the narrative doesn't end with 'uplift' (marriage, stable job, recognition) but with ongoing writing struggle, refusing the middle-class bildungsroman arc.
- Step 5: Synthesize—'From below' means the text's formal choices (fragmentation, interiority, open ending) enact the epistemological position of the subaltern subject.

ANSWER: 'From below' signifies an epistemic stance: the narrative authority resides in the domestic worker's lived experience, not the middle-class observer's interpretation. Sobti achieves this through: (1) Radical interiority—the protagonist's silent thoughts ("यह लोग क्या जानें") directly counterpoint her spoken deference, exposing the dual consciousness of subaltern labor. (2) Structural refusal of 'redemption'—the narrative rejects the reformist arc where the servant is 'saved' by education/marriage; instead, it ends with the protagonist still writing, still struggling, still *becoming*. This mirrors Sobti's own trajectory: a refugee woman who entered Hindi letters without patronage, writing on her own terms. The text thus doesn't just represent the subaltern—it *becomes* a site where subaltern speech acts are staged, heard, and legitimized as literature.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: "A Class 11 student argues: 'The protagonist in "व्यक्ति" is passive—she just moves from house to house, answers questions, endures insults. She doesn't *do* anything revolutionary.' Drawing on the concept of 'everyday resistance' (जेम्स स्कॉट) and the specific socio-historical context of the text, write a 200-word rebuttal that redefines 'agency' for this character."

COMPETENCY: Critical reinterpretation — applying a theoretical framework (everyday resistance) to challenge a surface-level reading, using historical context to redefine key terms.

STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the student's error—equating agency only with dramatic, visible rebellion (quitting, confronting, organizing).
- Step 2: Introduce James Scott's 'everyday resistance'—foot-dragging, feigned ignorance, pilfering, feigned compliance, hidden transcripts—forms of resistance available to those without structural power.
- Step 3: Map to textual evidence—(i) Protagonist's selective answering: performs 'good servant' while withholding truth; (ii) Burning letters: controls her archive; (iii) Learning to write in secret: claims literacy on her terms; (iv) Leaving the final household: exercises exit option when pushed.
- Step 4: Contextualize—In 1950s informal domestic work, open confrontation meant dismissal, blacklisting, starvation; 'passivity' was survival strategy.
- Step 5: Redefine agency—Agency = capacity to maintain selfhood within constraints; the protagonist's *narrative control* (she tells the story, she decides what we know) is the ultimate resistance.
- Step 6: Conclude—The student's 'revolutionary' standard is a middle-class luxury; for the protagonist, writing her life *is* the revolution.

ANSWER: The student confuses *visible rebellion* with *agency*. James Scott's "Weapons of the Weak" shows that for those in totalizing power structures (like 1950s live-in domestic work—no contracts, no unions, employer controls food/housing/reference), resistance takes coded forms: foot-dragging, feigned compliance, hidden transcripts. The protagonist deploys these precisely: she answers employers' interrogations with calculated half-truths ("कुछ नहीं बताती"), performs the 'grateful servant' while internally rejecting their moral authority ("मन में सोचती... ये क्या जानें"), burns letters to control her archive, teaches herself to write in stolen moments, and ultimately *writes the very text we're reading*—seizing narrative authority from the literary establishment that would silence her. Her departure from the final household ("मैंने घर छोड़ दिया") is not dramatic confrontation but a calculated exit when dignity's cost exceeds survival's value. In a context where a single bad reference meant unemployability, her 'passivity' is tactical discipline. The ultimate agency: she becomes the *author* of her life, not its subject. Sobti makes this explicit—रक्रूक gifts a notebook saying "लिख डाल अपनी कहानी," and the protagonist does, on her own terms. That act—claiming the pen—is more revolutionary than any strike, because it inserts the domestic worker into the literary canon as a speaking subject.
