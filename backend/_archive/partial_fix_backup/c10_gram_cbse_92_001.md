## DEFINITION
Modals are function words — each encodes a specific communicative job: ability (can/could), permission (may/can/could), obligation (must/should/ought to), possibility (may/might/could), request (would/could/will). Board questions test the FUNCTION, not the word: 'You ___ see a doctor' wants advice (should), not ability.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
Given a context, the student selects the modal matching the intended function (advice vs obligation vs permission vs possibility) and can articulate the nuance difference between near-synonyms (must/should, may/might).

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers the nine core modals and their functions in declaratives, questions, negatives. Does NOT cover modal perfects (92_002) or semi-modals used to/need to/dare (92_003).

## WALKTHROUGH
Line up the obligation ladder: may (permission) < should (advice) < must (command). 'You may leave' — I allow it. 'You should leave' — I advise it. 'You must leave' — I demand it. Same leaving, three power levels. Then possibility: may ≈ 50%, might ≈ 30%, could ≈ 'open option'. Numbers aren't exact but the ORDER is what boards test.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students treat modals as interchangeable politeness decorations, pick by 'sounds right' — usually overusing 'can' and 'should' — and believe 'must' and 'should' are synonyms.

## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Ask what the sentence is DOING — advising? permitting? guessing? commanding? Step 2: Pick the modal whose function matches, not the first modal that fits grammatically. Step 3: For near-pairs, name the strength difference out loud before answering.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
'You ___ work hard if you want a seat in IIT.' Fill with should/must/may and justify.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
'must' — necessity for a required goal; 'should' understates it as optional advice. A wrong answer 'should' reveals the student grades obligation by politeness rather than by consequence — boards mark the stronger obligation correct when the outcome is non-negotiable.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
'___ I borrow your pen?' — polite, formal permission. Which modal?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
'May' (or 'Could' for polite request). 'Can I borrow' is informal. A wrong answer showing no awareness of the register difference reveals the student thinks modals differ only in meaning, not in formality level.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
'It ___ rain tomorrow — the sky is clear.' Choose may/must/should.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The clear sky undercuts certainty — 'may' (possibility) or better 'might'. 'Must rain' claims certainty the context denies. A wrong answer 'must' reveals the student reads modals as filler and ignores the evidential clue in the sentence.
