## DEFINITION
Sentence structure is about clause count and dependence: simple = one finite verb, compound = independent clauses joined by coordinators (and/but/or/yet/so/for), complex = one main + subordinate clause(s) joined by because/although/which/when/that. Transformations move meaning between these containers — 'Being tired, she slept' ≡ 'She was tired, so she slept' ≡ 'Because she was tired, she slept.'

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
analyze

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student counts clauses correctly, identifies the connector type each structure needs, transforms among all three forms preserving meaning, and punctuates each correctly (comma-splice recognition).

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers the three structural classes and their mutual transforms. Does NOT cover compound-complex or clause-type identification (chapter 96).

## WALKTHROUGH
Take one meaning — 'tired → slept' — and pour it into three containers. Simple packs it into a phrase: 'Being tired…' Compound balances two equal boxes with 'so'. Complex subordinates the reason: 'Because…' The test for structure: count FINITE verbs — one = simple, two equals = compound, two with a subordinator = complex.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students call any long sentence 'complex', miss that 'and/but' between two full clauses is compound not complex, and write comma splices ('She was tired, she slept') believing a comma can join clauses.

## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Count finite verbs. Step 2: If two+, check the joiner — coordinator = compound, subordinator = complex, none = comma splice. Step 3: To transform, repackage the subordinate idea as a phrase (simple), an equal clause (compound), or a because/although-clause (complex).

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Classify: 'Although it rained, the match continued.'

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Complex — 'although' subordinates the first clause. A wrong answer 'compound' reveals the student classifies by clause COUNT alone, ignoring that the joiner type (subordinator vs coordinator) is the actual diagnostic.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Make it simple: 'He was tired, so he went to bed.'

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
'Being tired, he went to bed' (or 'Tired, he went to bed'). A wrong answer 'He was tired and went to bed' keeps two finite verbs — the student joined clauses but never reduced the count, so the sentence is still compound.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Is this legal? 'She loves maths, she practises daily.'

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
No — comma splice; needs 'and' (compound), 'because' with order flip (complex), or a full stop. A wrong answer accepting it reveals the student believes a comma has conjunctive power — the single most common punctuation-structure error.
