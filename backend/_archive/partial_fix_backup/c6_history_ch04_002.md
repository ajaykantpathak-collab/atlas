## DEFINITION
The Harappan Civilisation was one of the world's first major urban societies, flourishing in the Indus Valley between 2600 BCE and 1900 BCE. It was identified in the 1920s when archaeologists uncovered large-scale city remains at sites like Harappa and Mohenjo-daro.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify the approximate time period and geographical reach of the Harappan Civilisation and distinguish it as an urban society based on archaeological findings.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the discovery and the geographical extent of the Harappan Civilisation. It excludes specific excavation techniques, theories regarding its decline, and comparisons with contemporary civilisations like Mesopotamia.
## WALKTHROUGH
If you were exploring a dusty site in Pakistan and found ancient bricks and city walls, how would you know how old they are? To begin, we look at the work of archaeologists who, in the 1920s, realized that the sites of Harappa and Mohenjo-daro were not just random villages but massive, planned cities. Think of this like finding the blueprint of a giant city buried under the ground. We use the timeline of 2600 BCE to 1900 BCE to map these events. The key insight is that this civilisation was so large—stretching over 1.2 million square kilometres—that it represents the first major "urban" or city-dwelling culture in our history.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
In which decade did archaeologists begin the discovery of the major sites of the Harappan Civilisation?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The discovery began in the 1920s. A wrong answer here suggests the student has not distinguished between the historical period of the civilisation (2600 BCE) and the time it was "discovered" by modern historians.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why do historians classify the Harappan Civilisation as an "urban" civilisation?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
It is called urban because it featured large, complex cities like Harappa and Mohenjo-daro rather than small, scattered farming hamlets. A wrong answer indicates the student does not understand the difference between a simple village and a planned, city-based society.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If an archaeologist finds a Harappan site, does it automatically belong to the 2600 BCE–1900 BCE period? Explain your reasoning.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Not necessarily; they must use carbon dating or compare the pottery/artifacts found to known Harappan styles to confirm the date. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks all ancient ruins are from the same time period.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
How does the vast geographical extent (1.2 million square kilometres) change our understanding of these early people?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
It shows they were not an isolated group but a vast network of connected cities across a huge region. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks of the civilisation as a small, single town rather than a widespread culture.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Could we call the Harappan Civilisation "modern" because it had cities? Justify your stance.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, we call it "early urban," because while they had cities, they lacked modern technology like electricity or the internet. The student must distinguish between the structure of a society (cities) and the level of technology.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Imagine you are an archaeologist. What clues would you look for to prove a newly discovered site is part of the Harappan Civilisation?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
I would look for specific styles of baked bricks, urban planning (grid patterns), and types of pottery or seals characteristic of the Harappan culture. A poor answer would focus on vague items like "old tools" which could belong to any time period.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often confuse the "discovery" of the civilisation (1920s) with the "existence" of the civilisation (2600 BCE). They might assume that because we only found it recently, it must be a modern invention or not very old.
## ON_FAILURE
Clarify that history is like a puzzle: the "event" happened thousands of years ago, but the "discovery" is the moment we find the pieces. Use the analogy of finding a lost diary in your attic; the diary was written years ago (existence), but you only just found it (discovery). Emphasize that "1920s" is the date the researchers started the excavation, not the date the cities were built.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: If the Harappan Civilisation lasted 700 years and ended in 1900 BCE, in what year did it begin?
STEPS:
- Identify the duration: 700 years.
- Identify the end date: 1900 BCE.
- Recognize that BCE counts backwards.
- Add the duration to the end date: 1900 + 700.
- Result: 2600 BCE.
REASONING: Since BCE years count down towards zero, starting 700 years before 1900 BCE requires adding the time to get to the earlier starting point.
ANSWER: 2600 BCE.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: An archaeologist claims a site is Harappan because it covers 500 square kilometres. Is this evidence enough based on the text?
STEPS:
- Identify the total extent: 1.2 million square kilometres.
- Compare the claim (500 sq km) to the known range.
- Recognize that size alone does not confirm a site is Harappan.
- Note that other artifacts (pottery, bricks) are needed for identification.
- Conclusion: It is insufficient evidence.
REASONING: A large civilisation is made of many sites, but a single site's size is not the only factor; evidence must match the period and cultural style.
ANSWER: No, size alone is not enough; researchers need to find specific Harappan-style objects to confirm the site's identity.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Two students discuss the Harappan Civilisation. Student A says, "They were a small tribe in Punjab." Student B says, "They were a massive, spread-out civilisation." Who is correct and why?
STEPS:
- Define the term "civilisation" as a complex, large-scale society.
- Review the text: "covered a vast area — over 1.2 million square kilometres."
- Evaluate Student A: "Small tribe" contradicts the "urban civilisation" definition.
- Evaluate Student B: "Massive, spread-out" matches the 1.2 million sq km data.
- Synthesize the answer.
REASONING: The geographic scale proves it was not a small tribe, but a widespread society spanning from Afghanistan to India/Pakistan.
ANSWER: Student B is correct because the civilisation covered over 1.2 million square kilometres and consisted of multiple major urban centres, not just one small tribe.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If a new site is discovered in a different part of the subcontinent, what questions should an archaeologist ask to determine if it is "Harappan"?
COMPETENCY: Historical Inquiry
STEPS:
- Ask about the age of the site (does it fall near 2600–1900 BCE?).
- Ask about the city structure (is it planned?).
- Check for consistent artifacts (seals, pottery).
- Check the location relative to other known Harappan sites.
ANSWER: An archaeologist should ask: Does the site contain bricks and city planning typical of that era? Does the carbon dating of the remains match the 2600–1900 BCE period? Are there artifacts similar to those found in Harappa or Mohenjo-daro?
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Why is the discovery of Harappa in the 1920s considered a "turning point" in our understanding of Indian history?
COMPETENCY: Historical Significance
STEPS:
- Contrast the pre-1920s belief (that Indian history started much later).
- Identify the impact of finding a sophisticated urban culture.
- Explain how this pushed the timeline of Indian "urban" history back by thousands of years.
- Conclude that it changed the narrative of when civilization began in the region.
ANSWER: Before the 1920s, many believed cities in India were a much later development. Finding Harappa proved that highly organized, urban life existed in the subcontinent as early as 2600 BCE, proving that India had one of the world's oldest urban civilisations.
DIFFICULTY: hard
