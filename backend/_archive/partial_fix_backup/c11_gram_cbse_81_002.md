## DEFINITION
Synthesis merges separate sentences into one — using participles ('Hearing the news, she fainted'), appositives ('Mr Rao, our principal, retired'), infinitives of purpose, and relative clauses. The merged sentence must keep every idea with its original weight.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
create

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student combines 2-3 simple sentences into one grammatical sentence choosing the appropriate merging device, without dropping or distorting any idea.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers sentence-combining techniques. Does NOT cover splitting or paragraph-level cohesion.

## WALKTHROUGH
The merger menu: shared subject → participle ('She heard the news. She fainted.' → 'Hearing the news, she fainted'). Identity relation → appositive. Purpose → infinitive ('He saved money to buy a cycle'). Description → relative clause. The test: does the merged sentence still say everything the originals said?

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students simply stack sentences with 'and…and…and' or create dangling participles — 'Walking down the road, the house appeared' (the house wasn't walking).

## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Find the relationship between the sentences — sequence, cause, identity, purpose, description. Step 2: Pick the matching merger device. Step 3: Check the participle's implied subject = main clause subject (no dangling).

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Merge: 'She heard the news. She fainted.'

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
'Hearing the news, she fainted' — participle merge on shared subject. A wrong answer 'She heard the news and she fainted' is grammatically legal but misses the tested device.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Fix: 'Walking down the road, a snake appeared.'

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Dangling participle — 'walking' implies the snake walked. Fix: 'Walking down the road, I saw a snake' or 'As I walked down the road, a snake appeared.'

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Merge with an appositive: 'Mr Verma is our new principal. He introduced himself today.'

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
'Mr Verma, our new principal, introduced himself today.' A wrong answer 'Mr Verma who is our new principal' works but the appositive is the tighter, tested form.
