## DEFINITION
Modals are graded on function: ability (can/could), permission (may/can), obligation (must), advice (should/ought to), possibility (may/might/could), request (would/could). The board asks which modal the CONTEXT needs — the sentence's job, not the word's feel.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
apply

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student selects the modal matching the context's function and strength, distinguishes must/should obligation levels and may/might possibility levels, and uses the formal-permission 'may' correctly.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers the nine core modals in declaratives and questions. Does NOT cover modal perfects (81_002) or semi-modals (used to, needn't).

## WALKTHROUGH
The obligation ladder: may < should < must. The possibility ladder: might < may < could < will(probability). Context clues decide: 'You ___ see a doctor' — if it is a recommendation → should; if a strict instruction → must. Formal permission asks 'May I?'; casual asks 'Can I?'.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students pick by politeness-feel, overusing 'should' and 'can', and treat 'must'/'should' as synonyms — the strength distinction is invisible to them.

## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Ask what the sentence DOES — advise, permit, command, guess? Step 2: Gauge the strength the context needs. Step 3: Match the modal whose function+strength fits.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
'You ___ submit the form by Friday — it is compulsory.'

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
'must' — 'compulsory' makes it obligation, not advice. A wrong answer 'should' understates a requirement as a suggestion — the strength-mismatch error.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
'___ I use your phone?' — formal permission to a teacher.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
'May' — the formal permission modal. 'Can' is grammatical but casual; boards prefer 'may' when register is tested. A wrong answer 'must' changes the function to obligation.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
'It ___ rain tonight — dark clouds are gathering.' may or will?

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
'may' — clouds suggest possibility, not certainty; 'will' would claim the forecast is settled. A wrong answer 'will' shows the student reads modals as tense markers, not strength markers.
