## DEFINITION
Science is a systematic way of observing our surroundings, asking questions about how things work, and using a step-by-step method to find logical answers. It is not limited to textbooks but is a process of exploration applied to everything from cooking to the stars.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify the steps of the scientific method in a real-life situation and explain how curiosity drives the process of discovery.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the definition of science and the scientific method in daily life. It excludes industrial technology, future speculative tech, or advanced laboratory procedures.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever wondered why your pen suddenly stopped writing even though it looked full of ink? To figure it out, you probably checked the nib, looked for a leak, or tried scribbling on a different paper. Congratulations—you just used the scientific method! You observed a problem, guessed a cause, and tested that guess. Science is simply that "detective work" applied to everything, like why a plant grows toward the window or why ice melts. By staying curious and asking "why," you turn everyday moments into scientific discoveries.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is the very first step in the scientific method?
A) Testing a guess
B) Observing something interesting
C) Giving up
D) Reading a textbook
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
B) Observing something interesting. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks science starts with a pre-written answer rather than a genuine question sparked by observation.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you guess why a plant is dying, is that the end of the scientific process? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
No, it is not the end. You must test your guess through observation or experiments to see if it is actually correct. A wrong answer shows the student ignores the "testing" phase, which is what separates a blind guess from science.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Your friend says, "Science is only for people in white coats in labs." How do you use the scientific method to prove them wrong?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Explain a daily example, like a bicycle repair person finding a flat tire leak. By checking the tire (observation), guessing it's a puncture (hypothesis), and putting it in water to see bubbles (testing), they are performing the scientific method. A wrong answer indicates the student hasn't connected scientific steps to non-lab environments.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Why do we call science an "unending jigsaw puzzle"?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Because every new discovery creates more questions, and sometimes we have to rearrange our previous knowledge if a new piece (discovery) doesn't fit. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks science is "finished" or static, rather than an evolving process.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it better to do science alone or with others? Justify your choice based on the nature of science.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Working with others is often better because science is rarely a solitary task; team members can share observations and help solve problems faster. A wrong answer shows a lack of understanding that scientific inquiry thrives on collaboration and shared curiosity.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Think of a kitchen mystery (like why milk spills when boiled). Describe how you would investigate this using the scientific method.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The student should identify the observation (milk spilling), form a hypothesis (too high heat or full pot), test it by changing one factor (e.g., using a larger pot or lower heat), and observe the result. A poor answer lacks the structured "test" step.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often believe that if their "guess" (hypothesis) turns out to be wrong, they have failed at science. In reality, a "wrong" guess is a crucial part of the process because it helps eliminate possibilities and leads to the correct answer. For example, if a student guesses a lightbulb is burnt out but finds it is actually a loose switch, they haven't failed; they've performed successful science.
## ON_FAILURE
1. Explain that scientists often have "wrong" guesses that lead them to the right path. 
2. Ask the student to identify one thing they learned from a "mistake" (e.g., "I learned that the bulb wasn't the problem, so now I know how to check the switch"). 
3. Reframe the process as: "Observation -> Guess -> Test -> Result." Emphasize that the "Result" is the valuable part, regardless of whether it matches the initial guess.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: You notice your 500ml water bottle is always empty by lunchtime. You guess it's because you are thirsty after playing. How do you prove this?
STEPS:
- 1: Observe that the bottle is empty at 1:00 PM.
- 2: Guess that exercise causes the thirst.
- 3: Test by keeping a record: drink the same amount on a day with no exercise vs. a day with heavy exercise.
- 4: Compare results.
REASONING: By changing only one variable (exercise), you can see if it affects water consumption. This shows that testing a guess requires comparing controlled conditions.
ANSWER: If the bottle is fuller on rest days, your guess is supported.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: You want to know if salt makes water boil faster. How do you test this?
STEPS:
- 1: Take two identical vessels with 500ml of water each.
- 2: Add 1 teaspoon of salt to one vessel; leave the other plain.
- 3: Place both on identical stove flames.
- 4: Use a watch to record the time taken for both to boil.
REASONING: By keeping the volume and flame size constant, you isolate the effect of salt. This is the essence of controlled testing.
ANSWER: Comparing the time difference reveals if salt affects the boiling point.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A friend says a magnet picks up all metal objects. How do you disprove this?
STEPS:
- 1: Collect a paperclip, a copper coin, and an iron nail.
- 2: Guess that the magnet will attract all three because they are "metals."
- 3: Test each object by bringing the magnet close.
- 4: Record that only the iron nail is attracted.
- 5: Conclude that the "all metals" guess is false.
REASONING: Science uses testing to correct wrong assumptions. Even if your initial guess was wrong, the experiment provided the true answer.
ANSWER: The hypothesis is rejected; not all metals are magnetic.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You have two potted plants. One is in the sun, one in a dark cupboard. You think the sun makes them grow taller. How do you test this, and what is your "control" (the item you don't change)?
COMPETENCY: Experimental design and control identification.
STEPS:
- 1: Place one plant in the sun.
- 2: Keep the other in the cupboard.
- 3: Ensure both get same water/soil.
- 4: Measure height after one week.
ANSWER: The plant in the sun is the test; the plant in the cupboard is the comparison. By keeping water/soil the same, you ensure the only difference is light.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: If you find out your guess was wrong during an experiment, does that mean you are a bad scientist? Explain using the concept of the "jigsaw puzzle."
COMPETENCY: Analyzing the nature of scientific inquiry.
STEPS:
- 1: Define the jigsaw puzzle analogy.
- 2: Explain that a wrong guess is just a piece that doesn't fit.
- 3: Argue that finding the wrong spot helps you find the right one.
ANSWER: No, you are a good scientist. In the puzzle of science, discovering where a piece doesn't fit is just as important as finding where it does, because it clears the path to the truth.
DIFFICULTY: hard
