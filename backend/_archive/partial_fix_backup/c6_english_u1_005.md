## DEFINITION
Poetic devices are creative tools like rhyme, metaphor, and imagery that poets use to turn simple words into art that paints a picture, creates a rhythm, or highlights a hidden truth.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to identify a rhyme scheme (AABB/ABAB), spot a metaphor in a sentence, and describe the sensory details that create imagery in a poem. They must accurately label these in a short, unseen poem excerpt.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the identification and basic function of rhyme schemes, metaphors, and imagery. It does not cover advanced meter, free verse, alliteration, or personification.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever wondered why some song lyrics or poems get stuck in your head while others don't? It’s usually because they use "poetic shortcuts" to make you feel or see something quickly. To start, read a poem out loud to catch the rhythm of the end-sounds (rhyme). Then, look for sentences that compare two different things without using "like" or "as" (metaphor). Finally, close your eyes and ask, "What can I see, hear, or smell here?" (imagery). These steps turn a flat page into a living experience.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What is a rhyme scheme, and how do we label it?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
A rhyme scheme is the pattern of sounds at the ends of lines in a poem, labeled using letters (like AABB). If a student says "it’s just the last word," they are missing the pattern aspect—the labels show which lines match, not just the existence of the word.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Explain the difference between a direct description (e.g., "The field was green") and a metaphor (e.g., "The field was a velvet blanket").
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
A direct description states a fact, but a metaphor creates a deeper meaning by saying the field *is* the blanket, suggesting softness and comfort. A wrong answer usually confuses metaphors with similes (which use "like" or "as"), showing the student doesn't realize metaphors drop those comparison words.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Identify the imagery in this line: "The golden sun kissed the cold morning dew on the banana leaves."
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The imagery is "golden sun," "cold morning dew," and "banana leaves." To identify this, look for words that trigger sight (golden), touch (cold), or nature (banana leaves). If a student picks only one word, they aren't capturing the full sensory scene.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
If a stanza has four lines ending in: Cat (A), Hat (A), Dog (B), Log (B), what is the rhyme scheme?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The scheme is AABB. If a student says "ABAB," they are failing to see that the first two lines rhyme together, proving they are ignoring the sequence of the sounds.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
"Life is a rollercoaster." Is this a metaphor? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Yes, it is a metaphor because it compares life to a rollercoaster to suggest it has ups and downs, without using "like" or "as." If a student says no, they might be looking for a literal, physical machine, missing the symbolic comparison.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Create a line of imagery that describes a rainy day in a village.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good answer would include sensory details like "the smell of wet earth" or "the rhythmic tapping of rain on tin roofs." A poor answer simply says "it was raining," which lacks the sensory detail required for imagery.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often confuse metaphors with similes. They think any comparison is a metaphor, even when it uses "like" or "as." For example, if a student writes "He is like a lion," they call it a metaphor, but it is actually a simile because of the word "like."
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to circle the comparison word ("like" or "as") in their sentence. If they find it, explain that metaphors are "secret" comparisons that skip the bridge words. Show them: "The moon is a coin" (Metaphor) vs. "The moon is like a coin" (Simile).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: Analyze the lines: "The ocean is a wild, blue beast. It roars against the shore."
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the comparison (Ocean = Beast).
- step 2: Check for comparison words (None present, so it is a metaphor).
- step 3: Identify sensory words (Roars = sound, Blue = sight).
- step 4: Combine these to define the mood (Powerful/Scary).
REASONING: By breaking down the metaphor and the imagery separately, we see how the poet builds a frightening mood.
ANSWER: This is a metaphor comparing the ocean to a beast; the imagery of "roaring" creates a sound-based experience of the ocean's power.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Identify the rhyme scheme of: "The stars shine bright / In the dark night / The moon is high / In the deep sky."
STEPS:
- step 1: End of line 1 is "bright" (A).
- step 2: End of line 2 is "night" (A - rhymes with bright).
- step 3: End of line 3 is "high" (B).
- step 4: End of line 4 is "sky" (B - rhymes with high).
REASONING: By mapping each end-word to a letter, the pattern emerges clearly.
ANSWER: The rhyme scheme is AABB.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Describe the imagery and metaphor in: "The road was a ribbon of moonlight stretching across the desert."
STEPS:
- step 1: Find the metaphor (Road = ribbon).
- step 2: What does the metaphor imply? (Thin, long, winding, reflective).
- step 3: What is the imagery? ("Moonlight" - visual, light; "Stretching" - movement).
- step 4: Combine the impact. The ribbon suggests the road is beautiful and delicate.
REASONING: Metaphors provide the "what," and imagery provides the "how it looks/feels."
ANSWER: Metaphor: The road is a ribbon. Imagery: Moonlight and stretching; it makes the night scene feel calm and bright.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If a poem compares a person's anger to "a bubbling pot of hot water," why is this imagery effective?
COMPETENCY: Evaluating effectiveness of imagery.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the sensory trigger (bubbling, hot).
- step 2: Link to emotion (anger).
- step 3: Explain the connection (pressure building up).
ANSWER: It is effective because "bubbling" and "hot" show the reader that the person's anger is building pressure and is ready to spill over, making the abstract feeling of anger real and physical.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Change the following literal sentence into a metaphor and add a descriptive image: "The student was smart."
COMPETENCY: Creative application of literary devices.
STEPS:
- step 1: Choose a symbol for "smart" (e.g., a candle, an owl, a light).
- step 2: Write the metaphor (The student was a bright light in the room).
- step 3: Add imagery (e.g., "shining through the dark, dusty corners").
- step 4: Review for impact.
ANSWER: "The student was a bright lighthouse, guiding the class through the dark, dusty fog of confusion." This uses a metaphor (student as lighthouse) and imagery (dark fog) to describe intelligence.
DIFFICULTY: hard
