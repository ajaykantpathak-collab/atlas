## DEFINITION
Materials are substances used to create objects, and they are classified based on properties such as appearance (lustre), hardness, and solubility in water. These properties determine the suitability of a material for a specific purpose.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to categorize common household objects by their properties (e.g., classifying a steel key as lustrous, hard, and insoluble) and predict if a material is suitable for a specific use based on these properties.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers physical appearance, hardness, and solubility. It excludes complex chemical reactivity, quantitative hardness scales (like Mohs), and in-depth electrical/thermal conductivity.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever wondered why your water bottle is made of plastic or metal instead of cloth? To understand this, look at the objects on your desk and touch them. First, notice if they are shiny (lustrous) or dull. Next, try to press them—do they resist (hard) or change shape (soft)? Finally, think about what happens when you stir them in water—do they disappear (soluble) or stay behind (insoluble)? By testing these three properties, you can decide exactly which material is best for a job, like keeping water inside a container without leaking.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What do we call a material that has a shiny surface when light falls on it?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Such a material is called lustrous. A wrong answer like "hard" reveals the student is confusing the property of appearance with the property of texture or resistance to touch.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you can easily press a material with your fingers, how would you describe its hardness?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The material is described as soft. If the student says it is "non-lustrous," they are confusing the physical property of hardness with the property of light reflection.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
You have a glass of water. You add a spoonful of sand to it and stir. Will the sand disappear? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The sand will not disappear; it is insoluble. This is because particles of insoluble materials do not mix uniformly into water to become invisible, unlike sugar or salt.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare a piece of iron and a piece of rubber. Which one is harder, and how do you know?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Iron is harder than rubber because it is more difficult to scratch or compress. A student saying they are "equal" fails to understand that hardness is a relative property that can be tested through physical resistance.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Your friend wants to make a boat out of paper, but you want to make one out of metal. Which material is more suitable for staying in water for a long time? Justify your answer.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
The metal boat is more suitable because it is insoluble and holds its form, whereas paper becomes soggy and may disintegrate. A wrong answer suggests the student hasn't considered how solubility and material strength affect object durability.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Imagine a new material that is opaque, very hard, and insoluble in water. Suggest one object you could make with it.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
An example would be a construction brick or a heavy-duty hammer head. A poor answer would be a "drinking straw," which ignores the requirement for hardness or potentially the need for transparency/flexibility.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think that if a material is hard, it must be metal, or if it is soft, it cannot be a metal. For example, they might think a sponge is not a "real" material because it is soft, or that all metals must be extremely hard. This happens because they generalize properties of specific objects (like iron nails) to all members of a group.
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to find a piece of metal that is soft (like a thin aluminum foil) and a non-metal that is hard (like a piece of dry wood or a hard plastic toy). Have them press the foil and try to scratch the wood. Explain that properties like "hard" or "soft" are specific to the material's reaction to force, not whether it is a metal or non-metal.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: You have a sugar cube, a stone, and a piece of chalk. You place each in separate beakers of water and stir. Identify which is soluble and which is insoluble.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the materials given (sugar, stone, chalk).
- step 2: Define solubility as the ability of a substance to dissolve (disappear) in water.
- step 3: Predict the outcome for each: sugar dissolves, stone and chalk do not.
- step 4: Conclude that sugar is soluble, while stone and chalk are insoluble.
- step 5: Verify by noting that "soluble" means the substance mixes completely with the water.
REASONING: Solubility depends on whether the material particles can spread out uniformly in water. Sugar molecules break apart and mix, while stone and chalk particles are too large or chemically different to do so.
ANSWER: Sugar is soluble; stone and chalk are insoluble.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: You have three objects: a metal spoon, a rubber eraser, and a cotton cloth. Rank them from hardest to softest.
STEPS:
- step 1: Identify the objects: metal spoon, rubber eraser, cotton cloth.
- step 2: Apply the test of "resistance to scratching/compression."
- step 3: Test the spoon—it cannot be compressed (Hardest).
- step 4: Test the eraser—it can be compressed with force (Medium).
- step 5: Test the cloth—it compresses and folds easily (Softest).
REASONING: Hardness is a measure of a material's resistance to change in shape. The metallic bond in the spoon is strong, the polymer in the eraser is flexible, and the fiber structure of the cloth is soft.
ANSWER: Hardest: Metal spoon; Medium: Rubber eraser; Softest: Cotton cloth.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A shopkeeper wants to store three things: salt, oil, and iron nails. Which of these will disappear if accidentally mixed in a large tank of water?
STEPS:
- step 1: Check if salt is soluble in water (Yes).
- step 2: Check if oil is soluble in water (No, it floats as a layer).
- step 3: Check if iron is soluble in water (No, it sinks and stays solid).
- step 4: Identify which material "disappears."
- step 5: Confirm salt is the only one that dissolves.
REASONING: Solubility is specific to the substance. Salt (NaCl) is ionic and dissolves, whereas iron is a solid metal, and oil is a liquid that does not mix with water (it is immiscible).
ANSWER: Salt will disappear.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: You are designing a new school bag. You have two materials: Material A (shiny, hard, insoluble) and Material B (dull, soft, becomes soggy in water). Which one would you choose to make a rain-cover for the bag? Explain your reasoning.
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying
STEPS:
- step 1: Analyze the requirements for a rain-cover (must be insoluble and durable).
- step 2: Evaluate Material A: It is hard and insoluble, so it won't break or let water through.
- step 3: Evaluate Material B: It is soft and becomes soggy, meaning it will absorb water or break.
- step 4: Decide on Material A.
ANSWER: I would choose Material A because it is insoluble and hard, ensuring it protects the bag from rain without soaking through or tearing.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: A friend says that because a diamond is hard, it must be a metal. How would you test this claim using the concept of lustre and hardness?
COMPETENCY: Error identification and correction
STEPS:
- step 1: Understand the claim (Hard = Metal).
- step 2: Identify the error (Hardness is not exclusive to metals).
- step 3: Use the property of "lustre" (metals usually reflect light; non-metals like diamonds have a different type of shine but are carbon-based).
- step 4: Explain that hardness is just one property, and testing other properties (like conductivity or chemical makeup) is needed to classify it.
ANSWER: The claim is incorrect because hardness is found in many materials, including non-metals like diamonds or rocks. To correct this, I would explain that one property (hardness) cannot identify a material; we must look at a combination of properties like lustre, solubility, and conductivity.
DIFFICULTY: hard
