## DEFINITION
Matter is defined as anything that occupies space (having volume) and possesses mass (the quantity of material present). Materials are specific types of matter that we use to create objects based on their distinct physical properties.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to classify household items by their physical properties (transparency, solubility, hardness, lustre) and identify why a specific material is chosen for a specific purpose.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers physical properties of materials (appearance, hardness, solubility, transparency, mass, volume) and the definition of matter. It does not cover chemical reactions, molecular structure, or industrial manufacturing processes.
## WALKTHROUGH
Start by looking at the water bottle on your desk; why is it made of plastic rather than paper? To understand this, begin by observing the objects in your room and grouping them based on simple traits like "can I see through it?" or "is it shiny?". Feel the difference between a soft sponge and a hard stone to understand the property of hardness. Test what happens when you mix salt versus sand in a glass of water to learn about solubility. Finally, recognize that because these items take up space in your bag and have weight, they are all examples of matter.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What are the two requirements for something to be classified as "matter"?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
To be matter, an object must occupy space (have volume) and possess mass. Answering only one of these suggests the student is confusing "matter" with just "weight" or just "size," missing the complete scientific definition.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
If you can see through a glass window clearly, but you cannot see through a wooden door, how would you classify these two materials?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The glass is transparent, and the wood is opaque. A wrong answer here indicates the student has not grasped the interaction between light and material surfaces, which is the core of this property.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
You have a sugar cube and a pebble. You place both in separate glasses of water and stir. What happens, and what property does this demonstrate?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The sugar dissolves (soluble), while the pebble remains unchanged (insoluble). This demonstrates the property of solubility, showing that not all materials behave the same way in a solvent like water.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare a gold ring and a piece of coal. Why might we classify them differently based on appearance?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
The gold ring is lustrous (shiny), while the coal is non-lustrous (dull). This shows the student understands the difference between metallic surfaces and non-metallic surfaces.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Your friend says that a large balloon filled with air is not matter because it is "light." How would you correct them?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
You would explain that even though air feels light, it occupies space inside the balloon (volume) and has mass, therefore it is definitely matter. A wrong answer here reveals a misconception that "matter" must be heavy or solid.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
If you were asked to design a new type of umbrella, what properties would you look for in a material?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The material must be opaque, insoluble in water, and likely flexible. A poor answer would ignore the function (e.g., choosing a material that dissolves in water) and fail to connect property to purpose.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think that "hardness" means "cannot be broken." They confuse hardness with strength or durability. For example, they might think a glass marble is "soft" because it can shatter if dropped, or that a piece of chalk is "hard" because it's a solid, even though it can be easily scratched or crumbled.
## ON_FAILURE
Ask the student to take a metal key and try to scratch a piece of wood, a piece of chalk, and a stone. Explain that "hardness" specifically refers to the resistance of a material to being scratched or compressed, not how easily it breaks into pieces. Repeat the test with an eraser versus a piece of wood to show that even if an object is solid, it has a relative level of hardness.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: You have 500 mL of water in a bottle. You add 50 g of salt and stir it until it disappears. Does the total volume of the mixture increase significantly, and why?
STEPS:
- Identify that salt is soluble in water.
- Recognize that when salt dissolves, it occupies the tiny spaces between water molecules.
- Note that the mass of the system increases (500 mL water + 50 g salt).
- Conclude that volume does not increase by 50 mL because the salt is "hiding" in the gaps.
REASONING: Understanding solubility helps us realize that matter can occupy space in different ways, not just by adding height to a liquid.
ANSWER: The volume increases only slightly; the salt occupies space within the water's structure.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: You need to pack three items: a glass tumbler, a cotton shirt, and a metal spoon. Classify them by transparency and hardness.
STEPS:
- Observe the glass tumbler: Light passes through, so it is transparent; it resists scratching, so it is hard.
- Observe the cotton shirt: Light does not pass through, so it is opaque; it is easily compressed, so it is soft.
- Observe the metal spoon: Light does not pass through, so it is opaque; it resists scratching, so it is hard.
- List the results.
REASONING: By systematically checking each property (transparency and hardness), we can accurately describe any object.
ANSWER: Glass: Transparent/Hard; Cotton: Opaque/Soft; Metal: Opaque/Hard.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A container holds 2 kg of sand. You pour 1 L of water into it. What is the total mass of the mixture?
STEPS:
- Define mass as the quantity of matter.
- Identify the mass of sand = 2 kg.
- Identify the mass of water = 1 kg (since 1 L of water weighs approximately 1 kg).
- Add the two masses together: 2 kg + 1 kg = 3 kg.
- State the final mass.
REASONING: This combines the concept of mass with the understanding that water has a standard density, allowing us to combine quantities of matter.
ANSWER: 3 kg.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Your teacher gives you a block of wood and a block of ice. Both are solids. How can you test which one is "harder" using only a metal nail?
COMPETENCY: Comparing and justifying physical properties.
STEPS:
- Take a metal nail.
- Scratch the surface of the wood with the nail.
- Scratch the surface of the ice with the nail.
- Observe which one shows a deeper mark or scratch more easily.
ANSWER: The material that is harder will be more difficult to scratch. If the nail makes a deep groove in the ice but only a light mark on the wood, the wood is harder.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Why do we use opaque materials for bathroom doors but transparent materials for windows? Provide a scientific justification based on properties.
COMPETENCY: Translating real-world situations to scientific principles.
STEPS:
- Define the function of a bathroom door (privacy).
- Define the function of a window (letting light in).
- Select "opaque" because it prevents light transmission (and thus sight).
- Select "transparent" because it allows light transmission.
ANSWER: We use opaque materials for bathroom doors to prevent light from passing through, ensuring privacy. We use transparent materials for windows to allow sunlight to enter, which serves the purpose of illumination.
DIFFICULTY: medium
