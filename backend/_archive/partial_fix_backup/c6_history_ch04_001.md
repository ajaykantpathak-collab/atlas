## DEFINITION
Early cities were large, organized settlements that emerged after humans transitioned from hunting and gathering to farming, allowing populations to grow and develop complex social structures. Unlike temporary nomadic camps, these cities became permanent centers for trade, specialized labor, and collective living.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to explain the transition from nomadic hunter-gatherer life to sedentary city life and identify two key reasons (food surplus and permanent settlement) why this shift occurred.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the emergence of early cities as a consequence of agricultural development. It excludes detailed archaeological methods, specific Indus Valley site analysis, or comparisons with later historical urban centers.
## WALKTHROUGH
Think about your home: why do you live in a house in a specific town instead of moving to a new forest every week? For most of human history, people moved constantly to find food, but about 10,000 years ago, humans discovered they could grow their own food. This farming provided more food than they could eat at once, which we call a "surplus." Because they no longer needed to follow herds of animals, they built permanent homes, which eventually grouped together to form the first cities. The big takeaway is that cities are the result of humans deciding to stay in one place and work together.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
What was the primary way humans gathered food before they started farming?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Early humans survived by hunting animals and gathering wild plants and fruits. A wrong answer here suggests the student confuses "hunter-gatherer" lifestyles with "farming" lifestyles, likely forgetting that agriculture was a later invention.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why did farming help villages grow into cities?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Farming produced more food than people needed to survive, creating a "surplus." This surplus allowed people to stop moving, live in permanent homes, and support a larger population. A wrong answer suggests the student does not understand that population growth is tied to food stability.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If a group of people moves every season to find water and berries, would they be able to build a large city? Why or why not?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
No, because cities require permanent buildings and large populations that stay in one place to organize and trade. A wrong answer shows a failure to connect the "sedentary" (staying in one place) nature of farming with the structure of a city.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
How does a city differ from a small, temporary camp in terms of how people spend their time?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
In a temporary camp, everyone's energy is spent finding food; in a city, the surplus food allows some people to do other tasks like making pottery or tools. A wrong answer indicates the student hasn't grasped how "specialization" or the division of labor happens when survival isn't the only priority.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it accurate to say cities appeared immediately after humans started using fire?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
No, cities appeared much later, only after humans mastered farming and settled in one place. This tests the student's ability to sequence events on a timeline rather than assuming all human progress happened at once.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Imagine you are a leader of an early human group. What is one rule you would create to help your village grow into a city?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
A good answer would involve sharing resources, organizing labor, or storing food for the future. A poor answer focuses on things irrelevant to urban growth, such as "don't run in the halls," which shows a lack of understanding of social organization in early societies.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often assume early humans "decided" to build cities overnight. They miss the gradual process where small farming villages slowly expanded as they traded more goods and created better tools. This is a common error because students are used to seeing cities already built and fully functional today.
## ON_FAILURE
First, ask the student to draw a line representing 10,000 years. Explain that the transition from a single hut to a city took thousands of years, not a few days. Use the analogy of a "seed growing into a tree"—it needs time, stability, and nourishment (food surplus) to become large and strong.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A village of 20 people needs 50kg of grain to survive a month. They currently produce 50kg. Can this village turn into a city?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the current production (50kg) vs. needs (50kg).
- Step 2: Determine if there is a "surplus" (extra food).
- Step 3: Analyze if they have enough to support people who don't farm.
- Step 4: Conclude if it can become a city.
REASONING: Cities require people to do jobs other than farming (like building). If you have zero extra food, everyone must be a farmer.
ANSWER: No, because they have no surplus, everyone must focus entirely on gathering food.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: Two groups of people exist. Group A moves to follow wild berries. Group B stays near a river and plants seeds. Which group is more likely to build a permanent town?
STEPS:
- Step 1: Compare Group A (nomadic) vs Group B (sedentary).
- Step 2: Evaluate the impact of moving vs. staying on building infrastructure.
- Step 3: Apply the definition of a city (requires permanent structures).
- Step 4: Justify the choice.
REASONING: Permanent buildings require staying in one spot to maintain them.
ANSWER: Group B, because staying near the river allows them to build homes and store crops.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: A village has a harvest that is 200% of their needs. Explain how this changes the village's future.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Define the surplus (100% extra food).
- Step 2: Determine how many people no longer need to farm.
- Step 3: Identify the new jobs they might do (pottery, tools).
- Step 4: Explain how these new jobs lead to a "city" structure.
- Step 5: Summarize the social impact.
REASONING: Surplus is the fuel for civilization; it allows people to specialize their skills.
ANSWER: The village can now support "specialists" like potters or tool-makers, leading to trade and a more complex, structured society.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: Why is the presence of a river often a feature of early cities?
COMPETENCY: Cause and Effect Reasoning
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the needs of a farmer (water for crops).
- Step 2: Identify the needs of a settlement (drinking water).
- Step 3: Connect water to soil fertility.
- Step 4: Synthesize how this supports a growing city.
ANSWER: Rivers provide water for drinking and irrigation, and deposit fertile silt, making it easier to grow the surplus food needed to sustain a city population.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: If all the farmers in an early city stopped working, what would happen to the tool-makers and potters?
COMPETENCY: Systems Analysis
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the reliance of non-farmers on farmers.
- Step 2: Trace the impact of the lack of food surplus.
- Step 3: Analyze the collapse of specialized labor.
- Step 4: Conclude the result for the city's structure.
- Step 5: Formulate the final answer.
ANSWER: The tool-makers and potters would have no food to eat, so the city would either starve or everyone would have to return to hunting and gathering to survive, causing the city to dissolve.
