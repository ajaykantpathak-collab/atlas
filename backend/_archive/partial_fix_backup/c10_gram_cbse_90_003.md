## DEFINITION
Certain adverbials and structures force specific tense choices: 'since/for' demands perfect or perfect-continuous, 'by the time' pairs with past perfect, 'while' frames a continuous background action. These markers are the examiner's fingerprints — reading them first turns tense questions from guessing into decoding.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
analyze

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can name the tense(s) licensed by since/for/by-the-time/while/already/yet/just markers, justify the choice, and spot marker–tense mismatches in editing questions.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers the high-frequency marker→tense pairs in board papers. Does NOT cover the full grid (90_001) or narrative tense sequencing (90_004).

## WALKTHROUGH
Write two frames: 'I ___ here since 2019' and 'I ___ here in 2019'. The marker is the whole game — 'since' drags the action into now (perfect), 'in 2019' locks it in the past (simple past). Then 'while': 'While she ___ (cook), the phone rang' — while sets up a background film (was cooking), interrupted by an event snapshot (rang).

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students ignore markers and translate from their mother tongue — Hindi 'main 2019 se yahan hoon' feels like present, so they write 'I am here since 2019', producing the single most common board error.

## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Underline every time expression BEFORE choosing the tense. Step 2: Match marker → licensed form (since/for → perfect; by the time → perfect; while → continuous). Step 3: Check if a second verb needs the interrupting-event form (simple past).

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Fill: 'She ___ (live) in Pune since 2018.'

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
'has been living' or 'has lived' — 'since + point of time' demands perfect/perfect-continuous. A wrong answer 'is living' or 'lives' reveals the student processed the verb but not the marker — the diagnostic signature of translating from Hindi instead of reading the English signal.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
'By the time we reached, the train ___.' Choose: left / had left / has left.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
'had left' — 'by the time' orders two past events, so the earlier one takes past perfect. A wrong answer 'left' shows the student treats both events as simultaneous — missing the two-event ordering that 'by the time' encodes.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Which is wrong and why: 'While I walked home, it was starting to rain.'

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
The framing is inverted: 'while' wants the continuous background — 'While I was walking home, it started to rain.' A wrong answer that accepts it reveals the student knows 'while + continuous' as a slogan but cannot apply it directionally (background gets the continuous, not the interrupting event).
