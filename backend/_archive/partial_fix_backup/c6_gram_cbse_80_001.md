## DEFINITION
Every English sentence needs two parts that answer two questions: the subject (WHO or WHAT the sentence is about) and the predicate (what the subject DOES or IS). 'The little boy / ran home.' A group of words missing either part is a phrase, not a sentence.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can split any simple sentence into subject and predicate, and can tell a complete sentence from a fragment (phrase) every time.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers simple sentences with one subject and one verb. Does NOT cover compound/complex sentences (class 7+) or inverted order ('Here comes the bus').

## WALKTHROUGH
Cover the verb: 'The little boy ran home' — cover 'ran home'. What is left is the subject. Then the fragment test: 'Under the bed.' Who? What happened? Nothing — it is a phrase. Then flip: 'Ate the mango.' Who ate? Missing subject — also a fragment.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students think the subject is always the first word and the longest noun — so in 'Playing in the rain is fun' they hunt for a person instead of seeing the whole activity is the subject.

## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Find the verb — the action or being word. Step 2: Ask 'who or what + verb?' — that phrase is the subject. Step 3: Check both parts exist before calling it a sentence.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
Split: 'The tall girl with red ribbons skipped to school.'

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Subject: 'The tall girl with red ribbons' — predicate: 'skipped to school'. A wrong answer making 'girl' alone the subject or 'with red ribbons' the predicate shows the student cuts phrases at word boundaries instead of asking who-did-what.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Sentence or fragment: 'Because it was raining very hard.'

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
Fragment — 'because' makes it depend on a main clause that is missing. A wrong answer calling it a sentence reveals the student counts words, not completeness — length does not make a sentence.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Which is the complete subject: 'My elder brother, who lives in Delhi, is a doctor.'

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
'My elder brother, who lives in Delhi,' — the who-clause rides along inside the subject. A wrong answer stopping at 'brother' shows the student hasn't learned a subject can carry its own describing clause.
