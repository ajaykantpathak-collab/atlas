## DEFINITION
The decline of the Harappan Civilisation refers to the period around 1900 BCE when these advanced early urban centers ceased to function as organized cities, leading to the abandonment of their complex lifestyle. It marks the end of a 700-year flourishing era, likely caused by environmental challenges that made large-scale urban living difficult.
## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand
## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student must be able to list at least two environmental theories explaining the decline and explain why historians consider these to be "theories" rather than absolute facts.
## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
Covers the period around 1900 BCE and environmental theories of decline. Excludes post-Harappan cultural developments, archaeological data analysis, and the Aryan migration theory.
## WALKTHROUGH
Have you ever seen a house left empty because the well dried up or the roof collapsed? Imagine an entire city, like Harappa or Mohenjo-daro, facing this same problem on a massive scale. To begin, think about what a city needs to survive: water for drinking, water for farming, and a stable climate. When historians look at the decline, they look at what changed in nature—like rivers shifting their paths or the land becoming too dry to grow wheat and barley. We start by acknowledging that because we don't have a "history book" written by the Harappans themselves, we have to use clues from the earth to form theories. The key insight is that large civilizations depend heavily on their environment; if the environment changes, the civilization must change or disappear.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
In what year (approximately) did the Harappan Civilisation begin its decline?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
The decline began around 1900 BCE. A student who says "5000 years ago" or "recently" shows they are struggling to use the BCE/CE timeline correctly; they need to practice counting back from the Common Era.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
Why do historians use the word "theory" when discussing the decline of the Harappan cities?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
They use "theory" because there is no direct written record or single piece of evidence that explains exactly why the cities ended. A wrong answer suggests the student thinks history is a collection of absolute facts rather than a detective-like process of piecing together clues.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
If a river near a city changes its course, how does that impact the people living there?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
Steps: 1. Identify the river as a source of water. 2. Recognize water is needed for crops (agriculture) and daily life. 3. Conclude that if the river moves, the city loses its water supply. 4. Determine that this leads to food shortages and people moving away. A wrong answer suggests the student hasn't linked agriculture to river geography.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare the impact of a dry climate on a farming village versus a large city.
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
A large city relies on a massive, steady supply of grain from surrounding fields to feed thousands of people; if the climate turns dry, that supply chain breaks, causing the city to collapse. A small village might survive by moving to a new spot, but a city is fixed in place, making it more vulnerable. A wrong answer shows a lack of understanding regarding urban complexity.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Is it possible that the Harappan Civilisation disappeared because of only one reason?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
It is unlikely, as complex societies usually fail due to a combination of factors. Historians look at multiple theories because the environment is rarely affected by only one thing. A wrong answer shows "black and white" thinking, ignoring the complexity of historical events.
## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
If you were an archaeologist finding a dry, ancient riverbed near a ruined city, what would you write in your report to explain the city's end?
## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
The report should hypothesize that the river shift caused a lack of irrigation, leading to agricultural failure and the eventual abandonment of the city by its residents. A poor answer would just say "the city died" without connecting the environmental evidence to human behavior.
## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students often think the Harappan Civilisation was "destroyed" or "conquered" in a single war because they equate "decline" with "being defeated." In reality, the evidence points to a long, slow process of environmental stress. They confuse the gradual fading of a culture with the sudden end of a battle.

1. Ask the student to draw a picture of a river and a farm. 2. Ask them to erase the river and draw it somewhere else. 3. Ask them: "What happens to the crops now?" 4. Explain that this represents a 'decline' rather than a 'war.'

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A city of 50,000 people relies on the Ghaggar-Hakra river for wheat farming. Suddenly, the river dries up. What happens next?
STEPS:
- Identify the reliance on the river for food production.
- Recognize that 50,000 people cannot survive without a steady food supply.
- Analyze the impact on trade and daily life as resources dwindle.
- Conclude that the population must migrate or the city will be abandoned.
- Interpret the result as a decline in urban life.
REASONING: Cities are complex systems; when the foundation (water/food) is removed, the system cannot sustain itself.
ANSWER: The city would be abandoned because it could no longer feed its population.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: If a region becomes drier over 100 years, describe the stages of a city's decline.
STEPS:
- Year 1-20: Reduced rainfall, smaller harvests, grain storage used up.
- Year 21-50: Food prices rise, people begin to move to find better land.
- Year 51-80: Trade stops, city maintenance fails, buildings are not repaired.
- Year 81-100: The city is eventually left empty as the last families leave.
REASONING: This shows that "decline" is a process of slow degradation, not an overnight event.
ANSWER: The city slowly empties as resources disappear, showing a gradual decline rather than a sudden end.
## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: Why is it difficult for a historian to be 100% sure about why the Harappan Civilisation declined?
STEPS:
- Note the lack of readable written records from the time.
- Recognize that environmental evidence (like dry riverbeds) is indirect.
- Acknowledge that different experts have different theories based on their own field of study.
- Conclude that historians must piece together incomplete "jigsaw puzzles."
- Verify that this is the nature of historical inquiry.
REASONING: History is an interpretation of past evidence, which is often incomplete or missing.
ANSWER: It is difficult because we lack written accounts, forcing historians to rely on incomplete physical evidence and multiple theories.
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: If we find a city with very few tools and no signs of war, but lots of signs of "drought-resistant" plants appearing later, what does this tell us?
COMPETENCY: Evidence-based reasoning
STEPS:
- Look for signs of conflict (none found).
- Look for signs of climate (drought-resistant plants).
- Link the plants to the environment.
- Suggest that the environment changed to become drier, forcing the residents to adapt or leave.
ANSWER: The environment became drier, which supports the climate change theory of decline.
DIFFICULTY: medium
## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Compare the "shifting river" theory with the "climate change" theory. How are they related?
COMPETENCY: Analytical synthesis
STEPS:
- Define shifting river theory (water source moves).
- Define climate change theory (overall rainfall drops).
- Identify the common factor (water scarcity).
- Synthesize: A shifting river might be *caused* by the same climate change that reduced rainfall.
ANSWER: They are both environmental theories that share a common outcome: the loss of water necessary for large-scale agriculture, which was the heartbeat of Harappan cities.
DIFFICULTY: hard
