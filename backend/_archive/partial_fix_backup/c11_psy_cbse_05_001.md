## DEFINITION
Learning is any relatively permanent change in behaviour or behavioural potential that results from experience or practice, excluding temporary changes due to fatigue, habituation, drugs, or maturation.

## COGNITIVE_LEVEL
understand

## MASTERY_CRITERIA
The student can accurately distinguish learning from non-learning behavioural changes in five novel scenarios, justify each distinction using the criteria of permanence and experiential origin, and explain why learning is an inferred process rather than a directly observed one.

## SCOPE_BOUNDARY
This node covers the NCERT definition of learning, its three key features (experience-based, relatively permanent, inferred process), and the critical distinctions between learning, performance, maturation, fatigue, habituation, and drug-induced changes. It does NOT cover paradigms of learning (classical/operant conditioning, observational, cognitive, verbal, skill) which are addressed in subsequent nodes (c11_psy_cbse_05_002 onwards).

## WALKTHROUGH
Imagine you stayed up all night studying and now your hands shake while writing — is that learning? No, it's fatigue. Now imagine you practiced driving for 20 days and now drive smoothly without conscious effort — that is learning. Start by asking students to list five behavioural changes they observed in themselves this week. Classify each as learning or non-learning using two filters: (1) Did it arise from experience/practice? (2) Is it relatively permanent? Then introduce the "inferred process" concept: we see the *performance* (reciting a poem), but we *infer* the learning (memory formation). Connect this to the NCERT definition phrase-by-phrase. The key insight: not all behavioural change is learning; permanence and experiential origin are the gatekeepers.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_1
State the NCERT definition of learning verbatim, including the two exclusionary criteria for non-learning changes.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_1
Learning is "any relatively permanent change in behaviour or behavioural potential produced by experience." Changes due to fatigue, habituation, drugs, or maturation are excluded because they are temporary or not experience-based. A wrong answer (e.g., "learning is a change in behaviour") reveals the student misses the critical qualifiers "relatively permanent" and "produced by experience," leading to over-inclusion of temporary states like fatigue.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_2
A student reads a chapter once, closes the book, and immediately recalls 80% of it. The next day, they recall only 20%. Is the initial 80% recall evidence of learning? Explain using the distinction between learning and performance.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_2
The 80% recall is performance, not learning. Learning is the inferred relatively permanent change; the rapid drop to 20% shows the change was not permanent. The initial recall reflects short-term memory/performance, not consolidated learning. A wrong answer ("yes, because they recalled it") confuses performance (observable output) with learning (inferred permanent change), ignoring the permanence criterion.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_3
Classify each scenario as learning (L) or non-learning (NL) and justify using NCERT criteria:
(a) A newborn grasps a finger placed in its palm.
(b) After 10 cycles of bell-food pairing, a dog salivates to the bell alone.
(c) A person slurs speech after consuming alcohol.
(d) A child stops jumping at a loud noise after hearing it 20 times.
(e) A rat presses a lever more frequently after receiving food pellets.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_3
(a) NL — reflex (maturation), not experience-based. (b) L — experience (pairing), relatively permanent CR. (c) NL — drug-induced, temporary. (d) NL — habituation (repeated stimulus exposure), not associative experience. (e) L — experience (reinforcement), relatively permanent behaviour change. A wrong classification (e.g., marking (d) as learning) reveals confusion between habituation (non-associative, temporary) and associative learning.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_4
Compare and contrast "learning" and "maturation" using three specific criteria from the NCERT text. Provide one example where maturation enables learning but is not learning itself.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_4
Criteria: (1) Origin: learning from experience/practice; maturation from genetic unfolding. (2) Permanence: learning relatively permanent; maturation permanent but not "produced by experience." (3) Inference: learning inferred from performance; maturation observed as developmental milestones. Example: A child's neural maturation enables walking, but the walking skill itself is learned through practice. A weak answer conflates readiness (maturation) with acquisition (learning).

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_5
Evaluate this claim: "If a behaviour change lasts 24 hours, it qualifies as 'relatively permanent' learning per NCERT." Defend or refute with reasoning.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_5
Refute. "Relatively permanent" implies durability beyond transient states — typically days/weeks/months, not hours. NCERT contrasts learning with fatigue/habituation/drug effects that last minutes to hours. A 24-hour change could be short-term memory or residual drug effect. True learning shows resistance to extinction and spontaneous recovery (later concepts). Accepting 24 hours blurs the boundary with temporary states.

## DIAGNOSTIC_QUESTION_6
Design a 2-trial mini-experiment to demonstrate that learning is inferred, not observed. Specify: (1) pre-test, (2) experience/practice phase, (3) post-test, (4) the inference step. Use a verbal learning task.

## DIAGNOSTIC_ANSWER_6
(1) Pre-test: Ask participant to recall a list of 10 nonsense syllables — score 0. (2) Experience: Show each syllable for 2 sec, repeat list 5 times. (3) Post-test: Ask for recall — score 7. (4) Inference: Since pre-test was 0 and post-test 7 after practice, we *infer* learning occurred. We did not "see" learning; we saw performance change and inferred the internal change. Poor answer: omits pre-test or treats post-test score as direct observation of learning.

## COMMON_MISCONCEPTION
Students equate "relatively permanent" with "permanent forever" and argue that forgetting disproves learning. They also confuse *performance* (observable behaviour at a moment) with *learning* (the inferred underlying change). For example, a student who crams for an exam, performs well, then forgets everything a week later claims "no learning occurred." This misreads NCERT: learning *did* occur (relatively permanent change at that time), but extinction/forgetting happened later. The error stems from treating learning as a static state rather than a process with a trajectory.

## ON_FAILURE
Step 1: Ask the student to define "relatively permanent" in their own words. Step 2: Present the cramming-forgetting scenario. Step 3: Guide them to distinguish *acquisition* (learning happened) from *retention* (forgetting happened later). Step 4: Use the NCERT phrase "inferred process" — learning is the *inference* drawn from pre-post comparison, not the post-test score itself. Step 5: Have them re-classify the cramming scenario: learning occurred (pre 0 → post 80%), then forgetting occurred (post 80% → later 10%). Both are separate processes.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_1
PROBLEM: A researcher tests whether "studying with background music" produces learning. 20 students study a 20-word list with music (experimental group); 20 study in silence (control). Both take immediate recall test (Post-test 1) and a surprise test after 1 week (Post-test 2). Results: Experimental: Post1=14, Post2=9; Control: Post1=13, Post2=8. Using NCERT criteria, does music enhance *learning*? Justify in 4 steps.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the NCERT criteria for learning — relatively permanent change in behaviour/behavioural potential produced by experience.
- Step 2: Compare Post-test 1 (immediate performance) vs Post-test 2 (retention after 1 week) for each group. Both groups show decline (Exp: 14→9, Ctrl: 13→8), indicating forgetting.
- Step 3: Assess "relatively permanent" — both groups retain ~60% after 1 week, suggesting some relatively permanent change occurred in both.
- Step 4: Compare groups — difference in retention (9 vs 8) is negligible (1 word). Music did not produce *superior* relatively permanent change. Conclusion: Music may affect immediate performance (arousal), but not learning per se.
REASONING: Learning is inferred from relatively permanent change, not immediate performance. The 1-week delay tests permanence. The tiny inter-group difference at Post-test 2 shows music doesn't enhance the *learning* (permanent change), only perhaps momentary performance.
ANSWER: No, background music does not enhance learning. Both groups show similar relatively permanent retention (~60%) after 1 week; the small initial advantage (14 vs 13) disappears.

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_2
PROBLEM: A 6-year-old child cannot ride a bicycle. After 5 days of practice (30 min/day), the child rides smoothly. Two months later, without practice, the child rides again successfully. A second child watches a cycling video daily for 5 days but never practices. After 2 months, the second child cannot ride. Analyse using three NCERT features of learning.
STEPS:
- Step 1: Feature 1 — Experience/Practice. Child 1 had direct motor practice (experience); Child 2 had only observation (no motor experience). NCERT: learning requires experience.
- Step 2: Feature 2 — Relatively Permanent Change. Child 1 shows retention after 2 months (permanent); Child 2 shows no behavioural change (no learning).
- Step 3: Feature 3 — Inferred Process. For Child 1, we infer learning from pre-post comparison (couldn't ride → can ride → still can ride). For Child 2, no performance change to infer learning from.
- Step 4: Synthesise — Child 1 meets all three criteria; Child 2 meets none. Observation alone (without practice) did not produce learning in this motor skill.
REASONING: The NCERT definition requires *experience* (not just observation for skill learning), *relative permanence* (retention over time), and *inference* from behavioural change. This example isolates each feature.
ANSWER: Child 1 demonstrates learning (experience-based, relatively permanent, inferred). Child 2 does not (lacks motor experience, no behavioural change, nothing to infer).

## WORKED_EXAMPLE_3
PROBLEM: In a classical conditioning experiment, a tone (CS) is paired with air puff to eye (US) causing blink (UR). After 20 pairings, tone alone elicits blink (CR). The next day, 10 extinction trials (tone alone) are given — CR disappears. Two weeks later, tone alone elicits blink again (spontaneous recovery). Using ONLY the definition and features of learning from this node, explain: (a) Was learning demonstrated on Day 1? (b) Did extinction "erase" the learning? (c) What does spontaneous recovery prove about the nature of learning?
STEPS:
- Step 1 (a): Day 1 — Tone initially neutral; after pairings (experience), tone elicits blink (behavioural change). Change is relatively permanent (persists next day pre-extinction). Inferred from pre-post comparison. YES, learning occurred.
- Step 2 (b): Extinction — CR disappears, but NCERT says learning is "relatively permanent change in behaviour *or behavioural potential*." Extinction suppresses *performance*, not the underlying potential. The potential remains (proved by spontaneous recovery). NO, extinction did not erase learning.
- Step 3 (c): Spontaneous recovery — After 2 weeks with no practice, CR reappears. This proves the learned association (behavioural potential) was stored relatively permanently, not destroyed. Learning is an inferred *potency* that can be latent.
- Step 4: Connect to definition — "Behavioural potential" explains why learning survives extinction. The definition's "relatively permanent" refers to the potential, not the momentary response.
REASONING: This integrates all three features: experience (pairings), permanence (recovery after 2 weeks), and inference (we infer the latent potential from recovery). It foreshadows extinction/spontaneous recovery in c11_psy_cbse_05_003.
ANSWER: (a) Yes — experience produced relatively permanent behavioural potential inferred from CR acquisition. (b) No — extinction suppressed performance; behavioural potential remained. (c) Spontaneous recovery proves learning is a relatively permanent change in *behavioural potential*, not just observable response.

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_1
QUESTION: A teacher claims: "If a student performs well on a test immediately after studying, learning has definitely occurred." Using the NCERT distinction between learning and performance, write a 100-word rebuttal citing two specific reasons why this claim is flawed. Include the terms "inferred process" and "relatively permanent."
COMPETENCY: Evaluating claims using conceptual distinctions
STEPS:
- Step 1: Identify the claim's error — equates immediate performance with learning.
- Step 2: Cite Reason 1 — Learning is an *inferred process*; performance is only the observable behaviour. High immediate recall could be short-term memory, not consolidated learning.
- Step 3: Cite Reason 2 — Learning requires *relatively permanent* change; immediate post-test doesn't test permanence. Without delayed retention test, we cannot infer learning.
- Step 4: Synthesise into a concise rebuttal.
ANSWER: The claim confuses performance with learning. First, learning is an *inferred process* — we deduce it from pre-post change, not from a single performance snapshot. Immediate recall may reflect short-term activation, not structural change. Second, learning demands *relatively permanent* change; a single immediate test cannot establish permanence. Without a delayed retention measure (e.g., after 1 week), we cannot distinguish learning from transient memory. The student might forget entirely by tomorrow — then no relatively permanent change occurred. Thus, immediate performance is necessary but insufficient evidence for learning.
DIFFICULTY: medium

## COMPETENCY_QUESTION_2
QUESTION: Design a field study to test whether "learning to navigate a new city using GPS" produces relatively permanent spatial learning compared to "learning by paper map." Specify: (1) Participant groups, (2) Experience phase (duration, tasks), (3) Two post-tests (immediate + delayed) measuring behavioural potential, (4) How you will infer learning from data. Justify why GPS might impair *relatively permanent* change per NCERT.
COMPETENCY: Designing experiments to test theoretical constructs
STEPS:
- Step 1: Groups — 30 novice drivers each: GPS group (turn-by-turn audio), Map group (paper map only), Control (no aid, free exploration).
- Step 2: Experience — All drive 5 preset routes (10 km each) over 3 days. GPS follows instructions; Map plans then drives; Control explores.
- Step 3: Post-tests — Immediate: Drive routes without aid, record errors/time. Delayed (2 weeks later): Same routes without aid.
- Step 4: Inference — Learning inferred if (a) Map/Control show fewer errors than GPS at delayed test, and (b) GPS group shows large immediate→delayed decline (low permanence).
- Step 5: Justification — GPS offloads cognitive mapping (no mental representation formed); Map/Control require active encoding → stronger behavioural potential. NCERT: learning requires experience *processing*, not passive following.
ANSWER: (1) Three groups (GPS, Map, Control), n=30 each. (2) 3-day driving of 5 routes with assigned aid. (3) Immediate and 2-week delayed navigation tests without aids — measure wrong turns, time, hesitation. (4) Learning inferred from delayed test superiority of Map/Control over GPS, and GPS group's steep retention drop. GPS impairs relatively permanent change because it reduces cognitive engagement (experience processing), preventing formation of durable cognitive map (behavioural potential).
